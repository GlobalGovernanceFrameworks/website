# Executive summary

The GGF needs an implementation manual that helps communities, institutions, networks, and public bodies translate broad frameworks into context-sensitive practice. `IAF/1.1.1` established a strong constitutional discipline: context is not competence; assessment is not jurisdiction; adoption is not activation; funding is not permission; and a successful pilot is not authority to scale.

The recursive systems audit identified a second-order risk. Implementation can remain formally non-authoritative while acquiring practical agenda power through tool curation, compatibility rules, facilitator expertise, funding assistance, version control, and control over what counts as credible evidence. The implementation ecology can also become better at producing safe pilots and complete records than at producing timely, causally adequate, locally maintained change.

`IAF/1.2` therefore preserves the constitutional separation of v1.1.1 while adding five disciplines:

1. **causal responsibility:** local actors are not assigned responsibility for causes they do not control;
2. **causally adequate pilots:** a pilot must be small enough for safety and large enough to expose the mechanism it claims to test;
3. **proportional process:** `CP0`–`CP3` consequence profiles use a core-plus-trigger record model rather than one full burden for every activity;
4. **ordinary ownership:** pilot success is distinguished from transition, maintenance, and durability after exceptional support ends;
5. **recursive implementation review:** Catalyst Teams, facilitators, funders, vendors, tool stewards, AI systems, and learning custodians are reviewed for common-mode dependence and objective-reproduction pressures.

> **Context is not competence. Assessment is not jurisdiction. A tool is not a mandate. Ethical review is not approval. Cultural advice is not affected-nation consent. Adoption is not activation. A pilot is not public law. Pilot success supports only the claim that the pilot was designed to test. Support is not command. Compatibility is not legitimacy. Conformance is not success.**

The framework's role is precise:

- help an implementation principal understand conditions and causal constraints;
- identify affected parties, rights, authority, upstream controllers, and dependencies;
- offer tools and local adaptation options without owning the choice;
- support deliberation, consent, authorization, activation, and response handoffs through the correct interfaces;
- design pilots whose scale is proportionate to risk and adequate to the claimed mechanism;
- monitor distribution, harm, dependency, administrative burden, and transition readiness;
- close, repair, hand off, or institutionalize through lawful ordinary ownership;
- never manufacture the authority of the substantive decision.

## Constitutional result

The framework remains a **support, selection, documentation, implementation, transition, and learning system**. It is not:

- a legislature, regulator, court, fund, emergency command, or residual implementation sovereign;
- a source of BAZ status, institutional standing, FPIC, public office, jurisdiction, or ordinary-law enactment;
- a body that may classify communities as competent or unready;
- an approval mechanism for local cultural adaptation;
- an authority to activate a pilot, crisis stack, PIS function, or MGCC command;
- a power to condition essential support on GGF methods, compatibility, or values;
- a route for trade, security, geopolitical pressure, or compulsory institutionalization;
- a guarantee that a compliant pilot will be effective, transferable, scalable, or durable.

The retired **Hard Backstop Strategy** remains outside this framework. Trade, security, illicit-finance, cyber, diplomatic, and coercive strategies belong to their controlling substantive frameworks.

# 1. Purpose, scope, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

`IAF/1.2` provides a reusable pathway for implementing a framework, programme, tool, institutional reform, or local initiative while preserving local law, cultural form, rights, affected-nation authority, causal responsibility, domain-specific safeguards, and the distinction between experimentation and public power.

It supports:

- engagement and trust-building;
- context, capacity, causal-control, and dependency profiling;
- affected-party and power mapping;
- pathway and tool selection;
- cultural adaptation and local forks;
- advisory ethics and rights review;
- consequence-profile selection;
- pilot-type classification and causally adequate design;
- monitoring, evaluation, and learning;
- funding and participation support;
- response, refusal, and handoff records;
- transition to ordinary ownership;
- scaling proposals and permanent-adoption referrals;
- closure, remedy, and public learning.

## 1.2 Scope

The framework may be used by:

- Indigenous nations and communities that choose it;
- municipalities, public agencies, and public-service bodies;
- cooperatives, associations, networks, and civil-society organizations;
- international and regional institutions;
- GGF frameworks and pilot programmes;
- facilitators, technical teams, funders, vendors, and evaluators acting within bounded mandates.

The framework does not determine whether any actor possesses the authority required for the underlying action. That question is resolved through `IAF-28`, `GMEAIA-20`, the Constitutional Interface Specification, and the controlling domain interface.

## 1.3 Interface, not grant of power

Publication, translation, training, certification, funding, repository inclusion, compatibility recognition, or adoption by a Community of Practice does not activate `IAF/1.2` in any jurisdiction.

A participating actor must separately establish:

- its constituting instrument;
- authority to request and use implementation support;
- authority for each consequential pilot or permanent action;
- authority formation as `retained`, `delegated`, `transferred`, `concurrent`, `jointly_constituted`, or `absent`;
- applicable rights and public-law constraints;
- affected Indigenous authority;
- domain-specific interfaces;
- funding and procurement authority;
- response duties and clocks where consequential handoffs occur;
- review, remedy, expiry, and closure.

## 1.4 Governing doctrine

> **The framework can recommend how to proceed. It cannot decide that an actor may proceed. It can help test an intervention. It cannot enlarge what the test establishes.**

## 1.5 No upward inference

The following do not create authority, legitimacy, effectiveness, or durability:

- a readiness, trust, or implementation-health score;
- an Autonomy Statement;
- a completed Tool Stack;
- an Ethics Review;
- a facilitator certificate;
- an Indigenous participation quota;
- an EGP adoption record;
- a grant or AUBI payment;
- an AI recommendation;
- a positive dashboard;
- a successful pilot;
- GGF compatibility;
- a target year, pilot band, pilot type, or consequence profile;
- publication in the Failure Library or Story Bank.

# 3. Core principles

## 3.1 Causal subsidiarity with jurisdiction

Decisions should be made at the most local effective level that possesses relevant authority, practical capacity, and control over the material causes. Affected-party standing determines participation, challenge, consent, and remedy; it does not assign responsibility to those suffering the consequences.

Where causes are controlled upstream, externally, or jointly, the implementation must identify those controllers and their duties. Responsibility may not be assigned downward merely because consequences appear locally.

## 3.2 Anti-colonial by affected authority

Anti-colonial design requires more than external cultural sensitivity. It requires:

- the affected people naming themselves;
- control over representatives and knowledge;
- non-engagement without penalty;
- recognition of internal plurality;
- refusal of generic proxies;
- non-extractive data and learning;
- material support independent of methodological conformity.

## 3.3 Minimum causally adequate implementation within rights

Begin with the narrowest reversible action that is capable of producing useful evidence about the claimed mechanism.

> **A pilot shall be no larger than necessary for safety and no smaller than necessary to expose the causal mechanism it claims to test.**

“Minimum viable” does not mean minimum evidence, minimum consent, minimum rights, or a pilot so narrow that the difficult mechanism is excluded.

## 3.4 Low-tech first with equal standing

Every digital process must have an accessible analog, oral, assisted, SMS, paper, or offline route where feasible. Non-digital records receive equal substantive standing, response clocks, correction, and remedy.

## 3.5 Modular and interoperable, not jurisdictionless

Tools may be combined like modules, but each module retains its own authority effect, domain interface, data rules, prerequisites, and closure duties. Interoperability does not merge jurisdictions.

## 3.6 Compatibility without shadow governance

Compatibility may describe semantic, safety, lifecycle, or technical interoperability. It shall not become an indirect test of political legitimacy, cultural correctness, funding worth, or eligibility for essential support.

A compatibility decision shall state the exact technical or constitutional necessity, effects on local forks, alternatives, migration route, appeal, expiry, and whether incompatibility blocks only a compatibility claim or also a particular technical operation.

## 3.7 Accountability metadata, protected payloads

Non-sensitive purposes, responsible actors, authority states, funding, response status, review dates, and results should be transparent. Personal, worker, survivor, health, child, security, sacred, protected Indigenous, whistleblower, and legally sealed material remains bounded.

## 3.8 Ethical prudence without hidden veto

Ethics and rights review should reveal harm, uncertainty, capture, exclusion, missing safeguards, and common-mode dependence. It does not itself grant or deny authority.

## 3.9 Reversibility and continuing duties

Temporary powers and trials expire. Rights, wages, debts, pensions, compensation, records, restoration, safety, unresolved claims, and accepted handoff duties continue until discharged.

## 3.10 Support without dependency or conformity

Facilitation, translation, capacity building, independent advice, and participation support must not depend on accepting a recommended tool, GGF philosophy, compatibility claim, or favourable evaluation.

Support should build the principal's ability to maintain, adapt, fork, replace, or close the implementation without permanent dependence on the original Catalyst Team, facilitator, vendor, funder, or AI provider.

## 3.11 Learning without compulsory exposure

Failure and adaptation should be documented, but participation in an implementation process is not consent to permanent publication, storytelling, research, model training, or portable reputation.

## 3.12 Proportional process

Every activity uses a GMEAIA consequence profile:

- `CP0` — exploratory and non-consequential;
- `CP1` — voluntary bounded practice;
- `CP2` — operational or consequential action;
- `CP3` — protected-domain or constitutional action.

The selected profile uses a core-plus-trigger model. Record burden is proportionate to reversibility, rights effects, coercion, service effects, affected outsiders, ecological irreversibility, data sensitivity, duration, and scale.

## 3.13 Ordinary ownership and support withdrawal

A pilot is not durably implemented until a competent ordinary owner possesses or has lawfully arranged:

- mandate;
- budget;
- staffing;
- maintenance;
- data and vendor control;
- complaint and remedy routes;
- adaptation authority;
- closure responsibility.

Exceptional support withdrawal is a durability test, not an administrative afterthought.

## 3.14 Recursive review of the implementation ecology

Long-lived or systemically important implementation arrangements shall examine the incentives and common dependencies of Catalyst Teams, facilitators, assessors, funders, vendors, tool stewards, AI providers, evaluators, and learning custodians.

The review asks whether the ecosystem is rewarded for:

- durable local autonomy;
- accurate negative findings;
- tool retirement;
- ordinary institutionalization;
- reducing its own necessity;

or instead for pilot volume, record production, compatibility control, continued grants, and continuing facilitation.

# 4. Institutional roles and separation of duties

## 4.1 Implementation principal

The principal is the community, institution, network, or lawful authority requesting or accepting implementation support. It owns the decision to seek support and any lawful internal tool-selection decision. It does not gain new jurisdiction from the framework.

The principal should not be assigned responsibility for outcomes controlled materially by external authorities, funders, infrastructure owners, or upstream actors. `IAF-16`, `IAF-26`, and `IAF-28` identify the causal and authority envelope.

## 4.2 GGF Catalyst Team

The Catalyst Team may:

- maintain tools and compatibility information;
- provide non-binding technical assistance;
- train and support facilitators;
- synthesize voluntarily shared learning;
- identify missing interfaces and safety concerns;
- help prepare funding applications and records;
- support transition planning and local capability transfer.

It may not:

- approve or reject local adaptations;
- designate representatives;
- certify FPIC;
- declare a community ready or unready;
- activate pilots or emergency functions;
- condition essential support on conformity;
- command domain authorities;
- adjudicate complaints concerning its own conduct;
- make its own tool catalogue, training pathway, compatibility decision, or continued presence a practical monopoly.

For long-lived or high-consequence support, its mandate shall disclose:

- funding and appointment incentives;
- criteria for tool maintenance, visibility, deprecation, and training;
- common dependencies;
- exit and succession;
- evidence that it is building local autonomy rather than continuing dependence.

## 4.3 Community Weavers and facilitators

A Community Weaver is a facilitator, translator, recorder, and systems navigator within a defined mandate. Certification demonstrates one route to competence, not exclusive eligibility. A community may appoint a locally qualified equivalent.

Facilitators cannot convert process influence into decision authority. They must disclose conflicts, preserve dissent, protect data, submit to complaint and removal, avoid dependency creation, and transfer sufficient knowledge for local continuation where continuation is intended.

## 4.4 Assessors and advisory reviewers

Context assessors, ethics reviewers, cultural advisers, causal reviewers, and epistemic reviewers produce advisory records. They must be independent from material funding or approval decisions where feasible and disclose uncertainty, dissent, limitations, shared models, common data, professional ties, and conflicts.

## 4.5 Funders and vendors

Funders decide only within their lawful funding authority. Vendors provide contracted services only. Neither acquires governance authority, access to protected knowledge, rights over community data, or compatibility-control authority merely through financing or technical infrastructure.

Pilot finance and ordinary maintenance finance shall be distinguished. A funder shall not represent a pilot as transition-ready where no ordinary owner or post-grant resource path exists.

## 4.6 Community of Practice and learning custodians

The Community of Practice may compare methods and publish bounded patterns. The Failure Library or Story Bank custodian manages consent, protection, withdrawal, access, correction, and sample-bias disclosure. Neither ranks communities or uses learning records as a sanction or eligibility system.

## 4.7 Implementation ecology and common-mode review

A high-consequence or long-lived implementation shall map shared:

- funders;
- facilitators;
- assessors;
- tool maintainers;
- AI models and providers;
- data and cloud infrastructure;
- vendors;
- professional networks;
- methodological assumptions;
- reputational incentives.

Formal separation does not establish independent failure modes. Where genuine independence is infeasible, the responsible authority shall record the accepted correlation risk and fallback.

# 5. The implementation pathway, constitutionally separated

The familiar three-stage pathway remains, preceded by optional Stage 0. The stages organize support; they do not create a ladder of political maturity, legitimacy, or authority.

Before selecting a record bundle, the principal assigns a GMEAIA `CP0`–`CP3` consequence profile. Pilot type, pilot complexity, and consequence profile are separate classifications:

- **pilot type** states what proposition is being tested;
- **complexity band** describes the number and interaction of tools or jurisdictions;
- **consequence profile** determines proportional safeguards and records.

## Stage 0 — Requested trust-building and pre-assessment

Stage 0 is used when a principal requests relationship-building or when an outreach offer is accepted. It may include listening sessions, shared meals, story circles, community art, informal network awareness, accessibility planning, and seed support.

Stage 0 shall not:

- secretly score the community;
- select representatives;
- map political vulnerabilities for outside strategy;
- collect protected stories for later publication;
- imply consent to Stage 1;
- condition support on continued engagement;
- create a `CP2` or `CP3` profile merely because outside actors regard the community as difficult.

A simple `CP0` or `CP1` record may combine the necessary IAF semantics. Outputs are drawn from `IAF-06` through `IAF-10`, with protected content minimized.

## Stage 1 — Context, causal conditions, and implementation envelope: “Where are we?”

Stage 1 creates a multidimensional **Implementation Conditions and Causal Responsibility Profile**, not a competence score.

Required dimensions include:

- existing mandate and decision processes;
- affected parties and affected Indigenous authority;
- resources, time, finance, technology, and maintenance;
- accessibility and language;
- conflict, safety, capture, and retaliation;
- relevant evidence, uncertainty, and dissent;
- protected domains and external dependencies;
- material causal variables and their legal and practical controllers;
- upstream conditions and shared jurisdictions;
- administrative and uncompensated participation burden;
- desired support and self-identified strengths.

The profile distinguishes:

- what the principal controls;
- what requires cooperation from another actor;
- what requires jointly constituted authority;
- what is `capacity_limited`;
- what lacks lawful jurisdiction and constitutes a `constitutional_gap`;
- what cannot be meaningfully tested through the proposed local pilot.

The output may recommend support intensity, sequencing, upstream action, redesign, postponement, or an **Impossible Pilot Notice**. It cannot determine whether a community deserves support or may govern itself.

### Retirement of the single GGF Readiness Index

The Low/Medium/High index remains retired as a universal classification. A local actor may use a simple self-assessment for planning, but external systems must not treat it as authoritative or connect it automatically to grants, intervention, recognition, rights, or pilot-type claims.

## Stage 1.5 — Urgent support and Quick Start

Urgency changes the pace of support, not the source of authority.

The Quick Start route may:

- perform rapid listening and needs clarification;
- identify pre-existing local emergency or rescue authority;
- provide non-consequential templates and logistics;
- create an EGP `sense()` event;
- prepare a CDEE referral;
- support an already accepted ECRC/CDEE mission;
- document an immediate low-risk voluntary action;
- establish an authorized narrow safe state.

It may not:

- declare a crisis;
- activate PIS, MGCC, or Crisis Command;
- compel data sharing;
- deploy external teams without acceptance;
- override affected-nation authority;
- renew emergency powers;
- turn a 48-hour diagnostic target into legal permission;
- treat referral receipt, silence, technical compatibility, or urgency as acceptance.

Consequential response and handoff states are governed exclusively by `GMEAIA/0.2.1`.

## Stage 2 — Journey, tool, and compatibility selection: “What path fits?”

Stage 2 compares possible journeys and tools. Each resource card must state:

- purpose and intended users;
- authority effect;
- consequence-profile triggers;
- protected-domain flags;
- prerequisites and controlling interfaces;
- causal mechanism and claims supported;
- time, people, cost, maintenance, and administrative burden;
- accessibility and low-tech equivalents;
- data, AI, vendor, and security effects;
- common-mode dependencies;
- risks, failure modes, exit, transition, and remedy;
- version, compatibility, local-fork, and migration conditions.

The local principal may choose, adapt, fork, combine, postpone, reject, or replace tools within its lawful scope.

### Coherence and autonomy

The “non-negotiable core” is not ideological conformity. It is the minimum conformance needed to avoid falsely claiming GGF compatibility:

- accurate authority effect;
- rights and protected-domain safeguards;
- affected Indigenous authority;
- consent and refusal;
- accessibility and data protection;
- review, expiry, remedy, and closure;
- exact version and interface records;
- correct response-state and consequence-profile semantics.

The adaptable periphery includes language, ceremony, facilitation, sequencing, technology, record form, and locally chosen tools.

### Compatibility-power review

Where incompatibility may affect funding, support, recognition, interoperability, or participation, the responsible steward shall record:

- exact technical or constitutional necessity;
- the compatibility claim being protected;
- effects on local forks and non-GGF alternatives;
- switching and migration costs;
- appeal and expiry;
- whether the implementation can proceed without claiming compatibility.

“Incompatible” does not mean illegitimate, culturally defective, unsafe in all contexts, or ineligible for unrelated support.

### Cultural adaptation review

A cultural or Indigenous adviser may identify concerns or recommendations. No generic representative has a dual key over an affected nation or local lawful process. Binding consent and instructions come from the affected nation through `IAF-09` and `IAF-27`.

### Ethics review

The **Ethics, Harm, Rights, Anti-Capture, and Common-Mode Advisory Review** may conclude:

- no identified concern;
- concern requiring response;
- missing information;
- protected-domain route required;
- strong recommendation to pause;
- common-mode dependence requiring fallback or explicit risk acceptance;
- dissent or unresolved conflict.

It cannot label the pilot legally approved or rejected.

## Stage 3 — Pilot, learn, transition, close, and propose scale: “What can this test establish?”

Stage 3 contains distinct constitutional events:

1. pilot proposal and pilot-type classification;
2. causal-adequacy and non-action analysis;
3. affected-party and consent records;
4. authority verification and authority formation;
5. trial authorization;
6. EGP adoption registration;
7. operational activation;
8. implementation;
9. monitoring and incident response;
10. midpoint and final review;
11. termination and closure;
12. learning artifact;
13. transition-to-ordinary-ownership assessment;
14. optional scaling or permanent-adoption proposal.

### Pilot types

| Type | Primary question | What success may support | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acceptability | Can affected participants understand, access, and willingly use the intervention? | Acceptability within the tested population and conditions | Feasibility, causal effectiveness, system effect, or durability |
| Feasibility | Can the intervention operate safely with the tested resources and constraints? | Bounded operational feasibility | Causal mechanism, net benefit, transferability, or ordinary affordability |
| Mechanism | Does the claimed causal mechanism operate under specified conditions? | Evidence about the mechanism | System-wide effect, political durability, or scale |
| Operational | Can authorized ordinary actors deliver the intervention reliably? | Delivery capability under tested conditions | Net system effect or long-term institutionalization |
| System-effect | Does the relevant condition improve, including distributional and external effects? | Bounded system-effect evidence | Transferability to new contexts or ordinary ownership |
| Institutionalization | Can a competent ordinary owner maintain, adapt, fund, govern, and close the function after exceptional support ends? | Durability within the tested institutional context | Universal scalability or permanent superiority |

A pilot may combine types only where each question, evidence requirement, and limitation is separately stated.

### Minimum causal adequacy

The pilot proposal shall identify:

- claimed causal mechanism;
- variables the principal controls;
- variables controlled by others;
- required upstream conditions;
- scale-dependent effects;
- mechanisms excluded by the pilot boundary;
- conclusions the pilot cannot support.

Where the pilot cannot expose the claimed mechanism because authority, scale, resources, or upstream cooperation are absent, it shall be redesigned, narrowed to an honest question, routed upstream, or marked impossible.

### Pilot complexity bands

The original four tiers remain planning bands:

| Band | Description | Authority consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Single low-risk tool | None; still requires appropriate internal authority and consent |
| 2 | Multi-tool pilot | None; cumulative dependency and administrative-burden review required |
| 3 | Full local stack | None; public or protected-domain effects require specialized authority |
| 4 | Multi-community or multi-jurisdiction proposal | No automatic progression; new jurisdiction and authorization required |

A higher band is not evidence of maturity, certification, political legitimacy, causal adequacy, or system effect.

### Red Lines and protective pause

A Red Lines alert may flag demonstrable harm, rights violations, service risk, coercion, authority failure, or a material mismatch between the authorized and actual causal envelope. A technical system may enter a pre-authorized safe state. Legal suspension, rollback, funding action, or remedy follows the named competent route.

### Pilot-to-institution transition and support withdrawal

Before representing a pilot as transition-ready, identify:

- lawful ordinary owner;
- ordinary mandate and authority;
- recurrent budget;
- staff and labour arrangements;
- maintenance and replacement capacity;
- data, vendor, and platform control;
- complaint, correction, and remedy routes;
- local adaptation and succession;
- support-withdrawal sequence;
- failure and closure plan.

A pilot that works only while exceptional facilitation, grant finance, vendor subsidy, or Catalyst presence continues has not demonstrated institutionalization.

Repeated pilots addressing substantially the same mechanism shall explain what evidence remains missing and why transition, rejection, or structural action is not the appropriate next step.

### Scaling

Pilot success produces evidence and a proposal. It does not produce authority to scale. Every expanded population, territory, service, institution, causal environment, or risk profile requires `IAF-43` and `IAF-44`.

# 6. Implementation Health Dashboard

The Dashboard is a navigation and learning instrument. It is not a score of community worth, pilot legitimacy, causal truth, or a control panel for funding and activation.

It may display:

- participation distributions rather than a single engagement percentage;
- accessibility and exclusion;
- resource and maintenance conditions;
- pilot type, complexity band, and consequence profile;
- causal variables inside and outside the principal's control;
- assessment, response, authorization, activation, effect, and correction latency;
- administrative and uncompensated participation burden;
- trust and legitimacy evidence with uncertainty and dissent;
- financial, technical, facilitator, vendor, and funder dependence;
- common-mode risks;
- incidents, harms, corrections, and unresolved duties;
- ordinary owner, transition status, and support-withdrawal readiness;
- repeated-pilot signals;
- review and expiry dates;
- authority, handoff, and activation status.

Illustrative targets such as participation, adaptation time, resource independence, trust, or transition readiness are not universal thresholds. A project may adopt a target through a separate record with context, method, reasons, distribution, review, and non-disposition rule.

No dashboard result may automatically:

- activate, pause, terminate, institutionalize, or scale a pilot;
- determine funding eligibility or payment;
- classify a community as ready or legitimate;
- identify fraud, capture, or wrongdoing;
- publish protected information;
- renew authority;
- convert acceptability or feasibility evidence into a claim of system effect;
- declare support dependency resolved.

# 7. Tool families and flagship guides

The source flagship guides remain examples, subject to their controlling frameworks:

- **Bioregional Compass:** can support a community exploring a BAZ or another bioregional institution, but cannot create BAZ status, territory, Indigenous authority, public jurisdiction, or prove economic viability through a low-scale acceptability pilot.
- **Great Transition:** can support planning and worker/community dialogue, but industrial closure, security measures, finance, compensation, legal mandates, ordinary ownership, and worker continuity remain with their controlling frameworks.
- **Regenerative Resource Stewardship:** can integrate Hearthstone, ecological, trade, and Indigenous records, but cannot transfer title, recognize rights, issue permits, impose trade consequences, or assign local responsibility for upstream extraction it does not control.

Every guide shall carry:

- a prominent authority-effect statement;
- pilot types it can support;
- causal claims and limits;
- consequence-profile triggers;
- exact version pins;
- compatibility-power and local-fork conditions;
- maintenance, transition, and support-withdrawal requirements.

# 8. Funding and resourcing

## 8.1 Capacity seed support

Seed grants may fund meeting space, translation, childcare, accessibility, local facilitators, independent advice, causal assessment, and basic tools. They do not buy consent, political representation, favourable evaluation, or compatibility.

## 8.2 Proto-GCF and Regenerative Compact

A voluntary compact or proto-fund may support pilots under existing law. It does not create treaty authority, public taxation, BAZ status, ordinary ownership, or a future claim to compulsory funding.

## 8.3 Funding Onramp

The Funding Onramp separates:

- request;
- eligibility review;
- award;
- agreement;
- payment authorization;
- disbursement;
- audit;
- suspension;
- clawback;
- appeal;
- closure.

Funding may support implementation but cannot activate it. Essential services, worker wages, participant accessibility, independent advice, and safe transition require continuity safeguards.

Pilot funding shall distinguish:

- one-time design and learning costs;
- temporary implementation subsidy;
- recurrent ordinary operating cost;
- maintenance and replacement cost;
- transition and support-withdrawal cost;
- closure and remedy cost.

## 8.4 No conformity leverage

A funder may require lawful use, financial accountability, safety, rights safeguards, and honest limits on claims. It may not condition essential or participation support on:

- adopting a particular GGF tool;
- affirming MOS or another philosophy;
- accepting an external cultural classification;
- waiving claims or protected knowledge;
- surrendering local authority;
- suppressing criticism;
- claiming GGF compatibility;
- continuing pilots primarily to preserve the grant relationship.

## 8.5 Transition finance and funder exit

A scaling or permanent-adoption proposal shall identify whether ordinary finance exists independently of the pilot funder. A funder's withdrawal shall not silently abandon essential services, workers, participant data, remedies, or unresolved duties.

Where ordinary finance is absent, the result may remain a successful bounded pilot but shall not be represented as institutionally durable.

# 9. Data, AI, and public learning

## 9.1 Data minimization and community control

Implementation records are collected for declared purposes. “Community ownership” does not erase individual, worker, affected-nation, legal, or protected-source rights. Access, retention, publication, correction, deletion, withdrawal, model use, and handoff are separately governed.

## 9.2 AI support and common-mode dependence

AI may assist translation, accessibility, missing-field detection, pattern comparison, drafting, and non-consequential tool suggestions.

AI may not:

- classify community competence;
- select representatives;
- decide consent;
- pass an Ethics Review;
- verify legal authority;
- assign causal responsibility autonomously;
- activate, institutionalize, or scale a pilot;
- determine funding, sanctions, or closure;
- convert stories into risk or reputation scores;
- infer system effect from compliance or pilot completion.

Where the same model or provider supports assessment, tool selection, evaluation, and learning synthesis, the common-mode risk and fallback shall be recorded.

## 9.3 Failure Library and Story Bank

Public learning artifacts must distinguish:

- method lesson;
- identifiable participant story;
- protected operational record;
- whistleblower or evidence material;
- protected Indigenous or cultural knowledge;
- failed or abandoned implementation;
- transition failure after pilot success.

Publication requires purpose-specific authority and consent. Withdrawal, correction, de-identification, access restriction, and deletion must remain possible.

## 9.4 Learning-sample limits

The learning custodian shall disclose selection effects where feasible, including under-representation of:

- communities that declined participation;
- abandoned pilots;
- retaliation or political defeat;
- weakly documented cases;
- unfunded local alternatives;
- failures caused by handoff refusal or support withdrawal.

A learning library is not representative merely because it contains many cases.

# 10. Conflict, complaint, response, and remedy

Every implementation must offer a route proportionate to its effects:

- local feedback and safe reporting;
- facilitator complaint and substitute;
- independent review;
- interim pause or relief;
- correction of assessment, dashboard, compatibility, and public records;
- compensation or restitution where harm occurred;
- protection against retaliation;
- appeal to the competent domain body;
- reopening after closure when remedy remains unresolved.

The Catalyst Team cannot be the final reviewer of complaints against itself.

Consequential referrals and handoffs use the exact GMEAIA states:

`offered`, `acknowledged`, `accepted`, `partially_accepted`, `capacity_limited`, `refused`, `deferred`, `referred`, `timed_out`, `misrouted`, `constitutional_gap`, `returned`, and `closed`.

Each consequential handoff records the response-duty source and effect, clock source, expected harm during delay, sender custody, recipient status, provisional safe-state reference, post-acceptance action clock, escalation, and unresolved duties.

Timeout, technical receipt, schema compatibility, or silence does not transfer jurisdiction. Acceptance without subsequent action may become a performance omission and remains reviewable.

# 11. Emergency and security boundaries

## 11.1 Emergency

EGP carries asserted signals and adoption claims. CDEE carries cross-domain event identity and handoff. ECRC records emergency constitutional acts. The Implementation Framework may support those systems but cannot replace them.

A Quick Start referral remains `CP0`, `CP1`, or a support component of an already authorized `CP3` action according to its actual effects. The implementation framework does not lower consequence classification because time is short.

## 11.2 Security and coercion

The retired Hard Backstop Strategy remains outside `IAF/1.2`. Economic insulation, trade measures, illicit-finance targeting, cyber operations, asset restraint, security pressure, or legitimacy campaigns require their own frameworks, authority, evidence, rights, review, and SCPA handoff where coercive.

`IAF-47` may refer a coercive request through `GMEAIA-57`. It cannot accept, authorize, execute, or take custody of coercive power.

Implementation staff shall not collect political intelligence or use context, causal, power, compatibility, or dependency maps for pressure campaigns.

# 14. Adoption, normative force, consequence profiles, and effect labels

Implementation records use GMEAIA normative-force states and EGP effect labels.

A tool or method may be:

- `proposed`;
- `advisory`;
- `voluntarily_adopted`;
- `contractually_binding` within a valid contract;
- `internally_binding` within a valid charter;
- `locally_enacted`;
- `nationally_enacted`;
- `treaty_active`;
- `suspended`;
- `expired`;
- `terminated`.

No state may be inferred from publication, popularity, funding, certification, compatibility, pilot success, or a future date.

An EGP adoption must display an EGP/1.1 effect label. `active_bounded_trial` requires valid `IAF-28` through `IAF-31` records.

## 14.1 Consequence profiles

The selected `CP0`–`CP3` profile is recorded through the appropriate IAF and GMEAIA records.

- `CP0` uses a compact non-operative core. `IAF-07` or `IAF-50` is triggered only where an actual sandbox, prototype, simulation, or demonstration exists.
- `CP1` uses a compact voluntary-practice core and adds affected-party, data, funding, vendor, accessibility, or learning records only when materially present.
- `CP2` requires authority, affected parties, causal responsibility, authorization, activation, implementation ownership, monitoring, challenge, closure, and remedy semantics.
- `CP3` adds the controlling specialized interface and protected-domain records.

Complexity, implementation ambition, or the number of GGF tools does not raise authority. Consequence determines safeguards.

# 17. Implementation sequence

## Phase A — Constitute, classify, and request

1. Adopt or reference the framework without granting it public power.
2. Record the principal, objective, scope, actors, conflicts, source versions, and initial causal claim.
3. Select the provisional `CP0`–`CP3` profile.
4. Establish complaint and independent-review routes.

## Phase B — Engage, understand, and locate causal responsibility

1. Obtain engagement consent or record lawful outreach.
2. Map affected parties and affected Indigenous authority.
3. Provide accessibility and independent support.
4. Create the Context Assessment and Causal-Adequacy Charter.
5. Map causal controllers, upstream dependencies, shared authority, capacity limitations, and constitutional gaps.
6. Publish a conditions and support recommendation with uncertainty and dissent.

## Phase C — Select, compare, and adapt

1. Compare journeys, non-GGF alternatives, non-action, and simpler options.
2. Inspect tool authority effects, causal claims, consequence triggers, protected-domain flags, and compatibility power.
3. Document local adaptations and forks.
4. Receive cultural and ethics advice without treating it as approval.
5. Record the tool-selection decision and administrative burden.

## Phase D — Design, authorize, and activate a causally adequate pilot

1. classify the pilot type;
2. define the mechanism, alternatives, non-action, and conclusions unavailable;
3. test minimum causal adequacy;
4. obtain consent and participation records;
5. verify authority and authority formation;
6. route protected domains;
7. authorize the trial;
8. register the EGP adoption claim;
9. activate separately;
10. implement with service, rights, dependency, and response safeguards.

## Phase E — Review, close, transition, and learn

1. monitor distributions, causal contribution, incidents, latency, administrative burden, and dependency;
2. pause or roll back when required;
3. conduct midpoint and final reviews without expanding the pilot's claim;
4. terminate and return authority;
5. complete remedies, support withdrawal, and data disposition;
6. create bounded learning artifacts with sample-limit disclosure;
7. assess ordinary ownership and institutionalization;
8. open a new scaling or permanent-adoption process where justified.

# 19. Framework theory of change and falsification register

## 19.1 Theory of change

The framework assumes that implementation improves when actors:

1. establish legitimate authority and affected-party standing;
2. understand context, causal responsibility, and dependencies;
3. select and adapt tools without external ideological control;
4. use proportionate safeguards;
5. test an intervention at a scale adequate to its claimed mechanism;
6. monitor distribution, harm, latency, burden, and dependency;
7. transition useful functions into ordinary ownership or close them responsibly;
8. share bounded learning that improves later decisions.

## 19.2 Framework hypotheses and disconfirming evidence

### H1 — Context-sensitive selection improves fit

**Hypothesis:** conditions and causal profiles materially improve tool choice, safety, inclusion, or outcomes compared with generic implementation.

**Disconfirming evidence:** profiles mostly restate known facts, do not change decisions, or add burden without improved fit.

### H2 — Constitutional separation prevents overreach without defeating action

**Hypothesis:** separating consent, authority, authorization, adoption, activation, and scaling reduces unauthorized action while preserving timely response.

**Disconfirming evidence:** compliant implementations routinely miss decision windows, stall in handoffs, or abandon useful action because the chain is too slow.

### H3 — Local adaptation improves effectiveness and legitimacy

**Hypothesis:** local forks increase relevance and ownership without eroding rights or making learning unusable.

**Disconfirming evidence:** adaptation is cosmetic, compatibility becomes shadow governance, or local forks receive materially inferior support without safety justification.

### H4 — Causally adequate bounded pilots produce valid learning

**Hypothesis:** bounded pilots expose enough of the mechanism to support honest decisions.

**Disconfirming evidence:** pilots repeatedly succeed in protected conditions and fail at the mechanism, scale, or political transition excluded from the test.

### H5 — Proportional process reduces both risk and bureaucracy

**Hypothesis:** `CP0`–`CP3` core-plus-trigger profiles reduce over-administration without under-governing consequential action.

**Disconfirming evidence:** low-risk work still requires specialist-heavy records, or consequential harms arise because required triggers were omitted.

### H6 — Support builds autonomy rather than dependency

**Hypothesis:** Catalyst, facilitator, funder, vendor, and AI support increase local capacity to maintain, adapt, fork, replace, or close.

**Disconfirming evidence:** pilots collapse when exceptional support ends or local actors remain dependent on the original implementation ecology.

### H7 — Learning improves future implementation

**Hypothesis:** bounded learning changes later tool selection and reduces repeated failure.

**Disconfirming evidence:** libraries are biased, unused, dominated by publishable success, or disconnected from decisions.

### H8 — Transition architecture reduces permanent-pilot accumulation

**Hypothesis:** ordinary-owner and repeated-pilot requirements move useful functions into durable provision and terminate unsupported experiments.

**Disconfirming evidence:** pilot volume grows while transition rates remain low and the same mechanisms are repeatedly retested.

## 19.3 Narrowing, suspension, replacement, and non-scaling

The framework or a major mechanism should be narrowed, suspended, replaced, or not scaled where evidence shows that it:

- creates more delay than the condition permits;
- imposes disproportionate administrative or participation burden;
- produces no material improvement in fit, safety, causal learning, or outcomes;
- privileges already-capable actors despite support measures;
- creates practical Catalyst, facilitator, vendor, funder, or compatibility monopolies;
- systematically underpowers pilots;
- converts bounded experimentation into permanent temporary governance;
- fails to build ordinary ownership;
- produces learning selected mainly for legibility and positive narrative;
- performs worse than direct domain implementation or a simpler non-GGF alternative.

A framework revision, successful demonstration, or larger case library does not by itself defeat disconfirming evidence.

# 20. Migration, conformance, and machine-readable adoption

## 20.1 Migration from v1.1.1

Existing v1.1.1 records remain historically valid within their original authority and version envelope. They do not automatically acquire v1.2 consequence profiles, causal-adequacy findings, response states, or ordinary-ownership claims.

A consequential active implementation migrating to v1.2 should review:

- source and compatibility status;
- selected `CP0`–`CP3` profile;
- pilot type and claim limits;
- causal responsibility and upstream control;
- handoff and response states;
- common-mode dependencies;
- ordinary ownership and support withdrawal;
- unresolved duties and closure.

## 20.2 Conformance claim

An implementation may claim `IAF/1.2` conformance only when:

- applicable gates pass;
- exact versions are pinned;
- consequence profile and triggered records are justified;
- authority and activation remain valid;
- prohibited inferences are stated;
- record corrections propagate;
- no retired cluster registry is presented as current adoption.

## 20.3 Machine-readable release status

This Markdown specification remains the normative IAF text. Its machine-readable catalogue, schemas, examples, validator, package manifest, and checksums are adopted as a companion through `IAEG-CLUSTER/1.0`. Machine conformance establishes structural compatibility and traceability; it does not establish authority, legitimacy, causal success, or permission to scale.

# 21. Closing doctrine

The framework succeeds when it makes implementation easier **without making authority easier to counterfeit, causal claims easier to inflate, or local dependence easier to hide**.

> **Help people understand their context and the causes they can actually control. Offer tools without owning the choice. Support consent without manufacturing it. Document authority without inventing it. Test no less than the mechanism requires and claim no more than the test establishes. Start only after activation. Build ordinary ownership before exceptional support ends. Stop when the mandate ends. Return power, repair harm, and share learning without exposing those who made it possible.**
