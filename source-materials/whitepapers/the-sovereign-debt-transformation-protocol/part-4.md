# Part IV: Implementation

*A note on sequencing: The implementation pathway described here is not a project plan — it is a conditional roadmap. Each phase depends on preconditions that cannot be assumed: Treaty ratification progress, SRC establishment, development of verification infrastructure, and the political conditions that make creditor participation possible. The timeline estimates are illustrative. The sequencing logic — what must happen before what — is the more durable claim.*

---

## Chapter 11: Three-phase transition pathway

### 11.1 Why phasing matters

The SDTP cannot be deployed all at once. Its components are interdependent in ways that require sequential development: verification infrastructure must exist before performance obligations can be tracked, the SRC must be operational before conversion rates can be set, and creditors must have seen the instrument function before they will accept it at scale.

Beyond the technical sequencing, there is a political economy argument for phasing. Novel financial instruments earn legitimacy through demonstrated performance, not theoretical elegance. The history of debt restructuring is littered with proposals that failed not because they were technically unsound but because they were introduced before supporting institutions were ready or creditor trust had been established. Each phase of the SDTP is designed to generate evidence that makes the next phase more achievable.

The three phases are distinguished by scope, governance maturity, and the degree to which the SDTP operates within versus alongside existing debt architecture.

### 11.2 Phase 1: Foundation and pilots (Years 1–3)

**Objective:** Establish the minimum viable institutional and technical infrastructure, and generate initial evidence of the instrument's functioning from carefully designed pilot programs.

**Treaty and governance prerequisites.** Phase 1 begins as soon as the Treaty for Our Only Home has sufficient ratification to establish the SRC with a functioning secretariat. The SRC need not be fully composed with all intended constituencies in Phase 1 — a provisional body with core technical capacity and observer status for non-Treaty creditors is sufficient to manage the pilot phase. Full SRC composition is a Phase 2 requirement.

**Verification infrastructure development.** The primary technical task of Phase 1 is building the Love Ledger verification architecture to a standard adequate for pilot deployment. This means: specifying the data standards for Tier 1 national monitoring submissions, accrediting an initial cohort of Tier 2 civil society verifiers in pilot countries, and establishing the SRC technical secretariat's remote sensing and audit capacity. This is not small — it requires software development, institutional development, and capacity-building in pilot countries simultaneously. It is estimated to require two to three years of focused effort with adequate resourcing.

**Pilot country selection.** Phase 1 pilots should be selected to test the instrument across different debt profiles, ecological contexts, and governance capacities. Selection criteria:

- Significant external debt burden with documented sustainability concerns
- Existing ecological and social monitoring capacity that can be upgraded rather than built from scratch
- Strong civil society sector with independence from government sufficient to perform Tier 2 verification
- Government commitment to SDTP participation at ministerial level, with cross-party support where possible
- At least one creditor willing to participate on the debt side — bilateral development bank creditors are the most likely Phase 1 partners

Candidate profiles (not specific country recommendations, which require political assessment beyond this paper's scope) include: a small island developing state with acute climate-debt exposure; a sub-Saharan African nation with significant biodiversity assets and bilateral debt concentration; and a Latin American nation with existing debt-for-nature precedent and multilateral creditor relationships that could be adapted.

**Phase 1 debt scope.** Pilots cover a defined subset of participating nations' debt — not the full stock. Bilateral official development assistance debts are the natural starting point: lower political complexity than commercial bonds, greater flexibility in creditor negotiations, and development bank creditors who are already mandated to consider ecological outcomes. The commercial debt market remains untouched in Phase 1.

**Pilot evaluation criteria.** Phase 1 generates evidence on: verification system accuracy (comparing Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 outputs and identifying systematic discrepancies); government implementation capacity (whether debtor nations can design and execute qualifying regenerative programs at the required scale); creditor experience (whether bilateral creditors find the instrument administratively manageable and its outputs credible); and community impacts (whether pilot programs produce the local benefits claimed and avoid the harms identified in the risk chapter).

Phase 1 concludes with a public evaluation report — not an internal assessment — that is made available to potential Phase 2 participants, independent researchers, and civil society organizations. The credibility of Phase 2 depends on Phase 1 evaluation being genuinely honest about what worked and what did not.

### 11.3 Phase 2: Scaled deployment and secondary market development (Years 4–7)

**Objective:** Expand SDTP coverage to 20–30 countries, bring in private commercial creditors, establish functioning secondary markets for Leaves instruments, and complete full SRC governance composition.

**Governance maturation.** Phase 2 requires full SRC composition with all intended constituencies. Critically, this phase requires creditor nation governments to move from observer status to full participation — meaning they accept SRC authority over conversion rates for their own sovereign lending. This is a significant political step that Phase 1's demonstrated performance is intended to make possible. A provably functioning verification system and at least two or three years of creditor experience with Phase 1 instruments are the primary political prerequisites.

**Commercial creditor engagement.** Private bondholders represent a substantial share of heavily indebted nations' debt — in some cases the majority. Phase 2 introduces SDTP instruments into commercial debt restructuring contexts. This requires: legal template development for collective action clause applications, rating agency engagement to establish how SDTP instruments are rated relative to conventional bonds, and development of the secondary market infrastructure that makes Leaves instruments tradable by institutional investors.

Secondary market development is not a technical afterthought — it is central to the instrument's long-run viability. Creditors who cannot exit their positions in SDTP instruments face illiquidity risk that they will price heavily into their participation calculus. Creating a secondary market requires: a sufficient volume of standardized instruments (which Phase 2's scale provides), market-maker commitment from at least one or two major impact-oriented financial institutions, and integration with existing carbon credit trading infrastructure where Leaves overlap with verified carbon units.

**Expanded ecological and social scope.** Phase 1 focuses on the most established measurement methodologies — primarily forest carbon and primary healthcare access. Phase 2 expands to the full range of qualifying activities described in Chapter 5, as the measurement methodologies for those activities are validated through Phase 1 experience and ongoing scientific development. Marine protection, biodiversity metrics, and care economy measurement all require additional development before they can carry the financial weight of debt service obligations at scale.

**Bridgetown Initiative alignment.** Phase 2 engagement should actively seek alignment with the Bridgetown Initiative's political momentum. The Bridgetown Initiative, led by Caribbean small island states and supported by a growing coalition of Global South nations, has achieved significant international visibility in arguing for structural reform of the global financial architecture for climate-vulnerable nations. The SDTP is directly complementary to Bridgetown's objectives, and formal alignment — including potential endorsement from Bridgetown Initiative participants — would significantly advance Phase 2 creditor engagement.

### 11.4 Phase 3: Systemic integration (Years 8–15)

**Objective:** Integration of SDTP instruments into the standard toolkit of multilateral sovereign debt restructuring, recognition in major creditor-jurisdiction legal frameworks, and self-sustaining secondary market operation.

**Multilateral integration.** The ultimate measure of the SDTP's success is whether it becomes a standard option in IMF, World Bank, and Paris Club debt restructuring processes — not a niche instrument deployed when specific creditors choose to offer it, but a recognized mechanism that debtor nations can access as a matter of course. Achieving this requires:

- IMF recognition of SDTP instruments in debt sustainability analysis frameworks — specifically, that Leaves-denominated obligations are treated differently from conventional obligations in assessing debt sustainability ratios
- World Bank and regional development bank adoption of SDTP-compatible lending instruments for new loans to eligible nations, building the stock of transformable debt from origination rather than only through restructuring
- Paris Club procedural recognition, allowing the SDTP to be offered as a standard option in Paris Club renegotiations without requiring individual creditor-by-creditor negotiation

**Legal framework consolidation.** Phase 3 targets recognition of SDTP instruments in English and New York law — the dominant jurisdictions for sovereign debt contracts — as an established instrument type with defined legal treatment. This is partly a regulatory process (engaging financial regulators in major creditor jurisdictions) and partly a market process (as instruments accumulate a track record, legal frameworks adapt to accommodate them). The commodity-linked bond analogy from Chapter 7 provides a pathway; Phase 3 legal work consolidates and extends it.

**Self-sustaining market operation.** By the end of Phase 3, the SDTP should require no special institutional support to function. The SRC sets conversion rates transparently using established methodology; verification operates through the accredited civil society network and remote sensing infrastructure; secondary markets provide liquidity; and new debt is being originated in SDTP-compatible forms by multilateral lenders. At this point the instrument is no longer a reform proposal — it is part of the standard architecture of international finance.

This is a fifteen-year horizon from Treaty ratification. It is long. It is also realistic. The transition from the Brady Plan (which first introduced market-oriented sovereign debt restructuring in 1989) to the current Common Framework took approximately thirty years. The SDTP is attempting a more fundamental architectural change; expecting to complete it in less than fifteen years would be optimistic to the point of incredibility.

## Chapter 12: Pilot program designs

### 12.1 Design principles for pilots

Pilots serve two functions: they test whether the SDTP works in practice, and they generate the evidence that makes subsequent phases politically achievable. These functions create somewhat different design requirements — a test focused purely on learning might be designed differently from one focused on generating persuasive evidence for skeptical audiences.

The resolution is transparency. Pilots must be designed and documented so that both functions are served: rigorous enough that the learning is real, and public enough that the evidence convinces. This means explicit pre-registration of what the pilot is designed to test, defined success criteria established before deployment, and a commitment to publishing results regardless of outcome.

Three pilot designs are outlined here. They are not country-specific — specific country engagement requires diplomatic groundwork that is beyond this paper. They represent distinct pilot archetypes that test different aspects of the SDTP.

### 12.2 Pilot design A: Forest-carbon focus, bilateral official debt

**Context:** A nation with significant primary forest cover, substantial deforestation pressure, and official bilateral debt concentrated in one or two creditor nations.

**Debt scope:** Bilateral ODA debt to one partner creditor, representing approximately 15–25 percent of the nation's total external debt. This is a manageable subset for a first test — meaningful enough to generate real implementation experience, limited enough to be manageable if complications arise.

**Regenerative focus:** Leaves generated through verified primary forest protection and targeted reforestation in priority watersheds. The measurement methodology relies primarily on satellite remote sensing (established technology with decades of validation) combined with ground-truth sampling. This is the most technically mature component of the Leaves system and the appropriate starting point.

**Verification structure:** Tier 1 national forest monitoring submitted through a standardized reporting interface to the Love Ledger platform. Tier 2 verification by an accredited environmental civil society organization — ideally one with existing in-country presence and established relationships with forest-adjacent communities. Tier 3 SRC audit using open-source remote sensing analysis (Sentinel-2 and Landsat data are freely available; analysis pipelines can be built on existing open-source tooling).

**Key test questions:**
- Can Tier 1, Tier 2, and Tier 3 verification be brought into reasonable agreement, and what systematic discrepancies appear?
- What administrative burden does the reporting requirement impose on the national government, and is it proportionate to the debt relief obtained?
- Does the forest protection incentive shift government behavior in measurable ways, or does it primarily rebadge existing programs?
- How does the bilateral creditor experience the administrative process, and what would make it more workable?

**Anticipated challenges:** Political economy of forest protection (conflicts with agricultural expansion interests, Indigenous land rights tensions, enforcement against illegal logging). These challenges are the point — the pilot should surface them so the instrument design can respond.

### 12.3 Pilot design B: Care infrastructure focus, multilateral debt

**Context:** A nation with significant multilateral debt (World Bank, regional development bank), documented gaps in primary healthcare access, and reasonable existing administrative capacity.

**Debt scope:** A portion of multilateral development bank lending — ideally from a single institution prepared to participate as a development experiment. World Bank IBRD and IDA lending is governed by terms that allow significant flexibility in restructuring for development purposes; this is the natural institutional entry point.

**Regenerative focus:** Hearts generated through documented expansion of primary healthcare access — specifically community health worker programs, which have the strongest evidence base for rapid, scalable impact on health outcomes and which are relatively straightforward to measure (worker count, training status, catchment population, service utilization rates).

**Verification structure:** Tier 1 through national health management information systems, which most nations already maintain with World Bank support. Tier 2 through health-focused civil society organizations and patient advocacy groups. The community-accountability dimension of Tier 2 verification is particularly important for care infrastructure — service users are a direct source of evidence about whether claimed services are actually accessible and of adequate quality.

**Key test questions:**
- Can the Heart generation measurement methodology be applied consistently and at acceptable cost?
- Does the debt service incentive drive genuine expansion of care access, or does it primarily redirect existing health spending into SDTP-reportable categories?
- How do community health workers and their organizations experience the verification process — do they feel it accurately captures their work?
- What gaming strategies emerge, and how does the verification system detect them?

**Anticipated challenges:** Health system data quality varies significantly; the same intervention may appear well-documented in one country and essentially unverifiable in another. The pilot should produce honest assessment of minimum data quality requirements for Hearts measurement.

### 12.4 Pilot design C: Combined focus, climate-vulnerable small island state

**Context:** A small island developing state with acute climate-debt exposure, combined ecological (marine/coastal) and care infrastructure needs, and debt distributed across bilateral, multilateral, and commercial creditors.

**Debt scope:** A more ambitious structure than Pilots A and B — targeting debt from multiple creditor classes simultaneously. This requires more complex legal structuring and creditor coordination, which is precisely what makes it valuable as a pilot: it tests the SDTP's ability to function across the fragmented creditor landscape that is the actual reality for most heavily indebted nations.

**Rationale for this context:** Small island developing states are the most acute case for the SDTP. They bear the heaviest climate debt burden relative to their size, their primary ecological assets (coral reefs, mangroves, seagrass beds, fisheries) are among the most threatened globally, and their care infrastructure gaps are significant relative to the cost of addressing them. The Bridgetown Initiative's political energy is concentrated in this constituency. A successful SIDS pilot would generate the most politically compelling evidence for Phase 2 expansion.

**Regenerative focus:** Marine protected area establishment and management (Leaves), combined with community health worker expansion (Hearts). The dual focus tests the two-currency structure's operational feasibility.

**Verification structure:** As Pilots A and B, plus the additional complexity of marine verification — which requires combining remote sensing (for sea surface temperature, bleaching extent, turbidity) with in-water biological survey data. Marine surveys are more expensive and logistically demanding than forest surveys; the pilot should establish realistic cost benchmarks.

**Key test questions:**
- Is the two-currency structure operationally manageable for a small government with limited administrative capacity?
- Can multi-creditor SDTP restructuring be legally structured using collective action clause mechanisms?
- Does the combined marine-care program produce integrated benefits — healthier reef systems supporting fisheries that support community nutrition — that justify the integrated approach?
- What climate event triggers are appropriate for payment pause provisions, and how quickly can they be activated when needed?

**Anticipated challenges:** Small state administrative capacity is a real constraint; the reporting burden must be designed with this in mind. Creditor coordination across bilateral, multilateral, and commercial holders requires more complex negotiation than single-creditor pilots.

## Chapter 13: Capacity building requirements

### 13.1 The capacity gap

The SDTP makes significant demands on participating nations' institutional capacity. National ecological monitoring systems, health information systems, civil society verification networks, legal teams capable of negotiating novel debt instruments, and government departments capable of designing and overseeing qualifying regenerative programs — all of these require capabilities that are unevenly distributed across potential participating nations.

This is not a reason to scale back the instrument's ambitions. It is a reason to treat capacity building as a core component of the implementation pathway, not an afterthought. A program that requires capabilities that don't exist and provides no pathway to develop them will be disproportionately accessible to better-resourced nations — exactly the wrong selection effect.

### 13.2 National ecological monitoring capacity

Minimum requirements for SDTP participation in the ecological dimension:

**Remote sensing access and analysis.** Nations need the ability to process satellite imagery relevant to their priority ecosystems — at minimum, forest cover change analysis. The good news is that the tools are increasingly accessible: European Space Agency Sentinel data is freely available, Google Earth Engine provides cloud processing capacity at no cost for research and government use, and open-source analysis pipelines (like Global Forest Watch's) can be adapted. What is required is trained personnel to operate these tools and institutional structures to manage the data systematically.

**Ground-truth monitoring networks.** Satellite data must be validated against ground conditions. This requires trained field personnel operating systematic monitoring protocols — ranger networks, community monitors, academic partnerships. In many nations, elements of this infrastructure exist but are underfunded and inconsistent.

**Data management and reporting systems.** Standardized data formats and submission systems for Love Ledger integration require software development and institutional adoption. This is a defined technical deliverable that can be specified and resourced.

**Capacity building pathway:** The SRC technical secretariat should offer a structured national monitoring capacity assessment and development program, drawing on existing frameworks (UN-REDD's forest monitoring support, GBIF's biodiversity data infrastructure, WRI's monitoring partnerships) rather than building from scratch. Nations entering Phase 1 pilots receive dedicated technical assistance as part of their participation.

### 13.3 Civil society verification capacity

The Tier 2 verification function requires civil society organizations with specific capabilities: technical competence in the relevant ecological or care domain, organizational independence from both government and commercial interests, documented field presence in relevant territories, and institutional capacity to manage international accreditation processes.

In some nations, organizations meeting these requirements exist and need only orientation to SDTP-specific protocols. In others, the civil society sector is too small, too dependent on government funding, or too technically constrained to fulfill this function without significant development support.

Critically, civil society capacity cannot be developed under government control — organizations that are established or funded by participating governments to perform verification lack the independence that the verification function requires. Capacity development must be channeled through independent intermediaries: international civil society networks, foundations, or the SRC directly.

Legal protection for civil society verifiers is a non-negotiable prerequisite for SDTP participation. Governments that criminalize independent environmental monitoring, restrict civil society funding, or subject NGO staff to harassment cannot participate in the SDTP — not as a political condition but as a functional one. Verification that cannot be done safely cannot be done at all.

### 13.4 Legal and negotiation capacity

Negotiating SDTP debt transformation agreements requires legal expertise that most heavily indebted nations do not currently have in-house. Sovereign debt restructuring is a specialist field dominated by a small number of law firms operating primarily from New York and London — on behalf of creditors. Debtor nations frequently enter restructuring negotiations with inadequate legal representation, which contributes to the systematically creditor-favorable outcomes that existing mechanisms produce.

The SDTP implementation program should include dedicated legal capacity support for participating debtor nations: access to specialist counsel with SDTP-specific expertise, template documentation that reduces negotiation costs, and peer learning networks connecting debtor nation legal teams. The costs of this support are small relative to the amounts at stake and should be funded as part of the SRC's operational budget.

### 13.5 Government program design capacity

Qualifying regenerative programs must be designed and managed by national governments. This requires capabilities that differ from conventional government functions: ecological program management, care infrastructure expansion planning, and results measurement against defined metrics. Many heavily indebted nations have been through decades of austerity that have hollowed out exactly these capabilities.

Capacity building here is slower and more culturally specific than technical training. It requires sustained institutional development — creating agencies with the mandate and resources to manage regenerative programs over the multi-year timelines that SDTP performance requires. This cannot be imposed from outside; it must be supported from the inside, with international support that respects national ownership.

The implementation program should offer a structured government program design service — not prescribing what programs nations should run, but providing analytical and planning support for governments to develop programs that meet SDTP eligibility criteria while reflecting national priorities. This function sits within the SRC secretariat and is explicitly not a conditionality mechanism.

### 13.6 A note on resourcing

The capacity building program described in this chapter is not free. Staff, tools, legal support, training — these require sustained funding. The proposed funding source is the SRC operational budget, itself financed through a combination of Treaty-mandate multilateral contributions and a small administrative fee on SDTP transaction volumes.

For Phase 1 pilot deployment, capacity building costs should be treated as program development investment, funded through development finance channels — concessional lending or grants from development banks, philanthropic foundations, and willing creditor nation governments. The costs are modest relative to the sovereign debt amounts at stake: capacity building investment of tens of millions of dollars supports debt transformation arrangements worth billions.

This framing — treating capacity building as investment rather than overhead — matters for how it is positioned in funding conversations. The return on investment is the difference between a functioning SDTP arrangement and a failed one. Given the amounts of debt involved in even a Phase 1 pilot, the return is very high.
