# Part I: Diagnosis — The debt-growth trap

---

## Chapter 1: The architecture of extraction

### 1.1 How sovereign debt became a structural condition

Sovereign debt — governments borrowing to finance expenditure — is as old as the modern state. What changed in the mid-twentieth century was not the existence of debt but its architecture: who held it, in what currency, and under what conditions.

The Bretton Woods system established in 1944 created the institutional infrastructure for post-war sovereign lending — the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank — with mandates ostensibly oriented toward stability and development. The dollar's role as global reserve currency, anchored until 1971 to gold, meant that international borrowing almost universally required dollar-denominated repayment. This was not a neutral technical arrangement. It meant that debtor nations needed to generate dollars — not local welfare, not ecological health, not social stability — to service their obligations.

For industrialized nations with diversified export economies, this constraint was manageable. For the newly independent states of Africa, Asia, and Latin America emerging from colonialism in the 1950s and 1960s, it was structurally crippling. These nations inherited economies designed for extraction — mines, plantations, and resource concessions oriented toward supplying colonial metropoles — rather than domestic development. When they borrowed to build infrastructure, healthcare systems, and institutions, they borrowed in dollars. When commodity prices fell, their capacity to generate those dollars fell with them. The debt remained.

The 1970s oil price shocks compounded the dynamic. Petrodollars recycled through private international banks flooded emerging market borrowers with cheap credit. By 1982, the combination of rising US interest rates, falling commodity prices, and ballooning debt service costs triggered what became known as the developing-world debt crisis. Mexico's announcement in August 1982 that it could not service its external debt initiated a cascade: within two years, over forty nations were in acute debt distress.

The institutional response was structural adjustment. The IMF and World Bank offered conditional lending — debt rescheduling in exchange for economic reforms designed to restore market confidence and generate export earnings. The conditions were consistent: reduce public expenditure, devalue the currency, liberalize trade, privatize state enterprises, prioritize export production. In country after country, the result was the same: social spending collapsed, domestic industries were outcompeted by cheap imports, and export extraction accelerated. The debts were restructured, not resolved. The 1980s became a "lost decade" for development across Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa. GDP per capita in many heavily indebted nations ended the decade below where it had started.

### 1.2 The compounding mathematics of dollar-denominated debt

The structural problem with dollar-denominated sovereign debt is not primarily political — it is mathematical.

When a nation borrows in a foreign currency, it takes on an obligation that exists entirely outside its domestic monetary system. A government can, in principle, inflate away domestic currency debt by expanding the money supply. It cannot inflate away dollar-denominated debt. That debt can only be serviced by acquiring dollars — which means generating export revenue, attracting foreign investment, or borrowing more dollars to pay existing ones.

Generating export revenue at scale, in most lower-income economies, means extracting and selling natural resources: oil, minerals, timber, agricultural commodities. The more urgent the debt service obligation, the faster extraction must proceed. There is no equilibrium in this system where the pace of extraction voluntarily slows. Slower extraction means slower dollar generation means default — with all its attendant consequences: credit rating downgrades, loss of market access, capital flight, currency crisis.

The interest compounding dynamics make this worse over time, not better. A loan carrying a 5 percent annual interest rate doubles in approximately fourteen years. If the debtor economy is growing at 3 percent annually, the debt burden as a share of GDP grows by roughly 2 percent each year. To stabilize the debt ratio, the economy must grow at least as fast as the interest rate — and to reduce it, faster still. This is the growth imperative, and it is not a political preference. It is an arithmetic requirement built into the debt architecture.

For a resource-extracting economy, GDP growth and natural capital depletion are not separate variables — they move together. Growing faster means extracting faster. The debt system does not merely tolerate ecological destruction as a side effect; it requires it as a precondition of fiscal stability.

The trap is self-reinforcing.

### 1.3 Extraction as structural violence

There is a conventional way to discuss resource extraction in the context of development economics — as a necessary phase, a comparative advantage, a pathway to capital accumulation that can later be redirected toward diversification. This framing is not without historical examples: South Korea, Taiwan, and more recently Botswana have navigated commodity dependence toward more diversified economies. But these are exceptions made possible by specific institutional conditions, geopolitical timing, and, in some cases, resource scarcity that drove investment in human capital precisely because natural capital was limited.

For most heavily indebted resource-exporting nations, extraction-led growth has not produced diversification. It has produced resource dependence, institutional capture by extractive industries, and ecological deterioration. The mechanism is not mysterious. When debt servicing requires continuous export revenue, and export revenue derives primarily from natural resource extraction, the political economy of the state becomes aligned with sustaining extraction above all else. Environmental regulations that slow extraction threaten debt serviceability. Land rights that protect communities from displacement threaten mine and plantation expansion. Diversification that redirects investment from export sectors reduces dollar generation.

The debt system does not merely correlate with these outcomes. It creates the incentive structure that produces them.

To call this structural violence — using Johan Galtung's term for harm embedded in institutions rather than enacted by identifiable perpetrators — is analytically precise, not rhetorical. No individual creditor makes a decision to degrade an ecosystem. No single IMF program is designed to destroy a healthcare system. The harm emerges from the architecture, which is why addressing it requires architectural change rather than better intentions.

### 1.4 The false accounting: what debt calculations omit

Every sovereign debt sustainability analysis rests on an assumption so pervasive it is rarely stated: that natural capital is free.

When the IMF calculates a country's debt-to-GDP ratio and projects whether debt is "sustainable," GDP is the denominator. GDP counts the value of extracted resources when they are sold, but does not count the depletion of the underlying stock. A nation that clears a forest and sells the timber records a positive contribution to GDP. The loss of the carbon sequestration, watershed protection, biodiversity, and future timber value represented by that forest does not appear anywhere in the sustainability calculation. The debt is deemed serviceable because the income covers the repayment — even if generating that income has permanently reduced the nation's productive capacity.

This is not an obscure accounting quirk. It is a systematic distortion that makes extractive debt strategies appear sustainable when they are not. A nation running down its natural capital to service debt is not servicing debt sustainably — it is consuming its own future productive capacity, making each subsequent year of debt service more costly in real terms.

The Inclusive Wealth framework developed by the UN Environment Programme attempts to address this by measuring total capital — produced capital (infrastructure, machinery), human capital (health, education), and natural capital (ecosystems, minerals, fossil fuels) — as the true measure of a nation's wealth. When natural capital depletion is included, the "development" produced by extraction-led debt service often represents net impoverishment, not wealth accumulation. Nations that appear to be developing on GDP measures are frequently destroying wealth faster than they are creating it.

The Sovereign Debt Transformation Protocol builds this accounting correction into the debt instrument itself. By making regenerative output the measure of debt service rather than currency-denominated GDP growth, it makes the full cost of extractive strategies visible — and creates financial incentives for the alternative.

## Chapter 2: Why growth cannot solve the problem it created

### 2.1 The arithmetic impossibility

The standard prescription for debt-distressed economies is growth. Grow the economy fast enough and debt becomes manageable as a share of GDP. Generate sufficient export revenue and debt service becomes affordable. This is the logic behind every structural adjustment program, every IMF conditionality framework, every creditor insistence that the path to debt sustainability runs through economic expansion.

The arithmetic is straightforward to state but rarely stated plainly: for this to work, GDP growth must persistently exceed the real interest rate on outstanding debt. For many lower-income nations, real interest rates on external debt — particularly private market debt acquired in periods of cheap global capital — run between 6 and 12 percent annually. Sustained GDP growth above these rates is rare anywhere; it is essentially absent in economies whose growth model depends on extracting depleting natural resources while climate change progressively disrupts agriculture, fisheries, water availability, and infrastructure.

Consider the compounding dynamic from both directions simultaneously. The debt stock grows at the interest rate. The natural capital base — the primary source of export revenue for resource-dependent economies — depletes at the rate of extraction and degrades at the rate of climate impact. The GDP growth rate must exceed the debt growth rate while the productive base that generates GDP is shrinking. This is not a policy failure. It is an arithmetic impossibility over any sufficiently long time horizon.

The clean version of the counterargument is that growth need not come from natural resource extraction indefinitely — that accumulation of human capital and industrial capacity eventually drives the transition. This is historically accurate for a small number of countries under specific conditions. It is not a general law, and it is not occurring in the nations most severely trapped by extractive debt dynamics. The preconditions for industrial diversification — institutional quality, infrastructure, educated workforce, stable macroeconomic environment — are precisely what get destroyed when debt distress forces austerity, outward resource extraction, and political capture by extractive industries.

### 2.2 The climate compounding

A factor not present in previous debt crisis analyses is now inescapable: climate change is systematically undermining the productive capacity of the most heavily indebted nations while simultaneously generating new debt through disaster response.

The correlation is not coincidental. The nations most exposed to climate impacts — low-lying island states, Sahel-adjacent economies, South and Southeast Asian deltas — are disproportionately represented among heavily indebted lower-income countries. This is partly historical (colonial extraction concentrated in tropical regions, which are also most climate-exposed) and partly physical (tropical agriculture is more sensitive to temperature variation than temperate agriculture).

The economic mechanism is direct. When cyclones, floods, droughts, or heat events destroy agricultural output, infrastructure, or coastal assets, GDP falls. When GDP falls, the debt-to-GDP ratio rises and market access worsens. The affected government borrows to rebuild — typically at worsened terms, given the increased risk perception — adding to the debt stock. The next climate event, worsened by the accumulated carbon emissions of industrialized nations, destroys the rebuilt infrastructure and the cycle repeats.

Barbados, under Prime Minister Mia Mottley's "Bridgetown Initiative," has been among the most articulate voices for recognizing this structural injustice in international forums. The nations least responsible for cumulative greenhouse gas emissions bear the heaviest debt burden from managing the consequences. Climate adaptation spending competes directly with debt service for the same constrained government revenue. There is no path to adequate climate adaptation that runs through the existing debt architecture.

The Sovereign Debt Transformation Protocol addresses this directly. By converting debt service from currency extraction into ecological restoration and care infrastructure development, it transforms climate adaptation spending from a cost that worsens debt sustainability into a means of servicing debt. The debtor nation that invests in mangrove restoration, watershed protection, and community health resilience is simultaneously improving its climate resilience and reducing its debt obligation. The instrument architecture aligns incentives instead of inverting them.

### 2.3 The Zambia case: a detailed example

Zambia's debt trajectory illustrates the impossibility theorem in practice.

In the early 2000s, Zambia was among the beneficiaries of the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative — a multilateral debt relief program that cancelled approximately $3.9 billion in debt by 2005. The relief was real and provided temporary fiscal space for increased social investment. Zambia's health and education spending increased meaningfully in the mid-2000s.

Then copper prices spiked. Zambia borrowed heavily against its copper wealth — from bilateral creditors (primarily China), multilateral institutions, and private bondholders — to invest in infrastructure and development. Between 2012 and 2019, Zambia's external debt rose from approximately 20 percent of GDP to over 100 percent. Copper prices fell after 2011. The debt did not.

By 2020, Zambia had become the first African nation to default on external debt during the COVID-19 pandemic. Debt service consumed over 40 percent of government revenue. The IMF-backed restructuring process that followed stretched across three years, during which the Zambian economy contracted, social spending collapsed, and copper extraction — the only available source of foreign exchange — continued accelerating to generate the revenue that might eventually restore market access.

The restructured debt still exists. Zambia's copper deposits are depleting. The underlying dynamics have not changed. A generation of Zambians has experienced degraded healthcare, education, and economic opportunity to service obligations that were, from any reasonable analysis of the underlying resource base and climate trajectory, never repayable as structured.

This is not a Zambian failure. It is a system failure. The instrument was wrong.

### 2.4 The Goodhart's Law problem

There is a subtler dysfunction embedded in GDP-growth-based debt sustainability analysis: once GDP growth becomes the primary measure of debt serviceability, it becomes a target that distorts the economy in ways that undermine real productive capacity.

Charles Goodhart, the British economist, observed that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. In the context of debt-distressed economies, the perverse forms this takes are well-documented. Governments accelerate extraction to boost short-term GDP figures in advance of debt renegotiations. Informal care work, subsistence agriculture, and ecological services — which constitute a substantial share of real economic activity in many lower-income nations — are invisible in GDP calculations and therefore in debt sustainability assessments. Investments in health, education, and ecological restoration that improve long-term productive capacity but grow slowly do not generate the near-term GDP numbers that satisfy creditors.

The result is a systematic bias toward activities that look good on creditor-facing metrics while degrading the actual productive capacity of the economy. Nations optimize for the measure rather than the underlying reality it is supposed to represent.

The SDTP replaces GDP growth with verified regenerative output as the measure of debt serviceability. This shifts the optimization target. Governments that design policy around meeting SDTP obligations — restoring ecosystems, building care infrastructure — are simultaneously building the resilience and productive capacity that genuine long-term solvency requires. The measure and the goal are aligned rather than opposed.

## Chapter 3: The limits of existing debt relief mechanisms

### 3.1 A taxonomy of approaches

Five decades of debt crises have produced a range of institutional responses. Understanding why each falls short of addressing the structural problem clarifies what a genuine architectural solution requires.

**The Paris Club and bilateral rescheduling.** The Paris Club — an informal group of major creditor nations that coordinates debt rescheduling — has been the primary mechanism for managing bilateral sovereign debt distress since 1956. Its core function is rescheduling: extending repayment timelines and sometimes reducing nominal debt burdens. Paris Club agreements provide temporary relief without addressing the underlying growth imperative. They are patch operations on a failing architecture, and their creditor-coordination focus makes them increasingly inadequate as debt portfolios have diversified toward private bondholders and bilateral Chinese lending outside the Paris Club framework.

**The HIPC Initiative (1996–present).** The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative was the first multilateral attempt at substantial debt cancellation rather than mere rescheduling. For qualifying nations that completed a multi-year IMF reform program, HIPC offered cancellation of multilateral and bilateral debt to levels deemed "sustainable." The initiative genuinely helped — Zambia's post-HIPC fiscal space was real, as noted above — but HIPC's conditionality requirements embedded structural adjustment logic, qualifying nations were required to maintain IMF-approved policies before receiving full relief, and the relief proved insufficient as new borrowing cycles began. No HIPC graduate has achieved permanently sustainable debt dynamics.

**The Debt Service Suspension Initiative and G20 Common Framework (2020–present).** The COVID-19 pandemic prompted the G20 to launch first the DSSI (a temporary suspension of debt service payments) and then the Common Framework for Debt Treatments, a more structured approach to coordinating debt relief across G20 creditors. The Common Framework represented genuine institutional innovation in attempting to include China — now a major bilateral creditor — in coordinated debt treatment. In practice, it has been slow, opaque, and has covered very few countries. Zambia's three-year restructuring under the Common Framework is illustrative of both the potential and the limitations.

**IMF Special Drawing Rights allocations.** The IMF's 2021 allocation of $650 billion in Special Drawing Rights — reserve assets that countries can exchange for hard currency — was the largest in the institution's history and provided meaningful liquidity to many lower-income nations. SDR allocations do not address debt stock; they address temporary liquidity constraints. They are emergency tools, not structural solutions.

**Debt-for-nature swaps.** The most relevant precursor to the SDTP, debt-for-nature swaps have existed since the 1980s. In their basic form, a creditor (often a government or NGO) purchases sovereign debt at a discount on secondary markets and cancels it in exchange for the debtor nation committing equivalent funds to conservation. More recent innovations — including "blue bonds" for ocean conservation and the large-scale swap negotiated by Ecuador for Galápagos conservation in 2023 — have scaled the mechanism.

Debt-for-nature swaps demonstrate important things: that creditors will accept alternative repayment arrangements when they offer comparable economic value, that conservation commitments can be structured as binding instruments, and that there is political appetite for this kind of transaction among both debtor governments and certain classes of creditors. They have also revealed the mechanism's limitations: scale has been modest relative to total debt burdens, verification of conservation commitments has been inconsistent, and the basic architecture — debt cancellation in exchange for conservation spending — does not address the growth imperative built into the remaining debt.

### 3.2 The common failure mode

Each of these mechanisms shares a fundamental limitation: they treat debt relief as a quantity problem rather than an architecture problem.

The question they address is "how much debt should be cancelled or rescheduled?" The question they do not address is "why is this debt architecture systematically producing outcomes incompatible with human welfare and ecological survival?"

Cancelling 30 percent of Zambia's debt stock leaves the remaining 70 percent governed by the same architecture — denominated in dollars, requiring currency-generating export extraction, compounding at rates that will restore the original debt burden within years unless growth rates are implausibly high. The relief is real but temporary. The architecture reproduces the problem.

An architecture-level solution requires changing what debt service means — not how much of it is owed but what is accepted as payment, what measure determines sustainability, and what governance structure verifies and adjudicates compliance. This is what the SDTP attempts.

### 3.3 The creditor incentive problem

One reason existing mechanisms address quantity rather than architecture is that creditors — both institutional and private — have rational incentives to preserve the current architecture even when it produces poor outcomes for debtor nations.

Dollar-denominated debt with growth-based sustainability conditionality preserves creditor control. Creditors can continuously re-evaluate debt sustainability based on growth performance, creating leverage for policy conditionality. They can benefit from resource extraction by receiving repayment from export revenues. They can diversify their risk across many debtor nations without any single nation's distress threatening the system. The architecture serves creditor interests even when it fails debtor ones.

This is not a claim of bad faith. Most institutional creditors operate within fiduciary frameworks that require them to maximize return for their stakeholders — pension funds, taxpayers, shareholders. The architecture that harms Zambia is the same architecture that, from a narrow creditor perspective, functions as designed. Changing it requires either changing creditor incentives or offering creditors an alternative instrument that serves their interests better — which is precisely what the SDTP attempts through Leaves-denominated instruments backed by verifiable ecological value.

### 3.4 The missing element: regenerative accounting

What all existing mechanisms share — and what must change — is the treatment of ecological and social value as external to the financial calculation.

Debt relief frameworks measure sustainability in terms of debt-to-GDP ratios, debt service-to-revenue ratios, and export coverage ratios. None of these measures includes the value of the natural capital being depleted to generate the export revenue, the future costs of the climate impacts accelerated by that extraction, or the social value of the care infrastructure that debt austerity prevents building.

A complete accounting of sovereign debt sustainability would need to include:

- The net change in natural capital stock during the repayment period
- The projected future costs of climate impacts enabled by extraction-led growth
- The human capital losses from debt-service-driven austerity in health and education
- The opportunity costs of foregone regenerative investment

When these factors are included, many debt situations that appear marginally sustainable on conventional metrics become clearly unsustainable on any reasonable long-run analysis. And many alternative debt architectures that appear costly on conventional metrics — because they slow extraction and reduce near-term GDP growth — become clearly superior.

The Sovereign Debt Transformation Protocol is, at its core, an instrument for bringing this fuller accounting into the debt instrument itself. By making regenerative output the measure of debt service, it forces both debtor and creditor to engage with the full ledger of value creation and destruction — not just the portion visible in foreign exchange flows.
