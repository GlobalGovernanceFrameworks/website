## Chapter 14: Guidance for specific stakeholder groups

### 14.1 Why audience-specific framing matters

The SDTP requires different things from different actors. A finance minister in a heavily indebted nation needs to understand what SDTP participation would mean for their fiscal position and what political capital it would require. A bilateral development bank needs to understand the administrative process and how SDTP instruments fit their mandate. An Indigenous governance body needs to understand how their rights are protected and what role they can play. A civil society organization needs to understand what accreditation as a Tier 2 verifier involves.

This chapter addresses each of these groups directly. The goal is not to flatten the instrument's complexity into talking points but to identify the entry point most relevant to each audience — what they need to know first in order to engage seriously.

### 14.2 For finance ministers and treasury officials in heavily indebted nations

**The core question you need answered:** Is SDTP participation a genuine improvement on our current situation, or does it introduce new dependencies and constraints while providing inadequate debt relief?

The honest answer is: it depends on your debt profile, ecological and social baseline, and government program capacity. The instrument is not universally attractive to all debtor nations. Nations with strong export earnings and manageable debt trajectories have little reason to engage with the complexity of SDTP. Nations whose debt trajectory is clearly unsustainable on conventional terms — where the arithmetic of compounding interest against resource-dependent growth does not close — have the most to gain.

**What participation requires from your government:**

Establishing national monitoring baselines for the ecological and care dimensions you intend to target. This is investment that has value independent of the SDTP — you need this monitoring capacity for domestic governance regardless. Designing and managing qualifying regenerative programs that are genuinely additional to existing trajectories. Engaging seriously with the verification process, including accepting civil society verification that is independent of government control.

**What you should not accept:**

Conversion rates set without transparent methodology. Conditionality on policies beyond the regenerative programs themselves. Verification structures that allow creditors to audit domestic governance more broadly. Eligibility criteria that effectively mandate specific land use decisions.

**The political economy consideration:**

SDTP participation requires domestic political management. Agricultural, extractive, and development interests that have benefited from the current architecture will perceive regenerative program requirements as constraints on their activities — because they are. The instrument is designed to shift incentives away from extraction; those whose interests are bound to extraction will resist it. This is not a reason to avoid SDTP, but it is a reason to build coalition support before committing, and to sequence early programs in areas where political economy allows.

**Entry point:** Contact the SRC secretariat (when operational) to request a preliminary SDTP eligibility and baseline assessment. This is a non-committal analytical step that clarifies whether the instrument is worth pursuing further.

### 14.3 For bilateral creditor governments

**The core question you need answered:** Does accepting SDTP restructuring of our sovereign lending portfolio improve or worsen our expected returns, and how does it sit with our development mandates?

**On expected returns:**

For bilateral ODA debt to highly stressed nations, the realistic alternatives are continued non-performance, protracted Paris Club renegotiation, or write-down. SDTP restructuring offers a potentially superior path: conversion of a marginal asset into a verified regenerative output with secondary market potential. The expected value comparison favors SDTP engagement in most acute distress scenarios. The uncertainty is real — the instrument is novel — but so is the certainty of poor outcomes under conventional approaches for the most stressed debtors.

**On development mandates:**

Bilateral development lenders almost universally have climate and development mandates that the SDTP directly serves. Participating in Phase 1 pilots is consistent with — and in many cases explicitly aligned with — stated development objectives. It also provides demonstrable evidence of mandate delivery that is increasingly demanded by domestic political audiences.

**Practical considerations:**

Phase 1 SDTP arrangements require additional administrative capacity for SRC engagement and Love Ledger reporting integration. This is a real cost. For large bilateral programs, it is justified by the portfolio benefits. For small programs, it may not be — bilateral creditors with very small exposures to a given debtor nation may reasonably conclude that the administrative overhead does not justify participation.

**The first step:**

Engage with the SRC's creditor liaison function (when operational) to understand the administrative process and legal template structure. The instrument is designed to minimize novel legal drafting through standardized templates; creditor legal teams should not be required to invent new frameworks.

### 14.4 For multilateral development banks and the IMF

**The core question you need answered:** How does the SDTP fit within your existing debt sustainability framework, and what institutional changes would participation require?

**The debt sustainability analysis question** is the most consequential. If IMF debt sustainability analysis continues to treat Leaves-denominated obligations identically to currency-denominated ones — counting them as debt at face value without adjustment for their regenerative backing — the instrument provides no DSA benefit to participating nations, which significantly reduces its attractiveness. The SDTP requires IMF methodological engagement: developing a treatment for regenerative-denominated obligations that reflects their different risk profile.

This is not a small ask. It requires internal IMF policy work and, ultimately, executive board decisions. It is also not unprecedented — the IMF has adapted its sustainability frameworks in response to instrument innovation before (catastrophe bonds, climate-linked clauses). The case for adaptation here is that the instrument's risk profile is genuinely different from conventional debt, not that it should receive preferential treatment for political reasons.

**For World Bank and regional development banks:**

The opportunity is on the origination side as much as the restructuring side. New loans originated in SDTP-compatible form — with defined ecological and care performance provisions built into the loan agreement from the start — create the stock of transformable debt that Phase 2 and Phase 3 require. This is a design change in standard lending instruments that is within the operational discretion of development bank management.

**For the IMF specifically:**

The Resilience and Sustainability Trust (RST) and the existing framework of climate-linked conditionality provide institutional entry points for SDTP alignment. The argument is not that the IMF should run the SDTP — it should not; governance asymmetry would be reproduced — but that IMF frameworks should recognize SDTP instruments in ways that do not penalize participating nations in standard program contexts.

### 14.5 For private bondholders and institutional investors

**The core question you need answered:** Is there a market here, and if so, under what conditions?

**For distressed debt investors:**

SDTP restructuring of commercial bonds requires collective action clause application — supermajority consent binding all holders. Distressed debt investors who have accumulated positions specifically to extract value through restructuring negotiations may find SDTP less favorable than conventional restructuring in some scenarios. The instrument is not designed to be attractive to extractive creditor strategies. It is designed to be attractive to creditors who prefer performing assets over extended workout situations — a different investor profile.

**For institutional investors with ESG mandates:**

The secondary market for Leaves instruments, when developed in Phase 2, is the relevant opportunity. Verified ecological outputs with transparent governance and auditable performance are exactly the kind of instrument that ESG-mandated portfolios struggle to source in adequate supply. Early institutional engagement in secondary market development — providing the market-making function that creates liquidity — positions investors to access a growing asset class from the ground floor.

**For impact investors:**

Impact investors currently operating in development finance — blended finance vehicles, development finance institution co-investment — are the natural Phase 1 commercial partners. The credit enhancement structures described in Chapter 8, combining MDB guarantees with commercial co-investment, are designed to produce risk-return profiles compatible with impact investment mandates.

**The honest assessment:**

There is no established secondary market for Leaves instruments. The legal framework is undeveloped. The governance is untested. Phase 1 investment requires tolerance for genuine uncertainty. What it also offers is the possibility of being formative participants in an instrument class that, if it scales, will be very large indeed — the universe of climate-vulnerable sovereign debt that could eventually participate in some version of this architecture runs into the trillions.

### 14.6 For Indigenous governance bodies and communities

**The core question you need answered:** Does the SDTP protect our rights and recognize our authority, or does it create new mechanisms for outsiders to make decisions about our territories under an ecological banner?

The concern is legitimate and has historical grounding. Conservation programs have a long record of displacing Indigenous communities from their territories under the banner of nature protection. Carbon offset projects have sometimes proceeded without genuine FPIC, used procedural compliance to substitute for substantive consent, and captured economic benefits at the community level that communities were promised but did not receive. The SDTP must be evaluated against this history, not against its own stated intentions.

**What the design provides:**

FPIC requirements as eligibility criteria — activities that do not demonstrate genuine FPIC do not qualify for Leaves or Hearts generation. Indigenous governance body representation in the SRC and in Tier 2 verification. Community-accessible escalation pathways to the Digital Justice Tribunal that do not require national government intermediation. Prohibition on SDTP conditionality affecting Indigenous territorial governance.

**What the design requires you to assess:**

Whether FPIC requirements are genuinely enforced or are procedural compliance exercises. Whether SRC representation gives Indigenous governance bodies substantive influence over conversion rates and methodology, or is advisory only. Whether the Digital Justice Tribunal is genuinely accessible to communities — in terms of language, legal standing, and cost — or is practically available only to governments and large organizations.

These questions cannot be answered by the design specification. They depend on how the instrument operates in practice. This is an argument for Indigenous governance bodies to be involved in Phase 1 pilot design from the beginning — not as beneficiaries of a designed program but as co-designers of the instrument itself. Their participation in that process is what gives the FPIC requirements credibility.

**Entry point:**

Engage with the GGF project directly on the governance design questions — specifically SRC composition, Tier 2 verification accreditation criteria, and Digital Justice Tribunal accessibility. These are open design questions where Indigenous governance expertise is not supplementary but essential.

### 14.7 For civil society organizations and research institutions

**The core question you need answered:** How do you engage with an instrument that is still in design — and what does credible engagement look like?

**For environmental and development NGOs:**

The Tier 2 verification function is the most concrete near-term engagement opportunity. Organizations that wish to be part of the SDTP verification infrastructure should begin developing the technical capacity, institutional independence standards, and international accreditation frameworks that the role requires — even before the SRC exists to formally accredit them. The lead time on institutional development is long; starting now is appropriate.

The more important near-term role is critical engagement with the instrument design. The SDTP has significant potential failure modes — greenwashing, Indigenous rights violations, governance capture — and civil society organizations are the constituency most likely to detect and resist these in practice. Constructive critical engagement now, while the design is still open, is more valuable than opposition later.

**For research institutions and economists:**

The modeling gaps identified throughout this paper represent a research agenda that is both practically important and intellectually interesting. Formal macroeconomic modeling of SDTP transition effects, ecosystem service valuation methodology development, care economy measurement framework design, and legal framework analysis are all tractable research projects with genuine policy relevance. The GGF project invites formal research partnerships and is committed to publishing results regardless of whether they support or challenge the SDTP's claims.

The most valuable research contribution would be honest stress-testing: finding the conditions under which the SDTP does not work, the assumptions on which its viability most depends, and the instrument design choices most likely to produce perverse outcomes. Proposals that can only be validated by research that confirms them are not research hypotheses — they are advocacy. The SDTP is offered as a research hypothesis.

## Chapter 15: What this paper has and has not established

### 15.1 The honest summary

This paper has made a structural diagnosis and proposed an architectural response. What it has established with reasonable confidence:

The existing sovereign debt architecture is structurally incompatible with ecological sustainability and equitable human development for the nations most burdened by it. This is not a claim about intentions — it is a claim about architecture. The growth imperative embedded in dollar-denominated, compound-interest debt structures requires extraction at a pace that depletes natural capital faster than it can be regenerated, while simultaneously constraining the social investment that would enable genuine development. Existing debt relief mechanisms address quantity without addressing architecture, producing temporary relief that the underlying dynamics erode.

The logical structure of a regenerative debt instrument — one that makes ecological and care restoration the measure of debt service rather than a modifier on currency obligations — is sound. The SDTP's basic design addresses the right problem at the right level. Transforming what debt service means, rather than how much of it is owed, is the architectural intervention that the diagnosis requires.

The instrument is feasible in principle: it does not require creditors to absorb losses, does not require debtor nations to subordinate development to conservation, is compatible with existing legal frameworks as a starting point, and has genuine precedents in debt-for-nature swaps and sustainability-linked instruments.

What this paper has not established:

Formal macroeconomic modeling of the instrument's effects. The claims in Part III are analytical arguments, not model outputs. The modeling required to move from argument to evidence is identified and characterized but not performed.

Legal feasibility analysis by international debt law specialists. The legal framework described in Chapter 7 is preliminary. Engagement with the specialist legal community is required before the instrument can be structured for real transactions.

Verified engagement with creditor institutions, debtor governments, or Indigenous governance bodies. This paper is a proposal directed at these constituencies; it is not the outcome of a process in which they have participated. The instrument's design has been developed from the GGF project's internal research, not from stakeholder co-design.

A functioning verification infrastructure. The Love Ledger platform and its associated verification processes are design specifications. The gap between specification and working software and institutional infrastructure is substantial and should not be understated.

### 15.2 The central claim, restated

The SDTP rests on one central claim that underlies everything else: the instruments we use to govern sovereign debt determine the behaviors that debt-burdened nations adopt, and current instruments require behaviors that are incompatible with the future we need.

This claim is not novel. The Bridgetown Initiative makes it. The UNCTAD trade and development reports make it. Academic economists working on sovereign debt sustainability increasingly make it. What the SDTP adds is a specific architectural proposal grounded in the GGF's broader regenerative economics framework — a proposal specific enough to be implemented if the political conditions allow, and honest enough about its own limitations to invite the engagement that would make it better.

The goal is not to win the argument. The goal is to ensure that when political conditions create an opening for structural change in global debt architecture — as they will, as the costs of the current architecture accumulate — there is a worked alternative available. The opening may come from a cascade of defaults among climate-vulnerable nations. It may come from the fiscal impossibility of climate adaptation under current debt loads becoming undeniable to creditor governments. It may come from the political realignment that accompanies the decline of current geopolitical arrangements.

When it comes, something will fill it. This paper argues for what that something should be.

## Chapter 16: Research agenda

### 16.1 Priorities for formal modeling

**Macroeconomic transition modeling.** Country-specific CGE models of SDTP transition effects for representative debtor nation types — covering exchange rate effects, employment impacts of care sector expansion, trade balance dynamics, and fiscal position trajectories. This work is appropriate for development economics research institutions and should be commissioned as a funded research program rather than left to emerge organically.

**Creditor portfolio effects.** Formal expected value analysis comparing SDTP instruments to realistic alternatives (default, extended restructuring, continued non-performing carrying) across different debt profile scenarios. This requires collaboration with sovereign debt specialists at multilateral institutions or investment banks with relevant portfolio data.

**Game-theoretic analysis of adoption dynamics.** Modeling of how creditor behavior changes under different SDTP adoption scenarios — specifically whether the instrument creates precedent effects that materially widen spreads on conventional sovereign debt, and under what conditions coalition-building among debtor nations accelerates versus stalls adoption.

**Secondary market pricing models.** Financial modeling of Leaves instrument pricing under different ecological verification accuracy assumptions, liquidity scenarios, and ESG regulatory environments. This work is appropriate for quantitative finance researchers with access to comparable asset pricing data.

### 16.2 Priorities for measurement methodology development

**Biodiversity metric standardization.** The Leaves framework's biodiversity dimension is currently the weakest link in the measurement chain — there is no single accepted methodology for biodiversity accounting comparable to carbon accounting. The SDTP joins a broader scientific conversation about this gap. Priority research: identifying which biodiversity metrics are most closely correlated with ecosystem service value, and which are most verifiable with current monitoring technology.

**Care economy measurement framework.** A rigorous methodology for converting care infrastructure investment and service delivery into standardized Hearts units, calibrated across different country contexts. This requires collaboration between health economists, development researchers, and practitioners with experience in community health program monitoring.

**Additionality assessment methodology.** Developing the analytical framework for assessing additionality in both ecological and care dimensions — distinguishing activities that genuinely would not have occurred without the SDTP incentive from those that would. Drawing on carbon market additionality methodology while adapting it to the SDTP's broader scope.

### 16.3 Priorities for legal and institutional development

**Sovereign debt law analysis.** A comprehensive legal opinion on the SDTP instrument's compatibility with English and New York law governing sovereign debt, identification of the legal innovations required, and a roadmap for achieving regulatory recognition. This should be commissioned from a law firm with sovereign debt restructuring practice.

**SRC governance design.** Detailed institutional design for the Social Resilience Council — governance rules, composition requirements, decision-making procedures, conflict of interest policies, and accountability mechanisms. This benefits from comparative institutional analysis drawing on existing international governance bodies (IPCC, FSB, Basel Committee) for both positive models and cautionary cases.

**Digital Justice Tribunal access design.** Specific design work on how the Digital Justice Tribunal can be made genuinely accessible to communities affected by SDTP implementation — addressing language, standing, cost, and procedural barriers. This requires participatory design processes with communities who would use it.

### 16.4 Priorities for empirical evidence

**Debt-for-nature swap outcomes analysis.** Systematic review of existing debt-for-nature swap outcomes — what ecological results were achieved, what governance structures worked, what failed, and what the evidence shows about long-run conservation commitment compliance. This empirical base is the SDTP's most direct precedent and deserves rigorous analysis.

**Care infrastructure investment returns.** Economic analysis of the long-run productivity and fiscal returns to care infrastructure investment in low-income nations — quantifying the human capital, labor market, and fiscal multiplier effects that are theoretically claimed as benefits of the Hearts dimension. This evidence base strengthens the argument for care infrastructure as a genuine form of productive investment rather than consumption.

**Community impacts of conservation finance.** Field research on how existing conservation finance programs — including debt-for-nature swaps, REDD+, and payment for ecosystem services schemes — affect communities in and around target ecosystems. Understanding the distribution of benefits and harms at the community level is essential for designing SDTP programs that do not reproduce the displacement and exclusion that have characterized some earlier approaches.

### 16.5 An invitation

This research agenda is not a wish list — it is a specification of what is needed to move from design proposal to operational instrument. Different elements of it are appropriate for different kinds of institutions: academic economics departments, international financial institutions, specialist law firms, civil society research organizations, and communities with direct experience of the problems the SDTP addresses.

The GGF project is committed to open-sourcing the analytical work that underpins this paper, engaging seriously with critical responses, and revising the instrument design in response to evidence. We do not hold the SDTP as a finished answer. We hold it as the best current version of a question that urgently needs to be asked: what would it take to make sovereign debt serve the future rather than foreclose it?

We invite responses, critiques, collaborative research proposals, and direct engagement at globalgovernanceframeworks.org.
