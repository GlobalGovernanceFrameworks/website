## 1. Vision: Grief as a Sacred Commons

In the face of the polycrisis, humanity experiences unprecedented loss—of species, ecosystems, cultures, and stability. This collective grief, including the profound pain of ecological loss (*solastalgia*), is not a pathology to be suppressed but a sacred responsibility to be held.

This framework establishes the governance, rituals, and commons to honor death as a dignified transition, process collective grief as a source of communal wisdom, and memorialize loss as a commitment to intergenerational healing. We empower communities to lead their own practices, transforming grief from a private burden into a shared, regenerative practice, weaving remembrance into the fabric of a resilient, compassionate civilization.

**MVP Promise:** Every community has access to trained grief responders, dignified death care, and physical space to mourn—compensated through Hearts for those who hold this sacred work.

---

## 2. Core Principles

1. **Grief as a Sacred Commons:** Collective grief is a shared experience and a public good, requiring communal spaces and rituals for its expression and transformation.

2. **Dignity in Transition:** Every sentient being has the right to a dignified, compassionate, and culturally appropriate end-of-life process.

3. **Ecological Kinship & Mourning:** Humanity has a sacred duty to witness, mourn, and memorialize the loss of our non-human kin and the desecration of ecosystems, recognizing non-human grief as integral to this kinship.

4. **Ritual Integrity & Pluralism:** Grief and memorial practices must honor the diverse wisdom of global traditions, guided by the principle of non-appropriation and deep cultural respect.

5. **Remembrance as a Regenerative Act:** Memorializing loss is a living practice that informs future action and strengthens planetary stewardship.

6. **Community-Led Empowerment:** All rituals, responses, and practices must be co-designed and led by affected communities, with resources and structures to support their sovereignty.

7. **Subsidiarity in Compensation:** The value of grief work is determined locally by communities, not centrally by global frameworks. The framework guarantees the *principle* of compensation and protection from exploitation, not fixed rates.

8. **Care, Not Metrics:** The success of grief work is measured by whether communities feel held—not by algorithmic scores or bureaucratic indices.

9. **Standalone Viability:** The framework must function with minimal dependencies (Hearts, land, community) before integrating with larger GGF infrastructure.

## 3. A Typology of Grief (Non-Exhaustive)

To guide implementation and training, this framework recognizes distinct yet interconnected types of grief:

- **Personal Grief:** Loss of loved ones, family, or close relationships
- **Ecological Grief:** Distress from ecosystem collapse, species extinction, or environmental degradation (solastalgia, eco-anxiety, species grief)
- **Cultural & Historical Grief:** Loss of languages, traditions, ancestral lands, or cultural identity due to colonial trauma or displacement
- **Future Grief:** Anticipatory grief for potential losses (e.g., youth fearing climate-driven futures)
- **Unspoken Grief:** Grief not socially acknowledged, often experienced by marginalized communities

This typology is non-rigid, serving as a flexible guide for designing rituals, training grief responders, and tailoring interventions to community needs.

### Pillar 1: Dignified Transition Protocols

A set of community-developed standards and resources for end-of-life care, ensuring dignity and support for all.

**Core Components:**

| Component | Description | Status for MVP |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|

### Pillar 4: Care Indicators (Not KPIs)

The success of grief work is measured by whether communities feel held—not by algorithmic scores.

| Care Indicator | Question | Method | MVP Status |
|----------------|---------|--------|------------|
| **Availability** | Are death doulas and grief responders available when needed? | Service tracking; community reporting | Phase 1 Core |
| **Accessibility** | Can all community members access support regardless of identity? | Participation demographics; community feedback | Phase 1 Core |
| **Cultural Appropriateness** | Do rituals and practices align with community values? | Community reflection circles (qualitative) | Phase 1 Core |
| **Sustainability** | Are grief workers adequately compensated and supported? | Hearts distribution data; worker well-being circles | Phase 1 Core |
| **Community Ownership** | Does the community feel these are *their* practices? | Reflection circles; storytelling gatherings | Phase 1 Core |

**What This Does NOT Include in MVP:**
- Grief-to-Resilience Index (removed)
- Transformative Action Score (removed)
- Collective Efficacy Score (removed)
- AI-driven assessment (removed)

**Accountability Practice:**
- **Community Reflection Circles:** Quarterly gatherings to share what is healing and what is not
- **Story Gathering:** Documenting narratives of grief and resilience without scoring
- **Grievance and Repair Log:** Tracking unresolved harms and actions toward repair
- **External Companion:** Trained facilitator from another BAZ offers reflective feedback (not evaluation) every 2 years

**Do No Harm Protocol:**
1. Community consent for any data collection
2. Transparency about how information will be used
3. Right to opt out without penalty
4. Data sovereignty: community owns its own stories
5. No external comparison: no ranking of communities against each other

### Pillar 5: Indigenous Sovereignty & Cultural Integrity

All Indigenous practices are governed by FPIC 2.0, with Indigenous communities maintaining full authority over their grief traditions.

**Core Protections:**

| Protection | Description | Status |
|------------|-------------|--------|
| **FPIC 2.0** | No Indigenous practice used without explicit, revocable consent | Phase 1 Core |
| **Indigenous-Only Spaces** | Sacred burial sites; ceremonial grounds; restricted knowledge archives | Phase 1 Core |
| **Compensation for Sharing** | If a community shares a practice, direct compensation (Hearts) required | Phase 1 Core |
| **Right to Withdraw** | Shared practices may be withdrawn at any time | Phase 1 Core |
| **Cultural Authority** | Indigenous communities determine representation, protocols, and governance | Phase 1 Core |

**Standalone Mode:**
- Indigenous communities operate their own grief practices independently; framework provides funding and space but does not direct
- If a community chooses not to engage with the framework, full autonomy respected

## 6. Economic Integration (Locally-Determined)

### Core Principle

The Memorial Commons guarantees the *principle* of compensation for grief work without dictating fixed Hearts rates. Economic sovereignty resides with local BAZs.

### What the Framework Mandates

| Protection | Description |
|------------|-------------|
| **Compensation Guarantee** | All grief workers recognized by their BAZ must receive Hearts compensation for their labor |
| **Local Rate Setting** | Each BAZ determines Hearts rates through transparent, community-led process |
| **Rest & Rotation** | Mandated rest periods after intensive grief work (e.g., after 3 deployments, 2 weeks minimum) |
| **Supervision & Peer Support** | Funded through Hearts; prevents burnout and secondary trauma |
| **Emergency Hearts** | Available for grief workers experiencing secondary trauma or compassion fatigue |

### What the Framework Does NOT Mandate

- Fixed global Hearts rates
- Centralized compensation formulas
- External audits of local rates
- Comparison between BAZs

### Locally-Determined Rate Factors (Guidance, Not Rules)

BAZs may consider:
- Local cost of living and economic conditions
- Complexity and intensity of the grief work
- Training and experience level
- Cultural expectations around care work
- Available Hearts supply and circulation

### Example Local Processes

| BAZ Type | Rate Setting Process | Outcome |
|----------|---------------------|---------|
| Small rural BAZ | Town meeting consensus | Death doulas earn 40 Hearts/day; Grief First Responders 60 Hearts/deployment |
| Urban BAZ | Council vote after community consultation | Standardized rates across care roles; grief workers earn equivalent to teachers |
| Indigenous-led BAZ | Elder council with community input | Rates set seasonally; integrated with traditional gift economy |

### Appendix B: Hearts Compensation Guidance (Not Rules)

**For BAZ Councils Setting Local Rates:**

Consider:
- What is the local cost of living? (Hearts equivalent to meaningful economic participation)
- What is the complexity and intensity of the work? (Death doulas often require more training and emotional capacity than grief circle facilitators)
- What training and experience are required? (Apprentice vs. journeyman vs. master rates)
- What is the cultural context? (In some communities, grief work is considered sacred and may not be fully compensated in Hearts; supplementary support may be appropriate)
- What is the Hearts supply? (Rates should be sustainable within local circulation)

**Rest and Rotation Standards:**
- After 3 intensive deployments (e.g., disaster response): 2 weeks minimum rest
- After 6 months of continuous grief work: 1 week minimum rest
- Supervision: Monthly peer supervision for active grief workers
- Emergency Hearts: Available for grief workers experiencing secondary trauma; no questions asked

### Appendix C: Indigenous Sovereignty Protocols

**FPIC 2.0 Requirements:**
- Consent sought well in advance of any use
- Full disclosure of how practices will be used
- Right to say no without consequence
- Right to withdraw consent at any time
- Written agreement (oral where appropriate)
- Ongoing consultation, not one-time permission

**Indigenous-Only Spaces:**
- Sacred burial sites: Access restricted to community members
- Ceremonial grounds: Entry by invitation only
- Restricted knowledge archives: Not shared outside community
- Decision-making bodies: Indigenous-only for matters affecting Indigenous practices

**Compensation for Shared Practices:**
- If a practice is shared through Ritual Template Bank: one-time Hearts payment
- If a practice is used in training materials: ongoing royalty if requested
- If a practice is adapted: consultation with originating community; compensation as agreed

## 11. Conclusion: The Gift of the Memorial Commons

This framework offers something profound that no other GGF document addresses: **the right to grieve, and the infrastructure to hold grief collectively.**

By stripping away:
- **Dependency hell** (Standalone Mode ensures deployment without 30 other frameworks)
- **Tech distractions** (AR/VR removed; land-based memorials prioritized)
- **Grief bureaucracy** (qualitative care indicators replace algorithmic scores)
- **Centralized price-fixing** (local Hearts determination replaces global rates)

We reveal the framework's true gift:
- **Death doulas** who ensure dignified transitions
- **Grief first responders** who arrive when disaster strikes
- **Memorial forests** where communities can mourn lost species
- **Hearts compensation** (locally determined) for the heaviest emotional work
- **Community reflection circles** where grief is witnessed, not measured

This framework is designed to launch *small*, learn from communities, and grow organically. The grief workers of the world are already doing this work without compensation or systemic support. Giving them a commons—and local economic dignity for their labor—is enough for Phase 1. The rest can follow, by community invitation, not global mandate.
