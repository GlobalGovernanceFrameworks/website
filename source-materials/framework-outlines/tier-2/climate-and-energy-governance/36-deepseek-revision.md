# Climate & Energy Governance Framework v2

## A Charter for Planetary Stewardship

We, the stewards of Earth, commit to a sacred covenant: to restore and sustain planetary stability. Guided by justice, equity, and the wisdom of Indigenous and traditional knowledge, we pledge to recalibrate humanity's relationship with energy — from extraction to regeneration. This framework is our collective blueprint to secure a thriving, resilient, and equitable future for all life on our only home.

---

## Plain Language Summary

*This framework uses technical terms because precision matters. But we also want it to be readable. Here is what the key terms mean:*

- **Regenerative Trade Zone (RTZ):** A coalition of countries, regions, and communities that agree to trade on terms that reward clean energy and ecological restoration. Being in the zone creates economic advantages that draw others in — not through enforcement, but through attraction.

- **Bioregional Autonomous Zone (BAZ):** A self-governing region defined by ecological boundaries rather than political borders. BAZs are the building blocks of the regenerative economy.

- **Hearts and Leaves:** Currencies that reward care work (Hearts) and ecological restoration (Leaves). They circulate alongside money, creating value for work that markets ignore — caring for each other, restoring ecosystems, building community.

- **Sundown Protocol:** The framework for phasing out fossil fuels — not through protest and conflict, but through managed transition that protects workers and communities. It ensures that those who powered the extractive era are the first to benefit from the regenerative one.

- **Hearthstone Protocol:** The framework for building community-owned renewable energy — taking control of power generation and keeping the benefits local. It reclaims energy from abstraction and returns it to what it has always been: the capacity to keep each other warm, to power our lives, to build together.

For a full glossary, see Appendix F.

## 1. What This Framework Creates

Imagine a community that generates its own clean power — owned not by a distant utility, but by the people who live there. Imagine energy bills replaced by energy dividends. Imagine fossil fuel infrastructure not as a battleground of protests and arrests, but as decommissioned sites being restored to marshland, forest, or community garden — the workers who once extracted carbon now restoring ecosystems, their livelihoods secured through a just transition.

This is not a dream. It is already happening in communities from Germany to Costa Rica, from Indigenous-led territories to progressive cities. What is missing is not the possibility, but the architecture to scale it — to connect these islands of regeneration into a self-reinforcing system that outcompetes the extractive economy on every metric that matters.

**This framework is that architecture.**

It is built around a single strategic insight: **the transition will not be enforced from above; it will be pulled from the side.** The Regenerative Trade Zone (RTZ) is the engine of that pull — a coalition of nations, BAZs, and communities that agree to trade on regenerative terms, creating an economic gravity that draws others in.

## 3. Governance Structure

### 3.1 Global Oversight Body

**Role:** Coordinate policies, monitor progress, and enforce agreements. Integrates with UNFCCC processes and enhances Paris Agreement enforcement.

**Enforcement Tools:**
- Trade penalties and climate tariffs
- Restricted access to green technology funds
- Alignment with WTO exemptions (GATT Article XX)

**Dispute Resolution:** International Climate Tribunal — a specialized chamber within the Digital Justice Tribunal. Begins as an advisory body, evolving as consensus builds.

**Climate Emergency Clause:** Authority to invoke emergency powers, triggered by the Planetary Health Council when specific Biosphere Health Index (BHI) thresholds are breached:
- Global average temperature above 1.5°C for 12 consecutive months
- Methane release exceeding 50 gigatons CO2 equivalent annually
- Critical ecological tipping points reached (e.g., Amazon rainforest collapse)

**Safeguards:** Independent audits and rotating leadership prevent capture by powerful interests.

### 3.2 Regional Hubs

**Role:** Tailor global goals to regional contexts (Africa, Asia-Pacific, Americas, etc.). Coordinate with subnational actors — cities, regions, Indigenous territories.

**Focus:** Local capacity building, resource allocation, and resilience to political backsliding through multi-stakeholder coalitions.

### 3.3 National Implementation Units

**Role:** Execute policies and report progress, aligned with Paris Agreement Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs).

**Emphasis:** Sovereignty respected, with fallback mechanisms (e.g., regional oversight) during national political shifts.

### 3.4 Governance of Controversial Technologies

The framework recognizes that certain technologies — nuclear energy, carbon capture and storage, and solar radiation management (geoengineering) — are deeply contested. It does not resolve these debates but establishes governance structures to ensure that any deployment occurs with full accountability.

**Nuclear Energy:** Considered on a case-by-case basis with strict conditions:
- Consent from affected communities, including Indigenous nations whose territories would host facilities
- Demonstrated solutions for long-term waste storage with intergenerational accountability
- Adherence to safety standards set by the Global Technology Council, with independent oversight
- Preference for small modular reactors over large centralized facilities where they meet safety standards

**Carbon Capture and Storage:** Only considered as a complement to, not replacement for, emissions reduction. Criteria include:
- Net-negative outcomes verified by independent monitoring
- No use for enhanced oil recovery
- Community consent for storage sites
- Funding from fossil fuel industry, not diverted from renewable energy investment

**Solar Radiation Management (Geoengineering):** Subject to the highest level of governance scrutiny:
- Moratorium on deployment without global consensus
- Research permitted only under the Seven-Generation Impact Assessment framework
- Veto power held by the Global Youth Assembly and Indigenous governing bodies
- Binding prohibition on unilateral deployment by any nation or corporation

For all controversial technologies, the principle is the same: **when the risks are intergenerational, the consent must be intergenerational.**

## 4. Core Pillars of Transformation

### Pillar I: Radical Mitigation & Ecosystem Restoration

**Targets:** Net-zero emissions by 2050, with interim milestones (55% reduction by 2035).

**Strategies:**
- Carbon pricing that makes fossil fuels more expensive than renewables
- Nature-based solutions — restoring forests, wetlands, and mangroves that naturally sequester carbon
- Industrial decarbonization through circular economy practices (closed-loop manufacturing, waste reduction)

Funding and tracking for nature-based solutions flows through the AUBI Framework's Leaves currency, which rewards ecological restoration work.

### Pillar II: The Just & Sovereign Energy Transition

**Goal:** Shift to 90-100% clean energy globally by 2050 (aligned with IPCC pathways), with 80% community-owned renewable energy systems.

**How It Happens:**
- Phase out fossil fuel subsidies and redirect funds to renewable energy
- Modernize grids for decentralized, resilient energy systems
- Scale Community Energy Cooperatives (see Hearthstone Protocol below)
- Ensure just transition for fossil fuel workers (see Sundown Protocol below)
- Build sustainable supply chains for renewable technologies (ethical sourcing of critical minerals)
- Create circular energy systems (battery recycling, modular solar panel designs)

#### 4.2a The Sundown Protocol: Managing the Phase-Out

The Sundown Protocol is the operational framework for transitioning away from fossil fuel infrastructure. It provides:

**Pathfinder Compact:** Voluntary commitments from fossil fuel companies to begin transition planning, with incentives for early action — access to RTZ markets, reputational recognition.

**Legacy Transition Fund:** Capitalized by a Reparations Levy on fossil fuel companies and high-emission nations. Funds worker retraining, community economic diversification, and land reclamation.

**Decommissioning Roadmap:** Staged timeline for infrastructure closure, with milestones tied to workforce transition and community consent.

**Ceremonial Closure Templates:** Cultural protocols for marking the end of extraction — not as failure, but as collective healing. Co-designed with affected communities.

The Sundown Protocol is not punitive. It recognizes that workers and communities did not create this system alone. Its goal is to ensure that those who powered the extractive era are the first to benefit from the regenerative one.

#### 4.2b The Hearthstone Protocol: Building Community Energy Sovereignty

The Hearthstone Protocol is the operational framework for transitioning *to* community-owned renewable energy. It provides:

**Community Energy Cooperative Development:** Technical assistance, financing models (seed capital from the Global Commons Fund, matched by Hearts contributions), and governance templates for cooperative formation.

**Ownership Transition Pathways:** Mechanisms for transferring existing energy infrastructure to community ownership — through right of first refusal, worker cooperatives, or municipalization.

**Grid Interoperability Standards:** Technical protocols ensuring community-owned grids can connect, share power, and trade energy without surrendering sovereignty.

**The name** invokes the hearth — the ancient center of home, warmth, and gathering. The Hearthstone Protocol reclaims energy from abstraction (megawatts, commodities, financial instruments) and returns it to what it has always been: the capacity to keep each other warm, to power our lives, to build together.

### Pillar III: Traditional Knowledge-Led Adaptation & Resilience

**Focus:** Resilience for vulnerable populations and ecosystems, guided by Indigenous and local knowledge.

**Actions:**
- Infrastructure upgrades that protect communities from climate impacts
- Disaster preparedness coordinated through the Disaster Risk Reduction & Resilience Framework
- Nature-based solutions (mangrove restoration, watershed protection) aligned with biodiversity targets
- Climate finance for developing nations, with funds reaching communities directly

**Health-Resilient Adaptation:** All major energy and adaptation projects must include Health Co-Benefits Assessments. Results — reduced air pollution, improved community health outcomes — are tracked and contribute to a BAZ's score in the Love, Meaning, and Connection Index (LMCI), creating a clear incentive loop between climate action and public health.

### Pillar IV: Building the Regenerative Energy Commons

**The Narrative:** Actively promoting the vision of community-owned, resilient, abundant clean energy. This counters disinformation from entrenched fossil fuel interests and builds public demand for transition.

**The Lived Experience:** Community Energy Cooperatives do more than provide clean power. They create local wealth, strengthen community bonds (increasing the LMCI), and improve quality of life. People experience the transition as liberation, not sacrifice.

**The Economic Advantage:** The Regenerative Trade Zone creates powerful economic incentives for nations to join. As the zone grows, being outside it becomes increasingly costly — not through punishment, but through the loss of opportunity.

## 7. Implementation Roadmap

### Milestone 1: Governance Activation (Target: 18 months)

**Trigger:** Formal establishment of the Global Oversight Body and ratification of the framework by the initial Regenerative Trade Zone coalition (15+ nations and BAZs).

**Activities:**
- Recruit founding RTZ members
- Establish the International Climate Tribunal as a chamber of the Digital Justice Tribunal
- Launch the Legacy Transition Fund with initial capital

### Milestone 2: Economic Engine Online (Target: Year 3)

**Trigger:** Reparative Contributions mechanism is capitalized and making first disbursements. First Community Energy Cooperatives operational in pilot BAZs.

**Activities:**
- First round of funding for nature-based solutions via Leaves currency
- Community Energy Cooperative Development Guide published and in use
- Trade benefits for RTZ members begin to show measurable advantage

### Milestone 3: Systemic Shift (Target: Year 8)

**Trigger:** The Biosphere Health Index officially supplants GDP in public reporting within all RTZ member states. 50% of RTZ energy is community-owned.

**Activities:**
- Full integration of Hearts and Leaves currencies into RTZ economies
- Majority of fossil fuel infrastructure in RTZ members under active decommissioning
- Worker transition programs show measurable success

### Milestone 4: Global Tipping Point (Target: Year 15)

**Trigger:** RTZ encompasses over 60% of the global economy, making its trade and climate standards the de facto global norm. 90-100% clean energy achieved internally.

**Activities:**
- Remaining non-RTZ nations face significant trade disadvantages
- Global emissions on clear downward trajectory aligned with 1.5°C pathways
- Framework becomes foundation for post-2030 international climate architecture

## 8. How the Pull Effect Works: A Concrete Example

Consider a mid-sized industrial nation hesitant to join the RTZ. It is not ideologically opposed to climate action, but it fears economic disadvantage.

**Year 1:** Costa Rica — an RTZ member — begins exporting renewable energy components to other RTZ members under preferential trade terms. Its energy costs drop. Its manufacturers receive price advantages in RTZ markets.

**Year 2:** The hesitant nation's exporters begin to notice: they are losing market share in RTZ countries. Their goods face tariffs that RTZ members' goods do not. Their supply chains are not compliant with Digital Product Passport requirements, creating friction.

**Year 3:** A delegation of the hesitant nation's business leaders visits a former coal region in Germany that is now an RTZ member. They see reclaimed land, thriving cooperatives, workers who were retrained now training others. They ask: why not us?

**Year 4:** The nation applies for RTZ candidacy. The application process itself begins to transform its economy — industries start decarbonizing to meet RTZ standards, not because they are forced, but because they want access.

**The pull effect does not require enforcement.** It requires that the alternative become so obviously superior that staying out becomes the harder choice.

## 9. What Success Feels Like

Metrics tell us whether we are meeting targets. But success is also felt. This framework envisions a world where:

- **Energy bills are replaced by energy dividends.** Households receive payments from community-owned generation, not invoices from distant corporations.

- **Fossil fuel infrastructure is not a site of protest but of memory.** Former coal plants host community gardens. Pipelines have been removed, their corridors restored to wildlife habitat.

- **Workers who built the extractive economy lead the regenerative one.** They are not casualties of transition but its architects, their skills retooled, their communities thriving.

- **The air is clean.** Not as a policy target, but as a felt reality. Children no longer learn to associate their city with smog.

- **Energy sovereignty is not a slogan but a fact.** Communities that were once energy colonies — their resources extracted for distant benefit — now control their own power. They decide when to generate, when to share, when to sell.

- **The transition is not experienced as sacrifice.** It is experienced as liberation — from volatile prices, from extractive relationships, from the constant low-grade anxiety of a planet in crisis.

These are not utopian fantasies. They are already true in places. The work of this framework is to make them true everywhere.

## 12. Integration with the Global Governance Framework Ecosystem

### 12.1 Implementation Sequencing

Implementation follows the 15-year, three-tier roadmap outlined in the Meta-Governance Framework. Initial adoption is driven by a voluntary coalition of nations and BAZs forming a Regenerative Trade Zone under the Gaian Trade Framework. Binding enforcement mechanisms phase in as the Treaty for Our Only Home is ratified.

### 12.2 Technology & Data Governance

Technology deployed under this framework (smart grids, energy trading platforms) adheres to the ethical oversight of the Technology Governance Implementation Framework (TGIF). Energy data is governed by the Aurora Accord, ensuring community data sovereignty through community-controlled servers and Indigenous data protocols.

**Net-Zero AI Standards:** The Global Technology Council establishes standards requiring that all large-scale AI operations (data centers, model training) be powered by verifiable, additional renewable energy sources. Their energy and resource consumption data is integrated into the Biosphere Health Index for holistic accounting.

### 12.3 Crisis Response

Climate emergencies trigger the Crisis Command Protocol of the Meta-Governance Framework. Democratic accountability is maintained through time-limited emergency powers (90 days) and oversight from the Planetary Health Council.

### 12.4 Local-to-Global Coordination

Local energy cooperatives form Community Energy Federations operating as Community Work Teams under the Work in Liberation Framework. Federations send delegates to the Fractal Labor Parliament and to the Global Oversight Body, ensuring direct linkage from local practice to global policy.

### 12.5 Operationalizing Just Transition via the Sundown Protocol

While this framework sets overarching targets, the Sundown Protocol provides the complete, phased implementation pathway — from the Pathfinder Compact for voluntary actors to the mandatory decommissioning roadmap and Ceremonial Closure Templates for cultural healing.
