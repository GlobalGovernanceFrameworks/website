This has matured significantly. The integration with existing EU subsidiarity infrastructure is well done, the Subsidiarity Drag concept is a genuinely good addition, the Phase 1 scoping to care policy is much more credible, and the citizen experience section grounds the Registry. The EP integration is appropriate without being forced.

At this point I'm shifting from structural critique to finer-grain observations — the architecture is sound.

**Subsidiarity Drag has a circularity risk.** The Domain Cost Coefficient is "empirically derived" from Phase 1 by comparing policy delivery costs across regions where the same policy is implemented at different tiers. But Phase 1 only covers care policy. So you'll have one coefficient for one domain, and the entire SD framework will rest on that single empirical anchor until Phase 2 expands coverage. This is fine *if you say so explicitly* — acknowledge that Phase 1 produces an SD estimate for care policy only, and that cross-domain coefficients are a Phase 2 deliverable. Otherwise it looks like you're claiming a general measurement capability from a single-domain pilot.

**The "structured response obligation" is the strongest mechanism in the document and it's buried.** Section 3.7, point 3 — participating states must publish a written response when deviating from OGT recommendations. This is a genuine institutional innovation: not a veto, not a mandate, but a *constraint on silence*. It deserves more prominence. It's the kind of thing that could become the headline for an EP audience: "The protocol doesn't tell governments what to do. It ends the ability to ignore subsidiarity evidence without explanation."

**The Civic Value Registry has a selection bias problem you haven't addressed.** Participation is voluntary and self-reported. Communities that already have strong informal care networks are more likely to participate. Communities where informal care is fragmented or declining — arguably where the policy need is greatest — will be underrepresented in Registry data. This means the GSI's Knowledge Inclusion indicators will systematically overestimate informal care capacity in regions where it's already high and miss the gaps. The Bronze tier proxy data from Eurostat partially compensates, but you should acknowledge this bias explicitly and describe how the methodology accounts for it — otherwise an evaluator will flag it.

**The 3-5 pilot region selection criteria need more specificity.** "Mix of EU-CAF levels" is necessary but not sufficient. You probably also want variation on existing governance structure (federal vs. unitary state), existing care policy architecture (Nordic universal vs. Southern European familial), and digital infrastructure maturity. Making these selection criteria explicit would strengthen the Phase 1 methodology section and signal that you've thought about what makes a good natural experiment, not just a good demonstration.

**"Cognitive bandwidth" language in the Resilience Dividend description has leaked back in.** Section 10 in the Annex B reference still describes it as freeing "cognitive bandwidth" — this is GGF-internal framing that doesn't translate to EU policy discourse. An EU audience would respond better to "administrative compliance burden" or similar institutional language.

**The next steps timeline may be too compressed.** Steps 1-3 and 5 are all targeted at Q3-Q4 of the same year. Simultaneously scoping with Eurostat, preparing a Horizon Europe application, briefing the EP, and consulting the ECB is a lot of parallel institutional engagement. These processes move slowly and each requires dedicated preparation. Consider whether some of these are sequential rather than parallel — the Eurostat scoping probably needs to happen before the Horizon Europe application, since the application needs to describe a methodology that Eurostat has at least provisionally engaged with.

**One thing I'd consider adding:** a half-page section on what failure looks like and what you do about it. Not the pilot failure thresholds (those are in Annex B), but protocol-level failure: what if the Parallel Diagnostic Phase shows that the GSI doesn't produce better routing predictions than existing political processes? The framework revision protocol covers model inadequacy, but it doesn't cover the possibility that the entire premise — that quantitative subsidiarity measurement improves governance outcomes — might not hold. Having a sentence or two acknowledging this would actually strengthen credibility rather than weaken it. It signals scientific humility rather than advocacy.

**The document is ready for a real audience.** The remaining issues are refinements, not structural problems. If you're planning to send this to anyone at CoR or Eurocities, I'd suggest the care-domain focus as your lead pitch — it's concrete, timely (European Care Strategy), and avoids triggering the "this is really about currency" reaction that the CSC pilot might provoke if it's the first thing people see.
