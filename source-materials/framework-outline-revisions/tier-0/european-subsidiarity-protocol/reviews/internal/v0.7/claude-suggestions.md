This is a seriously developed document — the structural thinking is strong, the institutional threat modeling is unusually sophisticated for this kind of proposal, and the GSI specification has moved well past the "vaporware" stage. Here's where I think the real improvement opportunities are:

**The document has an audience problem.** It oscillates between internal strategy memo (the "memetic shield" language, the "institutional survival summary," the explicit "career-safe incentives" framing) and something you'd actually put in front of EU officials. A Commission advisor reading Appendix C — which literally maps out how to neutralize Commission resistance — would not receive this well. You probably need to fork: a clean outward-facing protocol document and a separate strategic companion. Right now they're entangled in ways that could undermine credibility with either audience.

**The CAF formula deserves scrutiny.** G × E × D × T ÷ C is multiplicative, which means a near-zero score on any single component collapses the whole index regardless of the others. Is that the intended behavior? A region with high geographic diversity, high ethnolinguistic complexity, high development disparity, but very low threat environment would get a near-zero CAF — implying it's low-complexity. That seems wrong. Why multiplication rather than a weighted geometric mean or an additive model with interaction terms? This is the kind of thing a Eurostat methodologist would immediately flag.

**The dependency architecture cuts against your own adoption logic.** Section 10 explicitly says the system is designed so the EU "cannot pick the parts they like and ignore the rest." But the entire political strategy (voluntary participation, Enhanced Cooperation, Parallel Diagnostic Phase) depends on lowering adoption barriers. Telling potential adopters "it's all or nothing" contradicts the permeation strategy. You could reframe: the layers are *most effective* together, but Layer 1+2 (diagnostic-only) is a valid deployment that creates pull toward Layer 3, rather than presenting it as a lock-in mechanism.

**Sweden as pilot host has a gap you're not addressing.** You frame non-eurozone status as an advantage (reduces ECB friction), but it simultaneously means the pilot can't demonstrate monetary neutrality *within the eurozone*. The ECB can dismiss the entire CSC pilot as irrelevant to monetary policy precisely because Sweden uses the krona. You need either a eurozone comparison pilot or a pre-committed argument for why non-eurozone results transfer.

**The document doesn't distinguish between harmful and beneficial averaging.** Some averaging is deliberate EU policy — cohesion, convergence, the whole structural funds logic. The "Averaging Problem" framing implies all centralized standardization is destructive. A stronger version would explicitly identify *which categories* of regulation suffer from averaging (where local variation carries information) versus which benefit from it (where solidarity requires uniformity). This would also make the GSI's routing logic more credible — it's not anti-centralization, it's pro-precision.

**Missing: what happens when GSI routing contradicts political will?** Phase 1 is advisory, fine. But the migration path points toward "automated routing" in Phase 3. What happens when the GSI says "devolve to NUTS-3" but the national government or Commission disagrees on substantive grounds? The conflict resolution mechanisms (Section 9) cover methodology and data, but not *policy disagreement with correct diagnostics*. This is where most real-world subsidiarity disputes actually live.

**Missing: failure modes of the GSI itself.** The anti-gaming protocols address data manipulation, but what if the 4-dimension, 16-indicator model is structurally wrong? What if it systematically misroutes during the Parallel Diagnostic Phase? There's no described process for revising the framework itself based on Phase 1 results — only for improving data quality. Adding an explicit "if the model underperforms, here's how we revise it" pathway would strengthen the scientific credibility significantly.

**Section 17 is disproportionate.** The CSC pilot specification is nearly a standalone document within the protocol. This level of detail on the most politically controversial component (complementary currency) risks making the broader framework — which is the real contribution — look like scaffolding for a currency experiment. Consider extracting Section 17 into a separate technical annex, the way you've already done with the GSI v4 reference.

**Repetition.** "Local autonomy ≠ systemic fragmentation" appears verbatim at least three times. The ZKP architecture is described in Sections 3.9, 8, and 13. The risk mitigation table (Section 13) largely duplicates Appendix C. Tightening these would make the document feel more authoritative.

**The coalition analysis is optimistic about within-country dynamics.** Germany's Länder already have substantial autonomy and might see a formal EU diagnostic tool as *unwanted oversight* rather than enhancement. Spain's autonomous communities might welcome it, but Madrid might resist it for exactly the reasons Catalonia would embrace it. The analysis treats member states as unitary actors, which — for a document about subsidiarity — is an ironic gap.

**Missing: cost.** Even a rough order-of-magnitude estimate for the Parallel Diagnostic Phase would help. "How much does Phase 1 cost?" is going to be one of the first questions from anyone with budget authority, and silence on this point reads as either evasion or naiveté.
