# DPI 2.0: The Subsidiarity Layer
### A Governance Routing Protocol for India Stack

**Version:** 0.2 (India Adaptation — Refined Specification)
**Target:** EkStep Foundation, iSPIRT Foundation (secondary), NITI Aayog (technical working group)
**Architectural Status:** Open Standard — DPI-Compatible

## Executive Summary

India has built the most successful Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) in the world:

- **Aadhaar:** Identity for 1.4 billion people
- **UPI:** Real-time payments at planetary scale (over 10 billion monthly transactions)
- **DEPA:** User-owned data empowerment
- **ONDC:** Open network for digital commerce

**This is the digital plumbing.**

What India lacks is the **governance routing layer**—the protocol that determines *where* decisions should be made, *how* resources should flow, and *which* scale (local, state, or national) is optimal for any given problem.

The result: a hyper-efficient digital infrastructure serving a **centralized governance architecture** that cannot match the variety of 1.4 billion people across 28 states.

**DPI 2.0** adds the missing layer. It is not a political argument for decentralization. It is a **technical solution to measurable governance problems**:

- **Disaster response:** Reducing latency from days to hours
- **Welfare delivery:** Improving targeting precision
- **Informal sector visibility:** Measuring economic activity that GDP misses

**What the center gains:** Faster, more visible outcomes. Better metrics. Strategic visibility into the informal economy. Electoral accountability for results, not process.

This is not a replacement for India Stack. It is the **governance operating system** that runs on top of it.

## 1. The Structural Problem: Latency at Scale

India possesses extraordinary digital infrastructure but faces a governance architecture that cannot fully utilize it. The problem is not digital. The problem is **governance latency**—the time between problem emergence and resource arrival.

### 1.1 The Disaster Response Problem (Phase 1 Domain)

**The problem is measurable:**
- 2018 Kerala floods: 483 deaths, ₹31,000 crore in damages. Central relief reached many districts only after 72 hours.
- 2023 Himachal Pradesh floods: 500+ deaths. State-level resources overwhelmed; central coordination delayed by bureaucratic routing.

**Why this happens:** Disaster response requires three layers of coordination:
- **National:** Budget allocation, military deployment, satellite imagery
- **State:** Infrastructure coordination, public health response
- **District/Block:** Local knowledge of vulnerable populations, informal networks, community capacity

**The current system routes everything through the center first.** The National Disaster Response Force (NDRF) is world-class—but it cannot be everywhere simultaneously. Local knowledge of vulnerable populations (the elderly, the disabled, informal workers) exists at the district level but is inaccessible to formal systems.

**DPI 2.0 solves this:** The Global Subsidiarity Index (GSI) identifies optimal routing for disaster response—some resources nationally coordinated, some state-managed, some locally directed. The result is not decentralization. It is **faster, more precise allocation**.

**What the center gains:** Measurable reduction in disaster mortality. Visible outcomes. Electoral credit for lives saved.

### 1.2 The Welfare Delivery Problem (Phase 2 Domain)

India's welfare system—PM-KISAN, NFSA, MGNREGA—delivers benefits to hundreds of millions. But targeting remains imperfect.

**The problem:** Central allocation formulas average across states and districts. A district with high informal employment receives the same per-capita allocation as a district with high formal employment, despite radically different needs.

**DPI 2.0 solves this:** The Civic Value Registry measures informal economic capacity—the 85% of employment invisible to GDP and tax data. This allows welfare allocation to match actual need, not administrative averages.

**What the center gains:** Better targeting. Reduced leakage. Improved metrics for program success.

### 1.3 The Agricultural Distress Problem (Phase 3 Domain)

A region in Maharashtra faces crop failure. The optimal intervention depends on local soil conditions, market integration, and informal credit networks.

**The problem:** Centralized agricultural policies average across vastly different agro-ecological zones. The farmer in Vidarbha receives the same intervention as the farmer in Punjab, despite radically different needs.

**DPI 2.0 solves this:** The GSI identifies that Maharashtra requires state-level debt restructuring, but local-level input distribution. Resources route accordingly.

**What the center gains:** Reduced farmer distress. Better agricultural productivity. Political stability.

## 2. India Stack: The Foundation

DPI 2.0 is built *on top of* India Stack, not parallel to it.

| India Stack Layer | DPI 2.0 Integration |
|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Aadhaar (Identity)** | Edge nodes authenticated via Aadhaar; privacy preserved through zero-knowledge proofs |
| **UPI (Payments)** | Value transfer remains in UPI; DPI 2.0 adds reputational layer without processing transactions |
| **DEPA (Data)** | User-owned data principles extended to reputation; users control disclosure of trust proofs |
| **ONDC (Commerce)** | Reputation portability across open network; trust builds across platforms |
| **DigiLocker** | Storage for zero-knowledge reputation proofs; user-controlled access |
| **Diksha (Education)** | Potential integration for skills verification in informal sector |

**Explicit boundary:** DPI 2.0 is a **Layer 2 Reputation Oracle**. It does not:

- Process financial transactions (remains in UPI)
- Store personal data centrally (remains user-owned via DEPA)
- Replace Aadhaar (authenticates but does not create parallel identity)
- Create new currency (strictly reputational)

## 3. Protocol Architecture: The Subsidiarity Layer

DPI 2.0 adds three capabilities to India Stack:

### 3.1. The Global Subsidiarity Index (GSI)

The GSI is a diagnostic algorithm that identifies the optimal governance scale for any given policy domain.

**Inputs (India-Specific Data Sources):**

| Data Type | Source | Purpose |
|-----------|--------|---------|
| **Administrative boundaries** | Census of India, Ministry of Panchayati Raj | Governance units |
| **Infrastructure** | Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MOSPI) | Capacity assessment |
| **Health outcomes** | National Family Health Survey (NFHS) | Needs identification |
| **Agricultural data** | Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare | Sector-specific diagnosis |
| **Informal economy** | NSSO Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), ILO estimates | Capacity measurement (Civic Value Registry) |
| **Disaster vulnerability** | National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) | Risk mapping |

**Output:**
- "This problem requires national coordination" (e.g., monetary policy, defense, international disaster relief)
- "This problem requires state-level autonomy" (e.g., agricultural extension, health infrastructure)
- "This problem requires local adaptation" (e.g., community resilience, informal sector support)

**Technical foundation:** Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety—the governance system must match the variety of the system it governs. The GSI mathematically identifies where centralized regulation destroys local variety and where local autonomy cannot achieve coordination benefits.

**Integration with NITI Aayog:** The GSI provides technical inputs to NITI Aayog's policy formulation. It does not replace political decision-making—it adds a diagnostic layer that makes the costs of overcentralization visible.

### 3.2. The Civic Value Registry: Informal Economy Visibility

The Indian informal economy employs approximately **85% of the workforce**—a figure derived from ILO estimates (2023) and NSSO PLFS data (2021-22) that consistently show informal employment between 80-90% depending on definition (agricultural labor, street vendors, domestic workers, construction laborers, gig workers). These actors are invisible to formal systems, yet they constitute the majority of economic activity.

**Why informal workers would participate voluntarily:**

| Incentive | Mechanism |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Credit access** | Reputation score enables micro-loans from formal lenders without collateral |
| **Disaster relief** | Verified reputation fast-tracks relief distribution during crises |
| **Market access** | ONDC participation requires trust; reputation provides it |
| **Skills verification** | Diksha integration for training credentials |
| **No surveillance** | Zero-knowledge proofs ensure raw data never leaves user control |

**What the registry measures:**
- Trust networks (who trades with whom, frequency, reliability)
- Community care capacity (informal eldercare, childcare networks)
- Local adaptive resilience (how communities respond to shocks)
- Informal credit relationships (chamas, VSLAs, rotating savings groups)

**How it works:**
1. **Voluntary registration:** Feature-phone via USSD; smartphone via app; Aadhaar authentication (opt-in)
2. **Transaction recording:** P2P attestations; cryptographically hashed; stored locally
3. **Reputation accumulation:** Algorithm aggregates trust signals; user-owned
4. **Selective disclosure:** User generates ZK proof for specific use cases (e.g., "I have Level 4 reliability for disaster relief eligibility")

**Integration with DEPA:** Users control their reputation data under DEPA principles—they decide what to prove, to whom, and when.

**Integration with UPI:** The registry attaches to UPI transactions as a reputational layer, without processing value. A vendor using UPI for payments can build a verifiable reputation score that travels with them across the economy.

### 3.3. The Routing Protocol

The Routing Protocol translates GSI diagnosis into resource flows:

| Layer | Function | Disaster Response Example |
|-------|----------|---------------------------|
| **National** | Budget allocation, military deployment, satellite imagery | NDRF pre-positioning; central funds released |
| **State** | Infrastructure coordination, public health response | State disaster management authority activates |
| **District/Block** | Local knowledge, vulnerable population mapping | District magistrate directs resources using GSI-identified priorities |
| **Community** | Trust-based coordination | Self-help groups activate informal networks; verified reputation scores fast-track relief |

**Dynamic routing:** As conditions change (e.g., floodwaters rise, new districts affected), the GSI re-diagnoses and the Routing Protocol adjusts—resources flow to the scale that can act most effectively.

## 4. Deployment Model: Single-Domain First

Rather than spanning climate, agriculture, and inequality simultaneously, DPI 2.0 Phase 1 focuses on a single domain where:

- **The problem is measurable** (disaster response latency can be quantified)
- **The incentive alignment is clear** (center gets faster, visible outcomes)
- **The political sensitivity is lowest** (disaster response is non-partisan)
- **The data infrastructure exists** (NDMA, IMD, state disaster authorities)

### Phase 1: Disaster Response (12–18 months)

**Pilot State Selection:**

| State | Rationale |
|-------|-----------|
| **Odisha** | High disaster vulnerability (cyclones); strong administrative capacity; ruling party aligned with center (BJD, neutral/non-opposition); proven disaster management infrastructure |
| **Gujarat** | High disaster vulnerability (earthquakes, cyclones); strong administrative capacity; ruling party aligned with center (BJP); existing digital infrastructure |

**Why not Tamil Nadu or Kerala initially:** Opposition-governed states would make the protocol appear partisan. The first pilot must demonstrate the tool works for the center's own priorities. BJD (Odisha) is non-aligned; Gujarat is aligned. Both provide administrative credibility.

**Triangular Activation:**

| Leg | Role | Partner |
|-----|------|---------|
| **Foundation Anchor** | Technical architecture, implementation | EkStep Foundation (primary), iSPIRT Foundation (advisory) |
| **Government Anchor** | Policy authority, data access | NITI Aayog, NDMA, State Disaster Management Authorities |
| **Civic-Tech Builder** | USSD/smartphone deployment, local capacity | Local civic-tech ecosystem; potential engagement with Wadhwani AI |

**Success Metrics:**

| Metric | Baseline | Target |
|--------|----------|--------|
| **Disaster response latency** | 72+ hours (current) | <24 hours for GSI-routed resources |
| **Vulnerable population coverage** | Unknown (no baseline) | 80% of identified vulnerable populations reached |
| **Informal worker registry** | 0 (formal systems) | 100,000 voluntarily registered |
| **NDMA integration** | None | GSI outputs used in NDMA operational planning |

### Phase 2: Welfare Delivery (Years 2–3)

- GSI applied to PM-KISAN, NFSA, MGNREGA targeting
- Pilot states expanded to include Tamil Nadu (DMK) and Uttar Pradesh (BJP) to demonstrate bipartisan utility
- Civic Value Registry integrated into welfare eligibility algorithms

### Phase 3: Agricultural Policy (Years 4–5)

- GSI integrated into Ministry of Agriculture planning
- State-level agricultural extension redesigned based on GSI diagnosis
- ONDC integration for market access based on reputation scores

| Component | Specification |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Primary access** | Smartphone app (Android/iOS) with USSD fallback (India's smartphone penetration ~75%, USSD for remainder) |

## 6. Privacy & Data Sovereignty

DPI 2.0 inherits India Stack's data sovereignty principles and aligns with the *Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India* (2017) privacy ruling:

| Principle | Implementation |
|-----------|----------------|
| **User ownership** | Reputation data owned by the individual; controlled via DEPA |
| **Zero-knowledge proofs** | Users prove "I have Level 4 reliability" without revealing transaction history |
| **No central repository** | Raw data remains at the edge; central system holds only anonymized aggregates |
| **Voluntary participation** | No mandatory registration; formal actors can ignore protocol |
| **Consent architecture** | DEPA-based consent; users approve each data disclosure |

**Explicit Safeguard:** The Civic Value Registry cannot be used for taxation enforcement. This is a non-negotiable architectural constraint.

## 7. Risk Mitigation

| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|------------|
| **Political perception as decentralization challenge** | Frame as disaster response latency solution; center gets measurable outcomes; political neutrality through pilot state selection (Odisha first) |
| **Informal worker distrust** | Concrete incentives (credit access, disaster relief); ZK proofs; no mandatory registration; DEPA consent; explicit taxation enforcement prohibition |
| **State resistance** | Voluntary participation; single-domain first (disaster response); demonstrate utility before expansion |
| **Privacy violation** | ZK proofs; user-controlled disclosure; no central repository; constitutional alignment |
| **Regulatory capture** | Distributed governance; civil society observers; parliamentary oversight |
| **Scale failure** | Phased deployment; single-domain first; continuous evaluation; algorithmic triggers |

## 10. Conclusion: From Plumbing to Intelligence

India has built the digital pipes. Now it needs the **governance intelligence** to route resources through those pipes to the places they are most needed.

DPI 2.0 is not a political argument for decentralization. It is a **technical solution to measurable governance problems**:

- Disaster response latency kills people. DPI 2.0 reduces it.
- Welfare targeting wastes resources. DPI 2.0 improves it.
- Agricultural distress destroys livelihoods. DPI 2.0 mitigates it.

**What the center gains:**
- Measurable outcomes (lives saved, resources optimized)
- Strategic visibility (informal economy data that doesn't exist today)
- Electoral accountability (visible results, not process)

This is not a foreign import. It is the next logical step in India's digital journey—built on Indian infrastructure, anchored in Indian constitutional principles, designed for Indian realities.

**The plumbing is ready. The intelligence is ready. It is time to route.**
