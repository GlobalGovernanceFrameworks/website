## 1. Executive Summary

The Cairn Protocol is a governance framework for addiction, recovery, and substance sovereignty. It offers an alternative to both carceral approaches (which criminalize health issues) and purely clinical models (which pathologize individuals while ignoring systemic causes).

### Core Commitments

| Commitment | Meaning |
|------------|---------|
| **Unconditional Survival Resources** | Food, shelter, safety, and biological stabilization are provided without any requirement of sobriety or participation |
| **Community-Led Healing** | Those with lived experience are the primary healers, not ancillary support |
| **Harm Reduction as Foundation** | Meet people where they are with evidence-based tools, including safe supply |
| **Substance Sovereignty** | Individuals and communities define their own relationship with substances |
| **Systemic Accountability** | Corporate profiteers and geopolitical drivers are addressed, not just individual users |

### How It Works

**Recovery Weavers** — trained community members with lived experience — provide outreach, crisis support, and connection to **Recovery Hubs**. Hubs offer unconditional services (food, shelter, biological stabilization, harm reduction supplies) alongside optional peer-led healing spaces (Somatic Sanctuaries, Healing Circles, Joy Labs).

The framework is designed for staged implementation, with offline-first infrastructure and explicit protections against co-optation by state, corporate, or illicit market actors.

### Document Structure

This is **Document 1: Core Governance Framework**, intended for policymakers, funders, implementing organizations, and BAZ governance bodies. A separate **Resistance Companion** contains supplementary materials for grassroots activists working in hostile contexts.

## 2. Philosophical Foundation

### 2.1 Survival vs. Evolution

This framework begins with a recognition: people surviving addiction in contexts of war, poverty, and systemic abandonment are not "failing to develop" or trapped in a "lower stage of consciousness." They are performing precisely what human nervous systems evolved to do: survive unbearable conditions.

Substance use in such contexts is often a rational response to unlivable environments. Healing requires changing those conditions, not just the individual.

The framework therefore rejects developmental hierarchies that position the observer as "higher" and the suffering as "lower." It begins instead from a single premise: **people deserve safety, connection, and the freedom to define their own path home — not because they have earned it through development, but because they are human.**

### 2.2 Declaration of Substance Sovereignty

This charter asserts:

- The right to bodily autonomy and informed consent in substance use and recovery
- The freedom to access sacred plant medicines under Indigenous governance
- Protection from predatory, addictive-by-design products and technologies
- The right to survive — including through safe supply and harm reduction — without coercion or institutionalization

This declaration is invocable in Digital Justice Tribunal cases and serves as a litmus test for all Cairn Protocol policies.

## 3. Core Principles

### 3.1 Five Foundational Principles

| Principle | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **1. Unconditional Survival Resources** | Food, shelter, safety, and basic medical care are provided without any requirement of sobriety, participation, or progress |
| **2. Addiction as Adaptation** | In contexts of war, poverty, and abandonment, substance use is often a rational response to unbearable conditions. Healing requires changing those conditions |
| **3. Community-Led Recovery** | Healing thrives in peer support, mutual aid, and connection. Those with lived experience are the primary healers |
| **4. Harm Reduction as Foundation** | Meet people where they are with evidence-based tools. Coercion does not work; safety does |
| **5. Systemic Accountability** | Recovery requires addressing root causes: trauma, economic insecurity, corporate profiteering, and geopolitical dynamics |

### 3.2 Operational Ethics

| Ethical Standard | Application |
|------------------|-------------|
| **Health-Centered, Not Punitive** | Addiction is a health and social issue, never criminal. Carceral responses are prohibited |
| **Anti-Institutional Architecture** | Recovery spaces must not replicate institutional trauma. They function as low-barrier "Third Spaces" — cafes, gardens, kitchens — where people exist without surveillance |
| **Geopolitical Honesty** | The global addiction crisis is a downstream effect of war, colonial extraction, and economic exploitation. The framework names these forces |
| **Local Adaptation** | Communities have the right to say "no" to implementation that does not serve them |
| **Non-Digital First** | All interventions must function without digital infrastructure. Digital tools are enhancements, not prerequisites |

## 4. Governance Architecture

### 4.1 The Cairn Council

A sub-council of the Planetary Health Council, overseeing global addiction and recovery strategy.

**Composition:**
- 60% members with lived experience (personal history of addiction, family member, or street-connected experience)
- Reserved seats for Indigenous healers (appointed by the Cultural & Ancestral Wisdom Council)
- Reserved seats for youth under 25 with current or former street or addiction experience
- Geographic representation: minimum 30% from Global South, with reserved seats for high-impact regions (Afghanistan, East Africa, Latin America)

**Term Limits:**
- 2-year terms, rotating to prevent entrenched bureaucracy

**Decision-Making:**
- **Substance Sovereignty Litmus Test:** All policies must pass: *"Does this intervention increase or decrease an individual's/community's capacity to define their own relationship with substances?"* Failure triggers a veto requiring 2/3 majority of lived-experience members to override
- Major policy changes require supermajority consensus from lived-experience members

### 4.2 Recovery Hubs

Decentralized, community-run centers operating under **Recovery Commons Charters** adapted to local context.

**Core Services (Unconditional):**
- Food and nutrition
- Low-barrier shelter (non-institutional, no registration requirements)
- Biological stabilization (see Section 5.2)
- Harm reduction supplies (needles, naloxone, safe inhalation equipment)

**Peer-Led Services (Optional, No Requirement):**
- Somatic Sanctuaries (trauma-release practices: breathwork, movement, yoga)
- Healing Circles (grief rituals, storytelling)
- Neighborhood Apothecaries (community gardens for plant allies)

**Flourishing Spaces (Optional, When Ready):**
- Joy Labs (dopamine-rich activities: music, art, sports)
- Recovery Capital Building (financial literacy, microenterprise)
- Eco-Therapy Programs (wilderness retreats, gardening)

**Design Principles:**
- Hubs must not resemble hospitals, clinics, or government buildings
- No surveillance architecture (cameras, check-in requirements)
- Open access during operating hours; no appointment needed
- Staff wearing casual clothing, no uniforms

### 4.3 Illicit Market Non-Interference Protocol

Recovery Hubs operate in contexts where illicit markets control territory and supply. The framework does not assume hubs can or should challenge these actors directly.

**Security Principles:**
- **Neutral Zone Negotiation:** Hubs seek agreement with local market actors to be recognized as non-commercial, non-threatening spaces
- **Community-Led De-escalation:** Local elders, neighborhood councils, or Indigenous governance bodies mediate hub recognition
- **No Direct Confrontation:** Hubs do not report market activity to authorities, creating a conflict of interest that endangers participants and staff
- **Red Line:** Hubs must not become sites of market violence, distribution, or recruitment. When this line is crossed, the hub closes or relocates

**Implementation Note:** This protocol is context-dependent. In some BAZs, hubs may operate with full autonomy. In others, survival requires negotiation. The principle is harm reduction applied to the security environment itself.

### 4.4 Recovery Weaver Integrity Protocol

The Recovery Weaver role is protected against co-optation by state, corporate, or illicit market actors.

| Protection | Mechanism |
|------------|-----------|
| **Lived Experience Requirement** | Minimum 2 years active in local addiction community, verified by peer attestation |
| **Peer Election** | Weavers selected by the community they serve, not by external authorities |
| **No Carceral Function** | Weavers do not report to police, courts, or rehabilitation camps. Carceral referral is grounds for immediate removal |
| **Economic Independence** | Weavers compensated through Hearts or fiat bridge (see Section 7), not government or corporate salaries |
| **Right to Refuse** | Weavers may refuse participation in any activity they deem coercive without penalty |
| **Transparency** | All Weaver funding sources publicly disclosed; funding from law enforcement or punitive institutions prohibited |

### 4.5 Conditions for Ethical Implementation

The Cairn Protocol cannot be ethically implemented in all contexts. Explicit conditions include:

**Required:**
- Meaningful consent from the affected community (not just government or NGO approval)
- Non-carceral guarantee: no use of the framework to justify forced rehabilitation or institutionalization

**In Authoritarian Contexts:**
- Implementation requires underground, diaspora-supported structures
- Explicit protection mechanisms for Weavers
- No public association with the Protocol that would endanger participants
- Success metrics recalibrated to survival, not flourishing

**Right to Refuse:**
- Communities have the right to say "no" to implementation
- The Protocol does not claim universality; it offers principles to be adapted

## 5. Core Interventions

### 5.1 Recovery Weavers

**Role:** Trained community members with lived experience providing outreach, crisis support, and connection to Hubs.

**Certification Levels:**
- **Basic:** Crisis response, harm reduction distribution, referral
- **Advanced:** Healing circle facilitation, grief ritual leadership, family support
- **Master:** Hub coordination, Weaver training, cross-BAZ mentorship

**Training Requirements:**
- Trauma-informed care
- Mental health first aid
- Crisis de-escalation
- Local cultural and religious competencies (e.g., Islamic frameworks in Afghanistan)

**Economic Model:**
- Hearts stipends or fiat bridge payments
- Explicit job creation target: minimum 60% of Hub staff from local addiction community
- Leadership pathways to Hub coordinator and Cairn Council roles

### 5.2 Biological Stabilization & Harm Reduction

**Biological stabilization** is the prerequisite for all other interventions. Individuals cannot engage in healing work when experiencing withdrawal, malnutrition, or acute toxicity.

**Core Components:**

| Intervention | Description |
|--------------|-------------|
| **Safe Supply** | Clean, regulated alternatives to street drugs. Prevents overdose, poisoning, and disease transmission |
| **Transitional Substances** | Methadone, buprenorphine, or culturally appropriate alternatives to manage withdrawal |
| **Medical Triage** | Wound care, infection treatment, nutritional support |
| **Overdose Prevention** | Naloxone distribution, supervised consumption, peer response training |

**Supply Chain:** See Section 6.3 (Compassion Club Procurement) for sourcing mechanisms.

**Relationship to Recovery:** Safe supply is not "enabling" — it is stabilizing. People with stable biology and predictable access are more likely to engage with healing services, but engagement is never required.

### 5.3 Community Healing

**Grief Feedback Pathway:**
- Community-led rituals, storytelling, and memorials within Recovery Hubs
- Includes space for **political grief** — mourning collapsed futures and stolen possibility, not just lost lives

**Healing Circles:**
- Peer-led, trauma-informed group processing
- Facilitated by Advanced Recovery Weavers
- Optional, no attendance tracking

**Cultural Healing Modalities:**
- Living database of traditional practices, stewarded by the Cultural & Ancestral Wisdom Council
- **Islamic Framework Integration:** Collaboration with imams and mosques; integration of *tawba* (return), *umma* (community), and *sabr* (patience) concepts
- Indigenous governance of sacred plant medicines

### 5.4 Optional Flourishing Spaces

These spaces are available to individuals when they are ready. No participation requirement for core services.

| Space | Purpose |
|-------|---------|
| **Joy Labs** | Dopamine-rich activities: music, art, sports, karaoke, communal meals |
| **Somatic Sanctuaries** | Trauma-release practices: breathwork, yoga, ecstatic movement |
| **Neighborhood Apothecaries** | Community gardens for non-addictive plant allies (chamomile, tulsi, local medicinal plants) |
| **Recovery Capital Building** | Financial literacy, microenterprise development, job training with employment pathways |
| **Eco-Therapy** | Wilderness retreats, gardening, nature connection — co-designed with Earth Council |

## 6. Implementation Pathways

### 6.1 Staged Implementation Model

| Phase | Scope | Activities |
|-------|-------|------------|
| **Phase 1: Foundation (Years 1-2)** | 5-7 pilot Hubs in communities with existing organizing | Establish Cairn Council; train first Recovery Weavers; pilot unconditional services; develop Compassion Club sourcing; establish Fiat Bridge |
| **Phase 2: Scaling (Years 3-5)** | Expansion to opt-in BAZs | Integrate referral pathways with hospitals; establish cross-BAZ mentorship; scale Hearts system; implement offline-first infrastructure |
| **Phase 3: Maturation (Years 6+)** | Global standard adoption | Global Recovery Weavers Network; continuous adaptation; Edge Labs for innovation |

### 6.2 Jurisdictional Analysis

The Protocol applies differently across governance contexts:

| Context | Implementation Model |
|---------|---------------------|
| **BAZs with functional governance** | Full implementation with Recovery Hubs, Hearts system, corporate accountability mechanisms |
| **Nation-state with harm reduction policies** | Hybrid: Hubs operate within legal frameworks; Compassion Clubs may require medical gatekeeper relationships |
| **Nation-state with prohibition** | Underground: diaspora-supported Hubs, no public association, protection protocols |
| **Active conflict zone** | Survival-only: unconditional resources prioritized; no flourishing spaces; metrics limited to overdose prevention and shelter provision |
| **Illicit market-controlled territory** | Neutral zone negotiation; community elders as mediators; no direct confrontation with market actors |

### 6.3 Compassion Club Procurement

Safe supply requires sourcing regulated substances in contexts where they are criminalized or restricted.

**Mechanism:**

| Level | Approach |
|-------|----------|
| **BAZ-Level** | Collective purchasing agreements among Hubs; volume negotiation with pharmaceutical co-ops |
| **Rogue Pharma Co-ops** | Where legal supply unavailable, BAZs may support community-based production of regulated alternatives (e.g., clean stimulants, opioid substitutes) under medical oversight |
| **Medical Gatekeeper Bridge** | In contexts requiring prescriptions, Hubs establish relationships with sympathetic medical providers who prescribe transitional substances |
| **Supply Chain Transparency** | All sourcing documented with harm reduction goals; no profit motive; quality testing for purity |

**Red Line:** Compassion Clubs do not engage with illicit market distribution networks. The goal is to bypass, not partner with, criminalized supply chains.

### 6.4 Conditions for Ethical Implementation (Expanded)

**Consent Requirements:**
- Community consent documented through Listening Circles or equivalent participatory process
- Consent is ongoing, not one-time; withdrawal of consent triggers phased shutdown

**Prohibition on Coercion:**
- No Hub may condition survival resources on participation, sobriety, or compliance
- No data sharing with carceral systems under any circumstances
- Forced rehabilitation is explicitly prohibited

**Authoritarian Context Safeguards:**
- Decentralized communication (no central database of participants)
- Diaspora-controlled funding streams
- Protection strategies for Weavers (anonymity where needed, escape routes)
- No public association with Protocol that would endanger participants

## 7. Economic Model

### 7.1 Funding Sources

| Source | Implementation Stage | Mechanism |
|--------|---------------------|----------|
| **Global Commons Fund** | Phase 1-3 | Baseline allocation for Hubs, unconditional |
| **NGO & Municipal Grants** | Phase 1 (Fiat Bridge) | Existing harm reduction funding channels |
| **Corporate Harm Levy** | Phase 2-3 | Tax on addictive product creators; staged implementation |
| **Addiction Impact Bonds** | Phase 2-3 | Private investment with outcome-based returns |
| **Community Safety Bonds** | Phase 2-3 | Divestment from carceral budgets |
| **Hearts Crowdfunding** | Phase 2-3 | Community-based digital or non-digital contributions |

### 7.2 Hearts: Non-Digital and Digital

**Hearts** are AUBI Layer 2 currency for care-based work. The system functions without digital infrastructure.

| Version | Mechanism |
|---------|-----------|
| **Non-Digital Hearts** | Physical tokens; community record-keeping (ledger book); trust-based distribution; redeemable at Hub for food, supplies, or external vendors participating in network |
| **Digital Hearts** | Ledger on Digital Commons; optional upgrade for contexts with reliable infrastructure; interoperability with other BAZ systems |

**Transition:** Communities may operate non-digital Hearts indefinitely. Digital Hearts are enhancements, not requirements.

### 7.3 Fiat Bridge Strategy

Before Hearts and Global Commons Fund are fully functional, Hubs require access to existing currency.

**Bridge Mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **NGO Fiscal Sponsorship** | Hubs operate under existing NGO 501(c)(3) or equivalent for grant access |
| **Municipal Harm Reduction Contracts** | Cities with harm reduction policies contract Hubs for overdose prevention services |
| **Mutual Aid Funds** | Community-based rotating funds, diaspora remittances |
| **Recovery Weaver Stipends** | Direct fiat payments to Weavers from pooled funds |

**Transition Goal:** Move toward Hearts as primary compensation mechanism within Phase 2, with fiat bridge maintained for external transactions (rent, utilities, supplies).

### 7.4 Corporate Accountability (Staged)

| Phase | Mechanism |
|-------|-----------|
| **Phase 1** | Voluntary contributions from corporations with BAZ operations; public recognition for contributors |
| **Phase 2** | Harm Levy enacted in BAZs with governance authority; impact bonds structured with outcome metrics |
| **Phase 3** | International frameworks for cross-jurisdictional enforcement; supply chain transparency requirements |

**Legal Note:** Corporate accountability mechanisms are implemented at BAZ level. International enforcement requires treaty-level frameworks beyond this Protocol's scope.

## 8. Metrics & Accountability

### 8.1 Systemic-Only Metrics Principle

The Cairn Protocol **does not track individual compliance**. Metrics measure the health of the Hub, not the behavior of participants.

**Prohibited Metrics:**
- Individual sobriety or abstinence
- Attendance tracking for optional services
- Clean drug tests
- Any metric used to condition access to survival resources

**Permitted Metrics:**

| Category | Metric |
|----------|--------|
| **Primary (Non-Negotiable)** | Overdose deaths prevented; number of unconditional beds provided; safe supply doses distributed |
| **Secondary (Desirable)** | Community Connection Score (LMCI sub-metric); Reduction in carceral referrals; Recovery Capital Index (Hub-level) |
| **Exploratory (Learning)** | Grief to Grace Ratio (rituals vs. overdoses); Cultural Resonance Metric (adoption of local healing practices); Weaver Retention Rate |

### 8.2 Data Sovereignty

- **User-Controlled Data Vaults:** Individuals control their own data via Digital Commons
- **No Carceral Sharing:** Data sharing with law enforcement prohibited
- **Aggregate Only:** Public reporting uses aggregated, anonymized data
- **Right to Erasure:** Participants may request data deletion at any time

### 8.3 Community Feedback Mechanism

**Listening Circles:**
- Quarterly, facilitated by Recovery Weavers not affiliated with Hub management
- Separate circles for participants, families, and Weavers
- Anonymized synthesis published

**Feedback Integration:**
- Immediate action for safety concerns
- Quarterly review for operational feedback
- Escalation to Cairn Council for systemic issues

## 2. The Sabotage Playbook

### 2.1 Principles of Sabotage (as Resistance, Not Destruction)

Sabotage in this context means **disrupting systems that harm your community** — not destroying infrastructure, but rendering it unusable for purposes of control.

| Target | Sabotage Tactic | Principle |
|--------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Forced Rehabilitation Camps** | Document conditions; leak to international media; map escape routes for participants; create diversion networks that intercept intake vehicles | Do not attack staff; make the camp politically unsustainable, not physically dangerous |
| **Corrupt Police Checkpoints** | Coordinate real-time alerts; use encrypted comms to warn Weavers and participants; create alternative movement corridors | Avoid confrontation; make the checkpoint ineffective through evasion |
| **Corporate Supply Chains (Diversion)** | Track industrial solvents (jet fuel, glue) from legitimate supply chains to street markets; expose diversion points; pressure corporate accountability through public campaigns | Target the diversion point, not the street dealer; the goal is supply chain transparency, not criminalization |
| **Predatory Advertising** | Document and expose targeted marketing of addictive products in low-income communities; amplify through community media; support Design Justice Tribunal cases | The goal is to make predatory design politically and reputationally costly |

### 2.2 The Neutral Zone Strategy

When operating in territory controlled by illicit markets or hostile state forces, Hubs can establish themselves as **neutral zones** — spaces where no violence occurs and no reporting happens.

**Negotiation Principles:**

- Approach through trusted intermediaries (local elders, religious leaders, neighborhood councils)
- State clearly: the Hub is not a competitor, not an informant, not a recruitment site
- Offer transparency: open doors, observable operations
- Accept conditions: no direct confrontation with market actors; no reporting of their activities

**Red Line:**

If the Hub becomes a site of violence, distribution, or recruitment — **close it**. Neutrality is a survival strategy, not an alliance. When neutrality is violated, the Hub relocates or goes underground.

### 2.3 The Diversion Network

In contexts where safe supply is criminalized, Hubs may need to operate underground procurement networks.

**Structure:**

- **No central database:** Participants known only to direct contacts
- **Decentralized storage:** Supplies held in multiple locations
- **Trust-based distribution:** Known participants vouch for new recipients
- **Medical oversight:** Where possible, sympathetic providers prescribe or certify

**Risk Management:**

- Separate roles: procurement, storage, distribution are handled by different individuals
- Communication compartmentalized: no single person knows the full network
- Escape routes: pre-planned exit strategies for compromised nodes

## 3. Underground Network Protocols

### 3.1 When to Go Underground

The Core Governance Framework assumes open operation. Go underground when:

- State forces are conducting raids on Hubs or arresting Weavers
- Illicit market actors have declared Hubs a threat
- Forced rehabilitation campaigns are actively sweeping your area
- Public association with the Protocol would endanger participants

### 3.2 Underground Hub Structure

| Element | Open Hub | Underground Hub |
|---------|----------|-----------------|
| **Location** | Fixed, publicly known | Rotating, shared only through trusted channels |
| **Services** | Full range (food, shelter, medical) | Reduced: harm reduction, crisis support, referrals |
| **Communication** | Public phone, social media | Encrypted messaging, signal drops, word of mouth |
| **Funding** | NGO grants, municipal contracts | Diaspora remittances, mutual aid, crowdfunding |
| **Documentation** | Public reporting, transparent metrics | No written records; verbal reporting only |

### 3.3 Security Culture

**Basic Practices:**

- **Compartmentalization:** Need-to-know only. No one knows more than necessary
- **Operational Security (OpSec):** No discussing operations in unsecured spaces; assume surveillance
- **Encryption:** Signal or equivalent for all digital communication; no SMS
- **Burner Systems:** Disposable phones, email accounts, financial instruments
- **Pattern Disruption:** Vary routes, schedules, meeting locations

**For Weavers in Active Danger:**

- **Anonymity:** Use working names; no public association with Hub
- **Escape Plans:** Pre-identified safe houses, exit routes, border crossing resources
- **Planetary Guardian Visa Applications:** Pre-submit for trusted individuals (see Section 7)

### 3.4 Diaspora Support Structures

For Hubs operating under authoritarian regimes or in conflict zones, diaspora communities are critical infrastructure.

**Diaspora Roles:**

- **Funding:** Remittances through informal channels (hawala, trusted individuals)
- **Communication:** Secure relay of information from inside to international audiences
- **Advocacy:** International pressure campaigns coordinated with Hub needs
- **Legal Support:** Immigration pathways for endangered Weavers
- **Supply Chains:** Procurement of medical supplies, safe supply materials through non-local channels

**Structure:** Diaspora nodes operate separately from in-country Hubs. No single individual connects both networks.

## 4. FAQ for the Frontlines

*These are questions you will hear from community members, skeptical participants, and hostile actors. Answers are for frontline organizers, not policymakers.*

**Q: "You're just giving them drugs. How is that helping?"**

*A:* We're giving them *safe* drugs. The drugs on the street are killing people — cut with fentanyl, contaminated with industrial chemicals, unpredictable potency. Safe supply keeps people alive. You can't recover if you're dead.

**Q: "What about the children inhaling jet fuel? Shouldn't we force them into treatment?"**

*A:* Forced treatment doesn't work. The data is clear — coercion creates resistance, not recovery. What children need is safety, food, and adults who don't abandon them. When you offer that without conditions, some will choose to stop. But that choice has to be theirs.

**Q: "Aren't you just enabling addiction?"**

*A:* Addiction doesn't need enabling. It's doing fine on its own. What we're enabling is survival. And from survival, sometimes, recovery becomes possible.

**Q: "Why don't you call the police on the dealers?"**

*A:* The dealers are often the only source of income in neighborhoods where there are no jobs. Calling the police doesn't solve that — it just creates violence. We need to change the conditions that make selling drugs the only option. That's not a police problem. That's an economy problem.

**Q: "How do I know you're not an informant?"**

*A:* You don't. Trust is built over time. Ask around. Talk to people who've been through our Hub. If you're not comfortable, don't come. We'll be here when you're ready.

**Q: "What if the government shuts you down?"**

*A:* They might. We have contingency plans. Some services go underground. Some Weavers relocate. We've already built relationships with diaspora supporters who can sustain us. The Hub is a structure, but the community is the infrastructure. They can't shut down community.

## 6. Advocacy Tactics Guide

### 6.1 International Pressure Campaigns

When local advocacy is impossible or dangerous, international campaigns can create leverage.

**Tactics:**

- **Media Exposure:** Partner with international journalists who can document conditions (see note on security in Section 6.4)
- **Human Rights Complaints:** File with UN mechanisms, regional human rights bodies
- **Parliamentary Inquiries:** Engage sympathetic legislators in countries with diaspora populations
- **Corporate Campaigns:** Target corporations profiting from addiction in your context

**Security:** International advocacy must be coordinated with in-country organizers. Never expose local Weavers or participants to additional risk. Use pseudonyms, anonymized footage, and secure communication channels.

### 6.2 Local Advocacy in Hostile Contexts

**When the state is the threat:**

- **Framing:** Avoid "harm reduction" language if it attracts repression. Use local frames: *tawba* (return), community protection, family preservation
- **Allies:** Religious leaders, elders, neighborhood councils, women's groups
- **Demands:** Focus on achievable goals — not full legalization but: stop forced rehabilitation raids; allow medical access; provide food and shelter without conditions

**When illicit markets are the threat:**

- **Framing:** Neutral zone, not competition
- **Allies:** Local elders, neighborhood councils, religious leaders who can mediate
- **Demands:** Non-interference with Hub operations; recognition as neutral space

### 6.3 Coalition Building

No Hub survives alone. Build coalitions with:

- **Harm reduction organizations** (existing infrastructure)
- **Mutual aid networks** (food distribution, housing support)
- **Religious institutions** (mosques, churches, temples with social service capacity)
- **Labor unions** (workers who understand economic precarity)
- **Legal aid organizations** (defense against carceral systems)
- **Diaspora associations** (funding, international advocacy)

### 6.4 Media & Documentation

**When to Document:**

- Forced rehabilitation raids
- Police violence against participants
- Corporate supply chain diversion
- Conditions inside camps

**How to Document Safely:**

- Use encrypted devices; no cloud storage
- Remove metadata from photos and videos before sharing
- Blur faces of participants who have not consented
- Store documentation with trusted third parties outside the country
- Never document in ways that could identify Weavers or participants to hostile actors

**When Not to Document:**

- If documentation would expose underground networks
- If participants could be identified and targeted
- If the documentation itself is the primary goal (documentation serves organizing, not the reverse)

## 7. Legal Defense & Planetary Guardian Visas

### 7.1 Legal Defense Protocols

**For Weavers Facing Arrest:**

- **Pre-arranged legal support:** Identify sympathetic lawyers before you need them
- **Know your rights:** In some jurisdictions, harm reduction work is protected; in others, it's criminalized. Know which you're in
- **Evidence destruction protocols:** Pre-planned methods to destroy records, participant data, supply information if raid occurs
- **Communication tree:** Designated individual to notify diaspora supporters, media, legal aid if arrest happens

**For Hubs Facing Raids:**

- **Early warning network:** Community members alert Hub when raids begin
- **Supply dispersal:** Pre-identified locations to move supplies quickly
- **Participant safety:** Escape routes; safe houses; pre-arranged emergency contacts

### 7.2 Planetary Guardian Visas

The Planetary Guardian Visa is a mechanism within the GGF for protecting individuals at risk due to their work in implementing the frameworks.

**Eligibility:**

- Recovery Weavers facing arrest, violence, or forced rehabilitation
- Hub operators in contexts where Hub is being targeted
- Whistleblowers exposing corporate or state complicity in addiction harms

**Application Process:**

- Submit through trusted intermediaries (see Section 3.4 on diaspora support)
- Applications expedited for active threat situations
- Resettlement coordinated with host BAZs or participating nation-states

**Note:** This mechanism assumes functioning GGF infrastructure. In early phases, use existing asylum pathways where available.

## 8. Rapid Response Hearts Fund

### 8.1 Purpose

The Rapid Response Hearts Fund provides emergency funding for Hubs facing immediate threats.

**Uses:**

- Legal defense for arrested Weavers
- Emergency relocation of endangered participants
- Temporary closure and re-establishment of Hubs
- Medical care for injured Weavers or participants
- Communication infrastructure during crackdowns

### 8.2 Access

- Applications through diaspora intermediaries or trusted BAZ partners
- Decisions within 72 hours for standard requests; 24 hours for active threat
- Disbursement through secure channels (hawala, diaspora remittances, cryptocurrency where appropriate)
- No reporting requirements that would expose recipients

### 8.3 Funding the Fund

The Rapid Response Hearts Fund is maintained through:

- Global Commons Fund allocation
- Corporate Harm Levy contributions
- Community crowdfunding
- Diaspora donations

**What you already know:**

- Who in your community can be trusted
- Which doors to knock on and which to avoid

- Connections to others doing similar work
- Frameworks that might help you explain what you're doing
- Resources when you need them
- A reminder that you are not alone
