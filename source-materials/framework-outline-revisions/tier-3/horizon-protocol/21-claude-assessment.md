## Revision 1: Developmental Stage Model (Core Principles + Section 2.1)

**Location:** Two changes. First, replace the current Core Principle 2 definition.
Second, add a structural section to the Youth Caucus (Section 2.1) that maps
stage-appropriate participation.

**Rationale:** The current framework defines youth as 16-30 without differentiation.
This spans adolescence through early adulthood — life stages with fundamentally
different cognitive capacities, institutional experience, and support needs. A
16-year-old forming political consciousness for the first time and a 29-year-old
with a decade of work experience and possibly a family need different things from
this framework. Without stage differentiation, the youngest risk being overwhelmed
and the oldest risk dominating.

### Part A: Core Principle 2 revision

**Replace:**
```
2. **Youth Empowerment**: Youth (16-30) hold voting roles in Meta-Governance
   Councils.
```

**With:**
```
2. **Youth Empowerment**: Youth hold voting roles in Meta-Governance Councils
   through a developmental stage model that matches participation to capacity:

   | **Stage** | **Age Range** | **Orientation** |
   |-----------|---------------|-----------------|
   | Emerging Youth | 16–21 | Learning, mentorship, graduated participation |
   | Core Youth | 22–26 | Full participation, leadership, committee authority |
   | Transitioning Youth | 27–30 | Bridge roles connecting Youth Caucus to adult governance |
   | Youth+ Extension | Community-defined | Flexible extension for marginalized youth needing continued support |

   Age boundaries are defaults. BAZ-level governance may adjust ranges by
   up to two years in either direction based on local cultural norms and
   developmental context, subject to Earth Council review.
```

### Part B: Section 2.1 (Global Youth Assembly) expansion

**Insert after the Anti-Tokenism Safeguards subsection:**

```
#### Stage-Appropriate Participation

The Youth Caucus structures participation by developmental stage to ensure
that engagement is empowering rather than overwhelming, and that no single
age cohort dominates:

**Emerging Youth (16–21)**

- **Role:** Observers with voice, graduated to voting members through a
  structured readiness pathway
- **Participation:** Attend Caucus sessions, contribute to committee
  discussions, participate in Reciprocal Mentorship pairings, and submit
  policy proposals through designated sponsors (Core or Transitioning
  Youth members)
- **Readiness Pathway:** Completion of civic education modules (Section
  3.2), participation in at least two BAZ-level governance activities,
  and endorsement by a Reciprocal Mentorship elder — at which point the
  Emerging Youth member gains full voting rights regardless of age
- **Protection:** Workload caps enforced by the Safeguarding Annex;
  dedicated well-being support; no requirement to engage with hostile
  media or adversarial proceedings; parental or guardian consent required
  for international travel and public representation under 18
- **Rationale:** Young people at this stage are developing political
  consciousness. The framework invites them in without making governance
  a burden. The readiness pathway ensures that voting rights reflect
  demonstrated engagement rather than arbitrary age thresholds

**Core Youth (22–26)**

- **Role:** Full voting members, eligible for all committee leadership
  positions, co-drafting authority for Treaty policies
- **Participation:** Standing for election to Youth Caucus leadership,
  chairing committees, leading IIA review panels, representing the
  Caucus in Meta-Governance Council sessions
- **Responsibilities:** Mentoring Emerging Youth members; maintaining
  active engagement with their BAZ constituency; contributing to the
  annual "State of Youth" report
- **Rationale:** This cohort has sufficient life experience and
  institutional literacy to exercise genuine governance authority while
  retaining the perspective and energy that the framework exists to
  channel

**Transitioning Youth (27–30)**

- **Role:** Bridge members connecting Youth Caucus to adult governance
  structures
- **Participation:** Serve on joint youth-adult committees, lead
  knowledge transfer to incoming cohorts, hold liaison positions with
  Meta-Governance Councils, Guardians of the Future, and BAZ governance
  bodies
- **Transition Protocol:** At age 30 (or community-defined equivalent),
  members transition out of the Youth Caucus into adult governance roles.
  The transition includes:
  - A formal handoff of institutional knowledge to a Core Youth successor
  - Eligibility for "Youth Alumni" advisory status (non-voting, available
    for consultation for five years post-transition)
  - Priority consideration for Reciprocal Mentorship elder roles
- **Rationale:** This stage prevents the Youth Caucus from becoming a
  career path while ensuring institutional continuity. Transitioning
  members carry youth perspectives into adult governance, preventing the
  generational disconnect the framework was designed to address

**Youth+ Extension**

- **Purpose:** Rigid age boundaries disadvantage youth whose development
  has been disrupted by disability, displacement, conflict, incarceration,
  or systemic marginalization. The Youth+ Extension allows continued
  Youth Caucus participation beyond age 30 for individuals who need it
- **Eligibility:** Determined by BAZ-level governance through a
  participatory process, not by individual application. Communities
  identify members whose circumstances warrant extended support
- **Duration:** Renewable in two-year increments, with no fixed upper
  limit but subject to periodic community review
- **Safeguard:** Youth+ members may not hold Caucus leadership positions
  to prevent age-based power concentration; their role is continued
  participation and support, not institutional authority
```

## Revision 2: CYIA Graduated Enforcement Protocol (Section 2.4 expansion)

**Location:** Insert in Section 2.4 (Corporate Youth Impact Assessments), after
the existing scorecard description and before the Implementation subsection.

**Rationale:** The scorecard creates visibility but not accountability. Without
enforcement, corporations can score poorly and face no consequences. The graduated
protocol provides clear pathways — from recognition through improvement to
sanctions — giving corporations both incentive and clarity while giving the Youth
Caucus real leverage.

```
#### Enforcement Protocol

Assessment scores trigger graduated responses. The protocol prioritizes
improvement over punishment but ensures that persistent harm has consequences:

| **Level** | **Score** | **Response** | **Lead Body** |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| Exemplary | 80–100 | Public recognition via GGF certification mark; eligibility for GGF procurement preference; featured in Annual Youth Impact Report; tax incentives where BAZ fiscal authority applies | Youth Caucus, Meta-Governance |
| Developing | 60–79 | Required improvement plan with specific, measurable targets and 12-month timeline; annual re-assessment; public disclosure of score and improvement plan; assigned a Youth Caucus liaison for consultation | Youth Caucus, BAZ governance |
| Concerning | 40–59 | Public disclosure of poor performance via Community Well-being Dashboard; loss of GGF certification; exclusion from GGF procurement and GGF-sanctioned market access; mandatory youth stakeholder engagement process overseen by BAZ governance | Youth Caucus, Digital Justice Tribunal |
| Harmful | Below 40 | Referral to Digital Justice Tribunal for systemic harm investigation; potential sanctions including market access restrictions, Hearts/Leaves economy exclusion, and mandatory reparative payments to affected youth communities via Global Commons Fund | Digital Justice Tribunal, Youth Caucus |

**The Youth Caucus Review Trigger:**

For any corporation scoring below 40, the Youth Caucus may trigger an
immediate investigation with binding recommendations. This requires:

- A two-thirds vote of the Youth Caucus (Core and Transitioning members)
- Documented evidence of specific youth harm (labor exploitation,
  environmental damage affecting youth health, digital manipulation,
  or intergenerational cost-shifting)
- Referral to the Digital Justice Tribunal within 30 days of vote

The Tribunal's findings are binding within the GGF ecosystem. Corporations
outside the GGF ecosystem are subject to reputational consequences and
exclusion from GGF-aligned markets and supply chains.

**Improvement Pathway:**

Corporations at any level can request a reassessment after implementing
changes. Reassessment is conducted by a joint panel of the Youth Caucus
and the relevant BAZ governance body. Movement from Concerning to
Developing, or from Developing to Exemplary, is publicly recognized —
the framework rewards genuine improvement, not just initial compliance.

**Assessment Frequency:**

- Corporations operating within GGF-aligned BAZs: annual assessment
- Corporations seeking GGF certification: assessment at application
  and every two years thereafter
- Corporations referred to the Tribunal: reassessment required before
  any sanctions are lifted
```

## Revision 3: Gamified Youth Platform Protective Design (Section 3.5 expansion)

**Location:** Insert in Section 3.5 (Gamified Youth Platform), replacing the
current Components subsection with an expanded version that includes protective
design principles.

**Rationale:** The framework builds a digital platform to engage youth, but
provides no protective guardrails against the exact harms the framework elsewhere
identifies — algorithmic manipulation, attention extraction, digital addiction.
The Cognitive Sovereignty Accord's No Addiction by Design principle (1.7 revision)
and the Social Fabric Framework's anti-algorithm design principles (0.9 revision)
establish ecosystem-wide standards that this platform must meet. Consistency
matters: a youth governance platform that uses engagement-maximizing mechanics
would undermine the framework's own values.

**Replace the current Components subsection with:**

```
- **Components**:
  - Edutainment voting tools, metaverse spaces for governance simulation.
  - Multi-lingual, sensory-inclusive, BAZ-managed.
  - Data sovereignty via Love Ledger.

- **Protective Design Principles:**

  The Gamified Youth Platform is governed by the same principles that apply
  to all digital consciousness environments under the Cognitive Sovereignty
  Accord and all community digital infrastructure under the Social Fabric
  Framework. Youth are the population most vulnerable to engagement-
  extractive design; the platform that serves them must be the strongest
  counter-model.

  **1. No Addiction by Design**

  The platform must not employ:

  - Variable-ratio reinforcement schedules (loot boxes, randomized rewards,
    unpredictable notification timing)
  - Infinite scroll or auto-play mechanics
  - Social comparison features that exploit status anxiety (public
    leaderboards, follower counts, visible engagement metrics)
  - Streak mechanics that create guilt for disengagement
  - Any algorithm that optimizes for time-on-platform rather than
    user-defined participation goals

  Platform design is subject to the Neuro-Ethics Review Board's
  Algorithmic Consciousness Impact Assessment (ACIA) under the Cognitive
  Sovereignty Accord, adapted for youth-specific vulnerabilities.

  **2. Real-World First**

  The platform exists to facilitate governance participation, not to
  replace it:

  - Hearts and recognition rewards are tied to real-world engagement
    (BAZ governance participation, Community Work Team contributions,
    Reciprocal Mentorship attendance) — not to digital activity metrics
  - Platform achievements are credited to the participant's Love Ledger
    record of actual governance contribution, not to platform-internal
    status systems
  - Periodic "offline challenges" prompt users toward in-person
    engagement with their BAZ community
  - LMCI tracking (per Social Fabric Framework) weights physical
    participation more heavily than digital interaction

  **3. Sovereignty Over Attention**

  Youth own their attention and their data:

  - All algorithmic curation is transparent and user-adjustable —
    participants can see why content is surfaced and change their
    preferences
  - Usage analytics are available to the user, not just the platform:
    "You spent X hours this week; here's how that compares to your
    self-set goals"
  - Weekly well-being check-ins (opt-out available) prompt reflection
    on whether platform use is serving the participant's goals
  - Attention data is never sold, shared, or used for purposes beyond
    the participant's explicit consent — governed by Aurora Accord data
    sovereignty provisions

  **4. Offline Parity**

  Full participation in Horizon Protocol governance must be possible
  without using the platform:

  - All voting, proposal submission, committee participation, and
    Reciprocal Mentorship functions have offline analogs (paper ballots,
    in-person assemblies, postal submission)
  - Youth who opt out of the digital platform receive no disadvantage
    in representation, access to resources, or eligibility for
    leadership positions
  - BAZs with limited digital infrastructure are supported through
    the Social Fabric Framework's offline-first design provisions

  **5. Age-Appropriate Design**

  Platform features adapt to developmental stage:

  - **Emerging Youth (16–21):** Simplified interfaces, guided
    onboarding with mentor support, content moderation with higher
    protection thresholds, mandatory cooling-off periods before posting
    in contentious threads
  - **Core Youth (22–26):** Full platform features, self-directed
    moderation preferences, advanced governance tools
  - **Transitioning Youth (27–30):** Bridge tools for connecting
    Youth Caucus activity to adult governance platforms

  Design is audited by the Foundation Protocol oversight body for
  cognitive equity, ensuring accessibility for neurodivergent and
  disabled youth across all stages.
```
