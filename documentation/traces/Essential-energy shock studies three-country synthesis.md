# Essential-energy shock studies: three-country synthesis

Nov 23, 2026 · @Björn Kenneth Holmström

## Summary

Three country studies (Germany, Brazil, Sweden) tested whether existing institutions can protect households' access to essential energy in a price shock and in a shortage, and whether the patched GGF frameworks would change that. Each study was reviewed externally and reconciled before closing.

- **Protection depends on the whole chain.** Each country breaks at a different link: Germany at authorization and reach (no price-shock instrument), Brazil at delivery and coverage (an in-kind entitlement that depends on retailers and stock), Sweden at implementation, timing and expiry (an automatic scheme that pays months after the bills and ends on 31 December).
- **Pre-authorization is neither necessary nor sufficient.** Fast lawful action can substitute for it (Brazil), and a pre-designed, funded scheme can still arrive too late (Sweden). The test is timely, effective protection.
- **Two gaps recur across countries:** households whose energy is bundled in rent fall outside the main channels, and household priority in shortage ranges from protected (Germany) to last (Sweden) to undefined (Brazil).
- **GGF's contribution was diagnostic, not operational.** It classified shocks, separated legal readiness from reality and recorded unheld duties. It added no executable protection, and its market-power referral duplicated existing routes in all three countries.
- **Every first draft missed something that already existed.** GGF's first duty in any jurisdiction is to discover what institutions already do.

**What follows:** DER becomes an evaluation rubric hosted in GMEAIA, not a framework. Four narrow patches (GMEAIA, Climate & Energy, AUBI, Disaster Risk Reduction) clarify or reduce GGF's footprint; nothing new is built. Whether GGF improves outcomes remains untested; the best next test is to run the rubric on real 2026 episodes.

## What was tested

**Question.** The investigation began by asking whether GGF needed a Democratic Essentials Resilience (DER) model before changing its economic frameworks. A gas-price shock trace through the corpus found that GGF already had most mechanisms, and that the real gap was the short window in which prices hit households before any authorized action can. Three framework patches followed (Climate & Energy v2.2, AUBI v2.7.1, Gaian Trade v0.9.3). The country studies then asked a sharper question:

> Can existing institutions move from detecting a shock to delivering lawful, timely, effective protection to households, and where does that chain break? Does adding the patched GGF procedures change the answer?

**Design.**

- **Three jurisdictions, one functional problem:** access to essential energy in a price shock (Case A) and in a physical shortage (Case B), over the first six weeks. Germany used networked gas, Brazil bottled LPG, Sweden electricity with district heating as a separate pathway.
- **Baseline against counterfactual:** existing institutions alone, then the same institutions with the GGF procedures. GGF was given no new authority or record type, and no response time or adoption was assumed.
- **Frozen versions:** Climate & Energy v2.2, AUBI v2.7.1, Gaian Trade v0.9.3 and GMEAIA v0.2.2 did not change between studies, so the three results are comparable.
- **Grading:** instruments graded specified, enacted, funded or operational; recorded handoff states kept separate from readiness; timing split into four measures (earliest legally possible, earliest for a stated household, expected processing, protection actually received).
- **Review:** each study went through an external review and a v0.2 reconciliation before closing.

**Evidence standard.** Desk tests from public sources, as of 23 November 2026. None of the studies tested whether GGF improves outcomes; the evidence concerns what existing institutions can do and where GGF procedures would add or duplicate.

## Three configurations

The three countries are not points on one scale. Each has built a different part of the protection chain and left a different part open.

|  | Germany (gas) | Brazil (LPG) | Sweden (electricity) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Price-shock instrument** | None pre-built; social assistance claim by claim; collective relief needs a statute | Gás do Povo: free refills for about 15 million low-income families | Högkostnadsskydd: automatic payment above a published price threshold, for all households with their own grid contract |
| **Shortage regime** | Notfallplan Gas; households are protected customers | Supply duties, monitoring, service plans, emergency law; no household-priority rule | Contracted 330 MW reserve, then operator-ordered load cuts; households lowest priority |
| **Fast lawful route** | None outside a new statute | Medidas Provisórias, in force on publication, lapse unless converted | Government ordinance under a budget decision |
| **Where it breaks** | Authorization and reach | Delivery-channel reliability, coverage and physical access | Implementation, payment timing, expiry and household priority |

Germany's price-shock chain breaks at the start (no instrument, no trigger). Brazil's holds at the start but weakens in delivery and coverage. Sweden's holds at the start but breaks at the end: payment arrives months after the bills, and the scheme expires on 31 December. The ratings are qualitative judgments from the three studies, not measurements.

## Cross-country findings

1. **Protection depends on the whole chain, not on any one instrument.** No country was strong on every link. An entitlement without a trigger (Germany), a trigger without reliable delivery (Brazil), or delivery that arrives after the bill and stops before the shock does (Sweden) each leaves households exposed. This was the working hypothesis; all three studies support it.
2. **Pre-authorization is neither necessary nor sufficient.** Brazil's executive acted within weeks in 2026 through emergency law that took effect on publication. Sweden designed and funded its scheme in advance, yet still needed an implementing ordinance after the trigger and pays months after the bills. The test is timely, effective protection, whatever the legal route.
3. **Each step of authority is separate.** Political activation, budget authority, the implementing legal instrument, administrative preparation and actual payment came apart in Sweden. In Germany, a payment channel existed in law but had no payout statute and bank details for under a fifth of tax IDs. A recorded `offered` handoff likewise means a referral was sent, not that anyone acted.
4. **Delivery through intermediaries moves risk; it does not remove it.** Brazil's voucher shields the household from the price and shifts the shock to the federal budget and to retailers paid a reference price with no published update trigger. If that price lags, the risk returns to the household as an empty shelf at the voucher counter, even when the country has enough LPG.
5. **Households whose energy is bundled in rent fall outside the channels.** German heating costs settled through landlords, Swedish electricity included in rent, and district heating supplied through landlords all sit outside the main household instruments. Brazil's nearest equivalent is the households outside the programme's eligibility rules.
6. **Household priority in shortage varies.** Germany treats households as protected customers; Sweden ranks ordinary households last and protects by power line, not by home; Brazil has no household-priority rule for LPG. The essential minimum in Climate & Energy §6.1 is a protection objective that none of the hosts guarantees in volume.
7. **Shortage triggers differ in kind.** Germany's emergency stage is a political declaration; Sweden's load cuts follow an operational decision by the system operator, executed within 15 minutes; Brazil has no pre-built trigger for LPG allocation.
8. **The cases were not hypothetical.** In November 2026, German wholesale gas had roughly tripled since February, Brazil was still absorbing the 2026 Gulf shock, and Sweden had just activated its support for SE4. Several of the gaps found are live this winter.
9. **Every first draft missed something that already existed.** Germany's regulator-commissioned value-chain study, Brazil's redemption dashboard and service plans, and Sweden's contracted strategic reserve were each found only in review. This is a finding about GGF, not only about method: Meta-Governance v1.6 sets the standard as coordination that names responsibility, respects jurisdiction, operates within the timescale of the condition, and reduces rather than exports administrative burden. A GGF interface that cannot reliably find what institutions already do would duplicate them.

**What GGF demonstrably contributed:** a common way to classify the shock, to separate legal readiness from operational reality, to record duties no one holds, and to check host instruments against conformance tests. That contribution is diagnostic.

**What it did not contribute:** any executable protection, any faster action, or any capability that the host did not already have or could not build itself. Where it proposed a new channel (referral, cascade map), the host usually had one already.

## DER evaluation rubric

DER becomes an evaluation rubric, not a framework. It asks whether a jurisdiction can protect households' access to an essential good in a shock, and where that protection breaks. It uses existing GMEAIA fields and creates no authority or record type. It applies beyond energy (water, food, medicines) with the same structure.

**Step 0: discovery before absence.** Before grading anything as missing, search for and list regulations, contingency plans, monitoring dashboards, administrative datasets, payment mechanisms and operational procedures. Record what was searched and what was found. A `constitutional_gap` without a discovery record is not a finding.

**Step 1: classify the shock.** Price shock (good available, unaffordable); physical scarcity (good not available); delivery-access shortage (good available nationally but not through the protected channel); local network failure (an outage, not a shortage). Each has different owners and instruments.

**Step 2: grade the six links** for the main household instrument in each case.

| Link | Question | Distinctions to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Authority** | Who can lawfully authorize and execute the action? | Political activation, budget authority, implementing legal instrument, administrative preparation and actual payment, each graded separately |
| **Activation** | What triggers it, and who verifies? | Automatic, operational or political trigger; whether the threshold is published in advance |
| **Delivery** | Through which channel does protection reach people? | Entitlement, practical redemption through the protected channel, and physical access to the good |
| **Coverage and capacity** | Who can actually be reached, at what scale? | Targeting by need or by consumption; households whose energy is bundled in rent; groups outside eligibility; intermediaries' incentives (cost pressure and opportunity cost) and whether payments to them track the shock; administrative, data and fiscal capacity |
| **Timing** | Can protection arrive before material harm? | Earliest legally possible, earliest for a stated household, expected processing, protection actually received; and when payment arrives relative to when the bill falls due |
| **Termination and correction** | How does it end, reverse or give remedy? | Orderly sunset or unplanned lapse; end linked to the shock or to the calendar; review and remedy |

**Grades:** in place (legally ready and operationally demonstrated); weak or conditional; break point; not applicable; not assessed. Each grade carries its evidence basis: primary source, secondary-verified, or not verified.

**Step 3: report separately** legal readiness, demonstrated operational capacity, protection actually received (where data exists), the first executable protective action, and the coordination burden.

**Step 4: test GGF's marginal value.** For each GGF procedure, state what it would add beyond the host's existing capability. If nothing, record the redundancy rather than the procedure.

## Decisions

The freeze ends here. The evidence supports a few narrow patches, all of which reduce or clarify GGF's footprint rather than extend it. None creates authority, a record type or a price-setting power.

**Narrow patches warranted**

| Framework | Patch | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GMEAIA** (first, since it hosts the rubric) | GMEAIA-08 attaches to a host's existing referral or surveillance duty and does not open a parallel route. A `constitutional_gap` needs a discovery record. Add the DER rubric as an assessment profile built from existing fields | GMEAIA-08 duplicated existing routes in all three countries; every first draft missed an existing capability |
| **Climate & Energy** | §6.1: the essential minimum is a protection objective, not a volume guarantee in shortage. §6.3: add the bottled-fuel equivalent (access to a refill at a protected price, plus an allocation rule in shortage). §6.2 and §6.5: name households whose energy is bundled in rent. Appendix E: a template composed from GMEAIA records. §7: refer to the DER rubric for host readiness | S1 Case B, S2, S3 bundled-rent gap, external review of S1 |
| **AUBI** | §5.1.5 gains three conformance tests: the implementing instrument exists before the trigger; protection arrives by the time the bill falls due, or a bridge exists; the end follows the shock indicator, with review, not the calendar alone. In-kind channels index payments to intermediaries to a published trigger | S3 timing and expiry; S2 reference-price lag |
| **Disaster Risk Reduction** | Appendix G requires that existing dependency evidence is current, challenged, shared and linked to the body that decides allocation, instead of adding a parallel cascade map | S1 Prognos study; S3 Styrel |

**Not to build**

- A stabilization interface or any GGF authority over prices or allocation.
- DER as a separate framework.
- A parallel GGF referral system.
- A separate dependency-review template: its fields become the rubric's evidence table.

**No change**

- Gaian Trade v0.9.3: its reserve priorities are consistent with all three hosts.
- Meta-Governance §5.1 convening: no evidence either way.

**Separate track, still open**

- The *Preventing 1933* audit of the Hearts firewall claim (including reading the Galofré-Vilà study first) was not touched by these studies and remains pending.

**Suggested order:** GMEAIA, then Climate & Energy, AUBI and DRR, each with its standfirst.

## Open questions and evidence to verify

**Facts to confirm before relying on them**

- Brazil: the dashboard figures (79% redemption, about 25,000 active retailers, service-plan counts) came via the external review and are secondary-verified.
- Sweden: whether and when the support ordinance for October–December is adopted, and whether it changes the announced design; how households with home medical equipment are identified in load cuts; how the 1 January 2027 legal change carries over disconnection and Styrel rules.
- Germany: the fate of the pending bills that change S1's grades: the gas reserve, the extension of §29 GWB, and the Wohngeld heating component.

**What none of the studies could measure**

- Protection actually received: arrears, disconnections, voucher redemption under stress, and payment dates. No country publishes outcome data good enough to answer this.
- Whether GGF improves outcomes. Every GGF response time remains a specification.

**How to test the remaining claim**

The strongest available test is retrospective and live, not modelled. Sweden's October–December activation will produce a real payment date in spring 2027; Brazil's 2026 shock and Germany's 2022 relief sequence are complete episodes. Running the DER rubric on these real events, with outcome data where it exists, would show whether the rubric identifies the break points that actually caused harm, which is the claim it has to earn.
