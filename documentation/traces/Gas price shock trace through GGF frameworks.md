# Gas price shock trace through GGF frameworks

Nov 23, 2026 · @Björn Kenneth Holmström

## Summary

The GGF handles the money side of a gas shock well and the long-run transition well. It is thin in the months between, where prices are set. That gap matches Weber's "short-run stabilization" layer almost exactly.

- **Three orphans.** No GGF framework owns household energy affordability, stabilizing the prices of essentials, or responding to market power outside seeds.
- **Two inconsistencies.** Climate & Energy v2.1 has not had the conformance passes the rest of the corpus has. An older AUBI stress-test line conflicts with §5.1.4.
- **One challenge to *Preventing 1933*.** The Hearts layer passes fiat input-cost shocks through the Treasury rather than shielding people from them.
- **What works.** Financial Systems liquidity rules, the Adaptive Tax windfall pathway, DRR forecast finance, and the CIS §7.7 record that keeps recurring hunger deaths on the books.

The energy and market-power orphans can be closed with targeted patches, not a new framework. Where to house price stabilization is the one real design decision. That is where a Democratic Essentials Resilience–style evaluation would earn its place.

## Scenario and method

**Shock.** Wholesale gas prices triple over about eight weeks because of a supply disruption abroad. Four channels follow:

1. household heating and electricity bills rise within weeks;
2. nitrogen fertilizer, made from gas, rises, and food prices follow over months;
3. Hearts Community Providers face higher fiat input costs;
4. import-dependent regions cannot afford fertilizer, so the next harvest falls and hunger rises.

Energy suppliers and traders take windfall profits along the way.

**Method.** Each function a response needs was traced to the framework that would own it, using current versions in the project. Each function is labelled:

- **Owned:** a framework holds the function and has specified it.
- **Partial:** a framework holds part of it, or holds it without the speed or scope the shock needs.
- **Referred, no receiver:** a framework recognizes the problem and refers it out, but names no competent recipient. In GMEAIA terms the handoff would land as `misrouted` or stay at `offered`.
- **Unowned:** no framework holds or refers it. In GGF terms this is a constitutional gap, though national authorities may hold the function in practice.

The trace checks what the documents say. It does not test whether the mechanisms would work.

## Stage-by-stage trace

### 1. Detection (weeks 0–2)

There are plenty of sensors but no one owns a state estimate that links energy to fertilizer to food.

- AUBI §5.1 lists "inflation monitoring" as a Layer 1 safeguard. The §5.1.2 Material Security Estimate tests effective access, but runs only before the baseline or the fiat/Hearts ratio changes.
- Housing §10.1 observes energy and utility burden. It owns no instrument.
- Nested Economies Appendix E.2 tracks material access and supply vulnerability, but only in BAZs that adopt the dashboard.
- DRR's System Dependency and Cascade Map (Appendix G) is the closest tool. DRR §4.1 covers "supply-chain disruption", but a price spike with no physical shortage is not clearly a hazard.
- Climate & Energy v2.1 says nothing about energy prices or household affordability.

### 2. Household protection (first weeks)

This is where the shock lands first, and the energy side is empty.

- **Heating and power.** Climate & Energy has no rules against disconnection, no lifeline tariff and no affordability floor. Its own tools (carbon pricing, ending fossil subsidies) raise fossil prices with no household protection attached. Conduit says it "does not set energy policy" and points to Climate & Energy. Compare Water §5.3: "Essential water and sanitation shall not be disconnected for inability to pay."
- **Income.** AUBI Layer 1 keeps paying. Utilities make up 15% of its regional index, but no cadence is set for raising the amount mid-shock. §5.1.3 controls reductions carefully; increases have no equivalent.
- **Hearts share.** Gas bills are fiat-only, so the 30% Hearts share loses real value. §5.1.2 makes a higher Hearts share inadmissible when it reduces practical freedom, but moving the ratio toward fiat goes through the published decision process in §4.5. That process has no fast path.
- **Legacy stress test.** AUBI §6.3 still reads "Inflation (>10%): Shift to baseline-only, deploy P4 reserves." Read literally, that cuts Layers 2 and 3 when they are most needed. It conflicts with §5.1.4, which keeps Layer 3 running during real shortages.
- **Anticipatory finance.** DRR §10 and §23.2 let a fiscal authority pre-authorize household surges with any trigger the instrument defines. A price-indicator trigger fits the text but is nowhere named.

### 3. Price transmission and windfalls (months 1–6)

Stabilizing essential prices has no owner. Windfall capture does.

- **Staples basket stabilization** (the Mexico-style agreement Weber cites): no framework holds it.
- **Predatory pricing.** AUBI §5.1.4 names "predatory pricing" as a distinct state that needs its own remedy. It refers to "service, care, housing, food, public-health, or other responsible" authorities, never a competition authority. Polycentric §12.1.1 explicitly does not "create competition-policy jurisdiction". Kinship Garden's dependency review covers seeds only.
- **Windfalls.** Adaptive Tax §5.2 lists "excess returns" and "sector-specific bases" among corporate instrument families. A jurisdiction can design a windfall levy there.

### 4. Fertilizer and the next season (months 3–12)

The GGF removes its own friction but provides no bridge supply.

- Gaian Trade's Food Sovereignty Circuit Breaker suspends tariffs on staples and "essential fertilizer inputs" in import-dependent regions. Those are GGF's own Planetary Boundary Tariffs, and Adaptive Tax §20.4 still lists the breaker for review.
- The Global Strategic Resource Reserve holds "grain, seeds, water, medical supplies, minerals" with "needs-based allocation". It holds no fertilizer or energy and has no price-band release rule.
- Kinship Garden's answer to energy price shocks is long-run: "renewable energy integration and localized supply chains". It has no short-term input bridge.
- **Recurring hunger.** This is the one stage with a forcing mechanism. CIS §7.7 covers serious harm that is "ongoing or recurring". If price-driven malnutrition deaths meet its criteria, the record cannot go dormant: it carries a cost-of-delay estimate and re-escalates every cycle. Treaty Art. 18.8 lets Parties fund a Standing Rescue Window, but it exists only if they authorize it.

### 5. Hearts economy

This is handled well procedurally, but it shows the Hearts layer passes the shock through.

- Community Providers redeem Hearts for "external operating costs" (Financial Systems §8.3). Higher gas and fertilizer costs mean more redemption.
- §8.4 correctly lists "external-input dependence" as a cause and says high redemption is "not proof of inflation". §8.5–8.6 give liquidity facilities and put essential providers first.
- §10.1 crisis tools need a "formally declared crisis". A price shock may not meet the CIS §13.1 emergency definition.

### 6. Coordination

Coordination exists only as convening, and the energy framework points to a body that no longer works that way.

- Meta-Governance §13.1: a council may convene within 24 hours; "convening is not command authority". It needs a declared emergency.
- Climate & Energy §11.3 says climate emergencies "trigger the Crisis Command Protocol of the Meta-Governance Framework". Meta-Governance v1.6.3 has no such protocol.
- Climate & Energy also names Hearthstone as the builder of community energy cooperatives. Hearthstone v2.0.3 is a stewardship-transition protocol.

### 7. Structural response (years)

The long run is well covered: the Kinship Garden post-petroleum transition, Climate & Energy's 80% community-owned energy target, the Sundown phase-out, GSCL localization and reserves, and Bioregional Polis land tools. Still missing are public options for essentials, buffer stocks with a price mandate, and concentration review beyond seeds.

## Findings

### Orphans

1. **Household energy.** Water has non-disconnection and lifeline tariffs. Energy, the sector where this shock starts, has neither. Climate & Energy raises fossil prices by design, with no consumer-side counterpart.
2. **Price stabilization of essentials.** Nothing holds staple-basket agreements, buffer stocks that buy low and sell high, or public options. This is deliberate: frameworks avoid owning instruments, and Housing calls affordability a "monitored distributed outcome". But no instrument owner on Housing's list stabilizes prices.
3. **Market power.** AUBI recognizes predatory pricing and has nowhere to send it. Kinship Garden shows the right pattern (a functional-dependency review that refers to competent competition authorities), but only for seeds. Weber's chokepoints (fertilizer, processing, commodity trading, retail) fall outside it.

### Inconsistencies

- **Climate & Energy v2.1** predates the conformance passes. It still carries enforcement tools, a Climate Emergency Clause with emergency powers, a Crisis Command Protocol that Meta-Governance no longer has, and a Hearthstone role Hearthstone does not play.
- **AUBI §6.3** "Shift to baseline-only" on inflation above 10% conflicts with §5.1.4 and with the spirit of §5.1.3.

### The challenge to Preventing 1933

*Preventing 1933* lists Hearts under "Economic Redundancy" to "decouple survival from speculation". The trace shows that holds only for essentials made locally without fiat inputs. Heating gas and nitrogen fertilizer are bought in fiat. Providers who need them redeem Hearts, so the Treasury absorbs the shock as redemption pressure. Financial Systems handles this soundly, but this is liquidity management, not insulation. The paper's claim should be narrowed, or tied to a measure of how much of each essential is produced locally without fiat inputs.

### What already works

- Financial Systems §8 tells external-input shocks apart from inflation and puts essential providers first in queues.
- Adaptive Tax can host a windfall levy without new architecture.
- DRR forecast finance can carry price-triggered household surges once a fiscal instrument defines the trigger.
- CIS §7.7 with the GMEAIA qualifier stops recurring, price-driven hunger from going silent. It is the corpus's clearest answer to Weber's point that each shock turns into hunger in the Global South.

## Recommended changes

Five patches and one design decision, in order of value for effort.

1. **Climate & Energy v2.2 conformance patch.** Add a household energy continuity section modelled on Water §5.1–5.3: essential minimum, lifeline tariff options and no disconnection for inability to pay. Remove the Crisis Command Protocol reference, recast the emergency clause under CIS §13, and fix the Hearthstone role.
2. **AUBI patch.** Add an out-of-cycle upward recalculation when essential index components move past a published threshold, and a fast path for shifting the ratio toward fiat when fiat-only essentials spike. Replace the §6.3 inflation line with one consistent with §5.1.4.
3. **Name the market-power receiver.** Add a GMEAIA-59 referral profile from AUBI §5.1.4 to the competent competition or price authority. Generalize Kinship Garden's dependency review into a shared template other sectors can adopt (fertilizer, food processing, retail, energy supply). It stays referral-only, with no competition jurisdiction.
4. **DRR clarification.** State that essential-price shocks without physical shortage are in scope for forecast finance instruments, and that price indicators are admissible triggers.
5. **Narrow the Hearts claim in *Preventing 1933*** as described under Findings.

**The design decision: where price stabilization lives.** Three options:

- extend the Global Strategic Resource Reserve in Gaian Trade with a price-band release rule and add fertilizer and energy;
- write a narrow interface specification for stabilizing essentials, in the style of ECRC or GMEAIA. It would name the functions (basket agreements, buffer stocks, public options, procurement standards) and route each to competent authorities without creating authority;
- leave stabilization entirely to external law and only name the receivers.

The interface option fits the corpus's style best. It is also where a Democratic Essentials Resilience evaluation belongs: as the criteria for choosing between candidate mechanisms, not as a new framework.
