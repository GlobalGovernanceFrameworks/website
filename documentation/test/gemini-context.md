# The Digital Commons Framework: The Synthesis & Stewardship Protocol (v1.0)

**Tier:** 3 (Cultural & Knowledge Commons)
**Status:** Draft Architecture

## 1. Preamble: The Lineage of Human Synthesis
For the past century, human knowledge has been trapped between two destructive paradigms: the hyper-enclosure of Intellectual Property (which treats knowledge as an extractive commodity) and the context-collapse of the early open internet (which treats knowledge as a frictionless, authorless void). 

The Digital Commons Framework rejects both. We define the digital commons not merely as a repository of free data, but as **Generative Heritage**—a living, curated, and rigorously maintained ecosystem of human synthesis. Knowledge requires maintenance; culture requires context. This framework establishes the operational protocols to ensure that the sum of human intellectual, technical, and cultural output is protected from private enclosure, sustainably maintained by its contributors, and protected from context-collapse. We acknowledge that not all knowledge *should* be digitized, and this framework explicitly defends the right to illegibility for tacit and sacred traditions.

## 2. Ecosystem Positioning & Dependencies
To clarify the architectural boundary: The **Aurora Accord (Tier 1)** governs the *raw material and sovereign boundaries* of data (e.g., "Who has the right to access my biometric signature?"). The **Digital Commons Framework (Tier 3)** governs the *collaborative architecture of public-domain synthesis* (e.g., "How do we collaboratively maintain the open-source blueprint for a bioregional water filtration system?"). 

**Relationship Map:**
* **Depends on the Aurora Accord (Tier 1):** For Cryptographic Provenance. The Digital Commons relies on Aurora’s decentralized identifiers to verify the consent and origin of contributed knowledge, ensuring corporations cannot launder stolen IP into the Commons.
* **Depends on the Synoptic Protocol (Tier 2):** For Epistemic Triage. The Commons relies on Synoptic’s "Public Epistemic Institutions" to flag malicious fabrications (e.g., synthetic biology hazards) before they are merged into the core Generative Heritage ledgers.
* **Enables The Capacity Engine (Tier 2):** By providing the open-access curricula, codebase, and cultural archives that the Capacity Engine uses to generate "Competency Passports" for global citizens. 
* **Enables the Phoenix Protocol (Tier 2):** By hosting the open-source Material Passports and circular-economy blueprints required for local manufacturing.

## 3. Core Governance Mechanisms

### Mechanism A: The Commons Reciprocity License (CRL) & The Extraction Toll
The Commons cannot survive if it is merely a free R&D department for legacy capitalist extraction. The CRL is a self-executing legal and cryptographic wrapper applied to all Tier 3 Commons assets (code, blueprints, cultural media).
* **Operational Detail:** If a Commons asset is utilized by an entity operating within the Commons network (e.g., a local Bioregional Autonomous Zone), access is entirely free. However, if the asset is utilized by a transitional, profit-seeking legacy corporation, the CRL’s smart contract triggers a mandatory **Extraction Toll**. This is a fractional royalty automatically routed to the **Maintenance Guilds** (see Mechanism B) and the originators of the work. It weaponizes legacy market mechanics to fund the post-market Commons.

### Mechanism B: Maintenance Guilds & The "Care-Work" Bounties
Open-source ecosystems historically fail due to the "Tragedy of the Maintainer"—creators are celebrated, but maintainers burn out. The Digital Commons treats digital maintenance as vital planetary care-work.
* **Operational Detail:** The framework establishes **Maintenance Guilds**, funded by the CRL Extraction Tolls and the overarching AUBI (Adaptive Universal Basic Income) framework. Guild membership is determined by a hybrid of sortition (random selection among qualified peers) and verifiable metrics from the Capacity Engine. Maintainers are compensated for debugging, translating, archiving, and contextualizing data. *Known Vulnerability:* We lack a perfect metric for evaluating the "quality" of maintenance; we will initially rely on peer-slashing mechanisms to prevent bounty-farming, but this remains an untested vulnerability at a planetary scale.

### Mechanism C: The Protocol of Sovereign Erasure (The Right to be Forgotten at Scale)
The Commons must not become a permanent trap for outdated or culturally harmful synthesis. 
* **Operational Detail:** While the ledger is immutable for provenance, the *access layer* is highly mutable. If a contributor, or a recognized community (e.g., an Indigenous council), determines that their contributed synthesis is causing harm or was extracted without deep consent, they can trigger a **Sovereign Erasure**. This does not delete the data from cold storage (preserving historical reality), but cryptographically severs all active access keys and APIs in the public layer, rendering the data functionally inert.

## 4. The Core Tension: Universal Openness vs. Contextual Collapse
**The Paradox:** The hacker/open-source ethos demands that *information wants to be free* and instantly accessible to all. However, many forms of knowledge (traditional ecological practices, powerful synthetic biology models, sacred cultural narratives) become actively dangerous or culturally destructive when stripped of their initiating context and made universally frictionless. 
**The False Resolution:** Putting a paywall or a centralized bureaucratic gatekeeper in front of the knowledge. 

**The Structural Hold (The Contextual Friction Protocol):**
The framework holds this tension through the mechanism of **Contextual Friction** (or "Warm Enclosures"). Information in the Commons is not restricted by *wealth* or *class*, but by *demonstrated context*. 
To access highly sensitive or potent cultural/technical nodes within the Commons, a user cannot simply click "download." They must present a cryptographic proof (generated via the Capacity Engine) that they have completed the necessary onboarding. 
*Example:* An open-source blueprint for a high-yield, localized CRISPR gene-synthesis tool is free to the world. However, the download is locked behind a Contextual Friction Gate. The user must provide a Competency Passport showing they have completed the Synoptic Protocol's bio-ethics and safety curriculum. The knowledge remains an un-enclosed Commons, but the *velocity of access* is intentionally slowed to match the *velocity of wisdom*.

## 5. Success Metrics (KPIs)
To ensure the framework is functioning as an evolving ecosystem rather than a stagnant archive, we will track:

1.  **The Maintenance-to-Creation Ratio:** Measures the financial and temporal resources allocated to maintaining existing digital commons versus creating new ones. A healthy system targets a 1.5:1 ratio, prioritizing the health of the existing heritage.
2.  **Contextual Bypass Attempts:** Tracking the number of times legacy entities attempt to scrape Contextual Friction nodes without the required Competency Passports. High rates indicate a need to upgrade Aurora Accord cryptographic wrappers.
3.  **Reciprocity Flow Rate (RFR):** The total volume of capital successfully extracted from legacy commercial entities via the CRL and redistributed to the Maintenance Guilds and local BAZs.
4.  **Epistemic Fork Resolution Time:** When a factual or structural dispute arises in a core Commons repository (a "fork"), this measures the time it takes for the Synoptic Protocol's sortition councils to mediate a functional synthesis or a peaceful divergence.
