# The Canopy Protocol
*Digital Knowledge Commons for Right Relationship*

**Draft Outline v0.1 — designed for the GGF ecosystem, not implementation-ready**

---

## Naming Rationale

"Canopy" rather than "Digital Commons" because the framework functions like a forest canopy: a shared emergent layer above individual knowledge holders (trees) that creates ecosystem conditions for everything beneath it. Canopies filter, shelter, connect — and they're never static. They have gaps, and the gaps matter. The name creates productive friction: *what does a forest canopy have to do with digital knowledge?* — which is exactly the question that opens up the framework's logic.

Key terminology proposals:
- **Canopy License** (tiered knowledge-sharing protocol)
- **Compost Protocol** (knowledge retirement and transformation)
- **Pollination Bridges** (cross-domain knowledge exchange mechanisms)
- **Understory** (local knowledge layers within BAZs)
- **Root Access** (community-governed knowledge that doesn't circulate freely — deliberately echoing and subverting the computing term)

## Ecosystem Position

**Tier 3**, Cultural & Knowledge Commons cluster.

### Depends on:
- **Aurora Accord** (Tier 1) for data governance infrastructure. The Canopy Protocol governs *knowledge* — structured, contextualized, meaning-bearing — while Aurora governs *data* — the raw substrate. The boundary is genuinely blurry (see Tensions section) but the operating distinction is: if you're asking "who can access this?" you're in Aurora territory; if you're asking "how should this be understood, preserved, challenged, or shared?" you're in Canopy territory.
- **Education Framework** (Tier 2) for knowledge literacy. The Canopy Protocol provides the commons infrastructure; the Education Framework develops the capacity to participate in it. Without knowledge literacy, open archives become extraction opportunities.
- **TGIF / Technology OS** (Tier 1) for technical infrastructure standards. The Canopy Protocol specifies *what* the knowledge commons should do; TGIF constrains *how* it's built.
- **Indigenous & Traditional Knowledge Framework** (Tier 1, Ethical OS) for TEK stewardship protocols. The Canopy Protocol doesn't govern Indigenous knowledge — it provides an interface layer that respects Indigenous governance while enabling voluntary participation. The Indigenous Framework has structural primacy here via the Earth Council's veto power.

### Enables:
- **Cultural Heritage Preservation** — uses Canopy archives and the Compost Protocol to maintain living cultural records
- **Arts Flourishing** — Canopy License provides alternatives to extractive IP regimes
- **Religious & Spiritual Dialogue** — Wisdom Repository is hosted on Canopy infrastructure with Root Access protections for sacred knowledge
- **Consciousness Development** (Tier 4) — the Canopy Protocol's epistemic pluralism infrastructure supports developmental diversity without flattening it
- **Inner Development & Leadership Protocol** — draws on curated knowledge commons for developmental resources

### Coordinates with:
- **Synoptic Protocol** on information integrity — but with a critical caveat addressed in the Tensions section
- **Hearthstone Protocol** on intangible asset stewardship — the Canopy License and Hearthstone's ownership→stewardship transition must be mutually legible
- **Sacred Data Protocol** — Root Access tier integrates with Sacred Data protections
- **Nested Economies** — knowledge contributions are recognizable via Love Ledger and can generate Hearts through Proof of Care validation

## Core Governance Mechanisms

### 1. Knowledge Stewardship Circles (KSCs)

Domain-specific rotating bodies responsible for knowledge curation within the Canopy. Not standing committees — rotating circles with structural role diversity.

**Composition per circle (5-7 members, 18-month rotating terms):**
- At least one Indigenous knowledge keeper (nominated by Earth Council or regional Indigenous governance)
- At least one domain practitioner (not academic — someone who *uses* the knowledge)
- One archivist/librarian (preservation expertise)
- One designated *Questioner* — a role explicitly tasked with challenging the circle's assumptions and identifying blind spots
- Remaining seats filled through BAZ-level sortition from qualified pools

**Decision-making:** Consent-based, not consensus. Proposals pass unless someone has a *reasoned, articulable objection* — not discomfort, not preference, but a specific claim about harm or incoherence. This matters because consensus-based knowledge governance tends to either deadlock or produce lowest-common-denominator decisions.

**Scope:** KSCs govern curation, classification, and Canopy License tier assignment. They do NOT govern access (Aurora's domain) or infrastructure (TGIF's domain). A KSC might decide that a body of traditional agricultural knowledge should be classified as "Root Access" at the request of its originating community — but the technical access controls are implemented through Aurora infrastructure.

**Accountability:** KSCs report to the Digital Commons Council (DCC), which is itself accountable to the MGCC. Earth Council holds veto power over any KSC decision affecting Indigenous knowledge.

*Uncertainty: I'm not confident the 5-7 member size is right. Larger circles might be needed for high-stakes domains. The 18-month rotation might be too fast for domains requiring deep contextual knowledge — but too slow for rapidly evolving fields. This needs domain-specific calibration that I can't do from here.*

### 2. The Canopy License (Tiered Knowledge Sharing)

A licensing framework that moves beyond the open/closed binary. Four tiers, assigned by KSCs in consultation with knowledge originators:

**Open Canopy** — Fully open. Attribution required. Non-extraction clause: knowledge can be used, built upon, and redistributed, but cannot be enclosed (patented, paywalled, or made proprietary). Analogous to copyleft but for knowledge broadly.

**Filtered Canopy** — Open with contextual requirements. Knowledge is accessible, but comes with mandatory contextual metadata: where it came from, what epistemic tradition it belongs to, known limitations, and competing perspectives. The "filter" is context, not restriction. Aims to prevent decontextualized extraction.

**Root Access** — Knowledge registered in the Canopy but access governed entirely by the originating community's own protocols. The Canopy Protocol provides the *interface* (discoverability, metadata, request mechanisms) but the originating community controls the *gate*. This is where Indigenous knowledge, sacred knowledge, and initiatory traditions can participate in the commons without being absorbed by it.

**Composted** — See mechanism #3. Knowledge that has been retired from active circulation but preserved with full context.

**Enforcement:** Canopy License violations are adjudicable through the Digital Justice Tribunal. Hearts-based penalties (not punitive fines, but redistribution requirements) for extraction from Root Access knowledge.

*Uncertainty: The boundary between "Filtered Canopy" and "Open Canopy" may be too soft to enforce. Who decides what "mandatory contextual metadata" is sufficient? This likely needs more operational specification than I can provide. Also: the Canopy License interacts with existing legal IP regimes globally — how it interfaces with copyright, patent law, and trade agreements is a massive question I'm not equipped to resolve.*

### 3. Compost Protocol (Knowledge Retirement & Transformation)

Knowledge that becomes outdated, superseded, harmful, or contextually inappropriate is not deleted. It is composted — moved to a distinct archival layer with:

- Annotation explaining *why* it was retired (with the retiring KSC's reasoning preserved)
- Cross-references to superseding knowledge
- The original knowledge preserved intact (no retrospective editing)
- A "decomposition timeline" — composted knowledge is reviewed periodically (suggested: every 5 years) to determine if it has value as historical record, cautionary example, or raw material for new synthesis

**Why this matters:** Deleting knowledge creates epistemic holes. But leaving outdated or harmful knowledge in active circulation without annotation is worse. The Compost Protocol creates a structural middle ground — knowledge has a lifecycle, and "death" is part of that lifecycle, but death in a compost heap is generative, not final.

**Censorship safeguard:** Any composting decision can be challenged through the DCC. The Questioner role in KSCs exists partly to prevent politically motivated composting. Composted knowledge remains accessible (not hidden) — it's the active circulation status that changes, not the existence.

*Uncertainty: This mechanism has obvious censorship risk despite the safeguards. "Outdated" and "harmful" are not neutral categories — they're sites of power. The safeguards proposed here (challenge mechanisms, Questioner role, preservation of original) are necessary but possibly insufficient. This is probably the mechanism most in need of adversarial stress-testing.*

### 4. Pollination Bridges (Cross-Domain Knowledge Exchange)

Formalized mechanisms for knowledge exchange between BAZs and between knowledge domains, with built-in friction to prevent extractive knowledge tourism.

**Structure:** A Pollination Bridge is a time-limited, purpose-specific exchange agreement between two or more KSCs or BAZ-level Understory nodes. It specifies:
- What knowledge domains are being connected
- What translation protocols are needed (linguistic, epistemological, contextual)
- What reciprocity is expected (not necessarily symmetrical — but explicitly articulated)
- Duration and review points
- How contributions are recognized (Love Ledger entries, Hearts allocation)

**Built-in friction:** Bridges require a "translation layer" — not just linguistic translation but epistemological translation. If a bioregional ecological knowledge system is being bridged with a quantitative environmental science knowledge base, someone has to do the work of articulating where these systems are commensurable and where they aren't. This work is compensated through AUBI Layer 2 (Community Weaver equivalent role).

*Uncertainty: I'm not sure whether Pollination Bridges should be governed at the DCC level or the BAZ level. BAZ-level governance preserves local autonomy but risks inconsistency. DCC-level governance ensures coherence but may be too slow for practical knowledge exchange needs.*

## The Central Tension: Epistemic Pluralism vs. Epistemic Integrity

The Canopy Protocol must simultaneously serve:

1. **The Synoptic Protocol's** concern for information integrity — the idea that there are better and worse claims about reality, and that knowledge commons should not become "everything is equally valid" relativism.

2. **The Indigenous Framework's** insistence that some knowledge systems operate with fundamentally different epistemologies that cannot be adjudicated by Western scientific standards without doing violence to them.

This is not a tension that can be resolved. It can only be held.

**How the framework holds it structurally:**

The Canopy License's tier system doesn't place knowledge on a single epistemic hierarchy. Root Access knowledge isn't "lower quality" than Open Canopy knowledge — it's knowledge that operates under different circulation norms. The tiering is about *circulation governance*, not *epistemic status*.

But this creates a second-order problem: if the Canopy Protocol doesn't make epistemic quality distinctions, how does it interface with the Synoptic Protocol, which exists precisely to maintain epistemic integrity? 

**Proposed structural approach:** The Synoptic Protocol applies *within* epistemic traditions, not *between* them. Claims made within a scientific framework can be evaluated for information integrity by scientific standards. Claims made within an Indigenous knowledge system are evaluated by that system's own integrity standards (which the Indigenous Framework governs). The Canopy Protocol provides the meta-infrastructure that keeps these systems legible to each other without forcing commensurability.

**Where this gets uncomfortable:** What happens when knowledge systems make *competing claims about the same material reality*? An Indigenous ecological knowledge system and a quantitative ecology model might disagree about land management. The Canopy Protocol cannot avoid this — and the honest answer is that the framework proposes *process* (Pollination Bridges, KSC dialogue with mandatory Questioner roles) rather than *resolution*. The MOS's principle of Cognitive Pluralism supports this, but it doesn't eliminate the difficulty.

**What I'm not resolving:** Whether this structural approach is actually *sufficient* or whether it just defers the problem. There's a version of epistemic pluralism that becomes epistemic paralysis — the framework must be tested against that failure mode. I also suspect the Synoptic Protocol's architects might have a different view of where its jurisdiction ends, and that disagreement would need to be navigated at the MGCC level.

## What's Missing and Where I'm Uncertain

1. **Technical architecture is entirely absent.** I've proposed governance mechanisms without specifying what the actual infrastructure looks like — decentralized? Federated? Blockchain-anchored? This isn't just an implementation detail; infrastructure choices *encode* governance choices. A decentralized architecture makes censorship harder but standards enforcement harder. A federated architecture preserves autonomy but creates interoperability burdens. This needs technical expertise I don't have.

2. **The relationship with AI-generated knowledge is unaddressed.** Aurora Accord covers AI training data, but the Canopy Protocol needs a position on whether AI-generated knowledge can be contributed to the commons, and under what conditions. This intersects with the Technology OS and with increasingly urgent questions about epistemic provenance.

3. **Scale and resource requirements are unspecified.** KSCs across potentially hundreds of knowledge domains, Pollination Bridges requiring compensated translation work, a Compost Protocol requiring periodic review — this is labor-intensive. I haven't addressed who pays for this or how it's resourced within the AUBI/Hearts economy.

4. **The Canopy License's legal status is unclear.** It proposes to operate alongside existing IP regimes but doesn't specify how. This is probably a multi-year legal design challenge requiring international law expertise.

5. **I may be overcorrecting away from the Aurora Accord's territory** and leaving gaps in the boundary layer between data governance and knowledge governance. The distinction is conceptually useful but practically messy — knowledge is encoded in data, and data governance decisions constrain knowledge governance possibilities.

6. **The "Spiral-Aware" communication approach** mentioned in the GGF entities should probably inform how the Canopy Protocol interfaces with communities at different developmental stages, but I haven't operationalized this. A Spiral-Aware knowledge commons would present and structure knowledge differently depending on developmental context — this is a significant design challenge I've only gestured at.

7. **I'm uncertain whether the Compost Protocol would actually survive contact with political reality.** The incentive to politically weaponize knowledge retirement is high, and the safeguards I've proposed are thin.
