## Governance mechanisms

### 1. Digital Commons Council
A **Digital Commons Council (DCC)** would set operational policy for the commons layer, including access tiers, archival standards, citation norms, redaction rules, and dispute escalation paths. It should not own the content outright; instead it would coordinate stewarding bodies and be constrained by Indigenous authority where TEK or sacred material is implicated. ### 3. Tiered access and stewardship protocol
A **three- or four-tier knowledge access protocol** would distinguish open cultural material, community-governed material, restricted heritage material, and sacred/closed material. This is structurally important because it lets the framework hold openness and sovereignty at the same time instead of pretending everything should be equally open. ## Structural tension

The central tension is **openness vs. Indigenous and community sovereignty**. The framework should not solve this by saying “open by default,” because that would erase legitimate restrictions, but it also should not make everything closed, because the commons would then cease to be commons. Structurally, the answer is a layered stewardship model with explicit veto or restriction powers for Indigenous and custodial authorities, paired with clearly defined public layers that remain open for cultural learning, repair, and transmission. ## How it differs from Aurora Accord

Aurora Accord governs **data**: consent, sovereignty, portability, fiduciary handling, and digital dignity. The Digital Commons governs **shared meaning infrastructure**: archives, repositories, cultural memory, epistemic framing, and stewardship of knowledge as a living commons. In other words, Aurora says who may handle data and under what terms, while Digital Commons asks how shared knowledge is preserved, curated, contextualized, and transmitted across generations.
