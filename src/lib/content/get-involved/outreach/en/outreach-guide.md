## Read this part first

If you talk about Global Governance Frameworks to other people, you will be representing it. So the most important thing in this guide isn't a talking point — it's an accurate picture of what you'd be representing.

GGF is one person's project. There is no organization, no staff, no board, no funding, and no institutional backing. The frameworks are extensive and carefully argued, but they have almost no external adoption. A few outreach conversations have happened; one municipal pilot is being explored. That is the whole picture.

This matters practically. If you describe the project as an initiative, a network, or a team, and someone looks into it, the gap between your description and reality does more damage than saying nothing would have. Accurate description costs nothing and survives scrutiny. Overselling costs credibility that is very hard to get back.

So: describe it as a body of work, not an institution. "Someone has written a detailed proposal for X, and I found it interesting" is both true and stronger than it sounds.

## The core message

Start with the problem, then what the work actually is.

> The world faces interconnected crises — climate, pandemics, economic instability — that governance systems designed in the twentieth century handle badly. Global Governance Frameworks is an attempt to write the open-source infrastructure that would let different governance systems coordinate without any of them being replaced. It's a proposal, published openly, and it's looking for people to argue with it and test it.

The last sentence matters as much as the first two. The project is asking for scrutiny, not agreement, and framing it that way is both honest and more likely to interest serious people.

**What it is not:** a world government proposal. This is the most common misreading and worth pre-empting. The design is explicitly polycentric — it's about coordination between systems, with decisions pushed to the most local level capable of making them.

## Adjusting for your audience

**Friends, family, general public.** Lead with the "why". Shared values, a livable future, the sense that existing systems aren't coping. Keep it concrete — pandemic response, climate adaptation, whether ordinary people have any say in decisions made above the national level. Don't reach for the framework vocabulary.

**Policy professionals, academics, NGO workers.** Use the precise language, because they'll recognize it. Subsidiarity, polycentric governance, the five-pillar structure of the Treaty for Our Only Home. This audience will also spot over-claiming faster than anyone, so the honest framing matters most here. They're likely to be the most useful critics you can find.

**Business and finance.** Frame it as risk. Instability is expensive; coordination failure is a cost that shows up on balance sheets eventually. The relevant concept is sovereignty bargains — pooling limited authority on genuinely global problems in exchange for predictability. Be ready for scepticism and don't argue it away; it's warranted at this stage.

**Local and regional government.** Probably the most promising audience, because bioregional and municipal-scale proposals are the parts of this work that could actually be tried by someone with the authority to try them. Lead with what's implementable now rather than with the planetary architecture.

## Handling the hard questions

**"Is this a world government?"**
No — close to the opposite. It's designed to strengthen local and regional governance while enabling cooperation on problems that genuinely cross borders. Pooling authority on pandemics doesn't mean pooling it on everything.

**"Is this realistic, or utopian?"**
Both, honestly, depending on which part. The near-term proposals draw on things that already exist — pooled sovereignty in the EU, existing treaty mechanisms. The long-horizon material is speculative and doesn't pretend otherwise. It's fair to say which parts you think are which.

**"Who's behind this?"**
One person, working with AI systems for drafting and critique, publishing openly. Say it plainly. It's an unusual answer and people generally respect it more than a vague one.

**"Has anyone actually implemented this?"**
No. Not yet. Don't soften this — an unimplemented proposal that admits it is far more credible than one that implies otherwise.

## Ways to contribute

**Share and discuss.** Post about the parts you find interesting, with your own commentary about why. Personal framing beats promotional framing every time. Write critiques — a well-argued objection is more useful to the project than agreement, and more interesting to read.

**Explain.** Write threads, summaries, or explainers that translate a framework into plain language for a specific audience. The documents are long, and shorter routes into them are genuinely missing.

**Present and convene.** Bring a framework to a reading group, a university seminar, a civil society meeting, a local council. Small rooms with the right people beat large audiences.

**Connect.** If you know someone whose work this is relevant to, an introduction is worth more than a hundred shares.

**Visual work.** Diagrams, infographics, and video explainers for concepts that are currently only prose. This is a real gap.

## Tone

Hopeful but pragmatic. Collaborative rather than dogmatic — this is a living proposal, not a doctrine. Confident about the ideas, humble about the project's current standing. Invite disagreement; it's the fastest route to work that holds up.

And avoid movement language. Words like "join the movement" describe something that doesn't exist yet and make the whole thing sound less serious than it is.

## Materials and getting help

Framework documents, the blog, and downloadable summaries are all on the site and openly licensed — use, adapt, and rework them freely. There's no approval process and no brand police.

Discord is the place to check a claim before you make it publicly, ask which framing works for a particular audience, or say that something you tried didn't land. That last one is especially useful, and there's no one else currently collecting it.
