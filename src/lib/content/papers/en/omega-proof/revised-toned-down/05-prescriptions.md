# 5. The Prescription: Derived Architectural Requirements

## Deriving Requirements from Diagnosed Constraints

The diagnostic analysis in Section 4 identified four structural constraints that appear to prevent the current international system from processing a certain class of coordination-intensive solutions. This section performs the inverse operation: deriving the **minimum viable capabilities** that any successor architecture would need to possess to overcome these constraints.

### Design Philosophy: From Constraints to Capabilities

The four requirements presented below are not political preferences or ideological commitments. They are derived through **first-principles reasoning** from the failure data of the Omega simulation. The logic is straightforward: if the system encountered an unprocessable instruction when asked to perform function X, then any viable alternative must possess capability Y that enables function X.

This approach mirrors fault-tolerant engineering: when a component fails under a specific stress test, the system must be redesigned with new capabilities—redundancy, monitoring, automatic failovers—that make that specific failure mode avoidable. The four requirements are those **new capabilities** for the planet-scale coordination system.

We then examine one proposed implementation—the Global Governance Frameworks (GGF)—as a **candidate architecture** that attempts to instantiate these capabilities. The GGF is evaluated not as a normative ideal, but as an engineering hypothesis: a concrete example of how the derived requirements might be realized in practice.

## The Four Derived Architectural Capabilities

### Requirement 1: Meta-Sovereign Guarantor Capability

**Derived from:** Constraint 1 (Sovereignty Rigidity)
**The diagnosed limitation:** Binary sovereignty logic makes territorial ambiguity existentially threatening.
**The derived capability:** The system must possess an authority layer above the nation-state that can:
- Credibly enforce temporary, ambiguous sovereignty arrangements.
- Distinguish such arrangements from permanent territorial loss.
- Operate with legitimacy that does not depend solely on the consent of the states involved.

**Minimum specifications for viability:**
- **Legal framework:** Binding authority to administer zones where national sovereignty claims are in conflict.
- **Enforcement mechanism:** Capacity to impose costs on parties who violate transitional arrangements.
- **Legitimacy basis:** Grounded in something more durable than state consent (e.g., ecological integrity, human rights mandates, or a broad global consensus).
- **Operational model:** Must be able to function even when major powers object, requiring distributed enforcement and automatic triggers.

**Implementation considerations:** This capability cannot be achieved through traditional international organizations that operate on unanimous consent. It likely requires either:
- Consensus algorithms that enable action without unanimous approval.
- Ecological or bioregional boundaries that create a natural basis for jurisdictional authority.
- Automatic enforcement mechanisms (economic, technological) that do not depend on the continuous political will of individual states.

**Feasibility Gates** (conditions that would indicate progress toward viability):

1.  **Algorithmic Legitimacy:** A governance algorithm (e.g., delegated proof-of-stake, quadratic voting) achieves greater perceived legitimacy than national veto power among a significant portion of the global population.
2.  **Ecological Jurisdiction:** The concept of "bioregional sovereignty" gains legal recognition in international courts for issues of ecological management.
3.  **Enforcement Independence:** The meta-sovereign authority possesses economic or technological enforcement capability that functions without requiring unanimous Security Council approval.

### Requirement 2: Resource De-Weaponization Protocol

**Derived from:** Constraint 2 (Resource Control)
**The diagnosed limitation:** Strategic resources are treated as sovereign instruments of power, not as shared planetary assets.
**The derived capability:** The system must be able to:
- Convert control over strategic resources from absolute to conditional.
- Create enforceable rules for resource extraction, allocation, and benefit-sharing.
- Make participation in cooperative resource management more advantageous than weaponization.

**Minimum specifications for viability:**
- **Commons governance framework:** Clear rules for managing resources as planetary assets with defined extraction limits.
- **Automated monitoring:** Real-time verification of resource flows that cannot be easily manipulated by national actors.
- **Benefit distribution mechanism:** Transparent allocation of resource value that rewards contribution rather than control.
- **Enforcement capability:** Automatic penalties for weaponization that exceed any plausible strategic gains from defection.

**Implementation considerations:** This capability requires technology that enables:
- Tamper-proof metering of resource flows (e.g., distributed ledger systems).
- Distributed decision-making about resource allocation that is not dominated by resource-rich nations.
- A credible threat of economic isolation for weaponization attempts.

This is fundamentally incompatible with absolute resource sovereignty. Any implementation requires states to accept *conditional* control—they manage resources but do not possess absolute ownership.

**Feasibility Gates:**

1.  **Metering Technology Trust:** Tamper-proof resource tracking technology is deployed and trusted by a critical mass of major resource producers.
2.  **Economic Superiority Proof:** Commons governance demonstrably generates higher resource rents for participating nations than weaponization strategies in pilot implementations.
3.  **Enforcement Coalition:** A coalition representing a substantial portion of global GDP credibly commits to economic isolation of weaponization attempts.

### Requirement 3: Absolute-Gain Optimization Framework

**Derived from:** Constraint 3 (Relative-Gain Dominance)
**The diagnosed limitation:** Great powers optimize for relative advantage over absolute stability.
**The derived capability:** The system must create incentive structures where:
- Cooperation is assessed as more valuable than zero-sum competition, even when rivals also benefit.
- Contributions to planetary stability generate concrete, predictable rewards.
- Blocking coordination generates costs that exceed any competitive advantages gained.

**Minimum specifications for viability:**
- **Metric system:** Measurable indicators of planetary stability contribution (distinct from economic output or military strength).
- **Automatic reward allocation:** Benefits (market access, technology licenses, reconstruction contracts) distributed based on contribution scores.
- **Free-rider penalty mechanism:** Costs automatically imposed on actors who block coordination or profit from systemic instability.
- **Prestige recalibration:** Status and influence derived from solving collective problems rather than zero-sum dominance.

**Implementation considerations:** This requires:
- Global monitoring infrastructure capable of assessing which actors contribute to versus undermine stability.
- Binding mechanisms that allocate scarce goods (technology, finance, legitimacy) based on cooperation metrics.
- A cultural and institutional shift from "power over" to "power to coordinate."

A critical challenge is legitimacy: this framework must be perceived as fair by rising powers who currently benefit from contesting the Western-led order. Legitimacy cannot be derived solely from Western institutions.

**Feasibility Gates:**

1.  **Non-Western Legitimacy:** The measurement methodology must be co-designed and vetted by institutions from multiple non-Western civilizational centers.
2.  **Enforcement Credibility:** At least one major benefit stream (e.g., technology licenses, development lending, market access) is demonstrably allocated based on contribution scores.
3.  **Rising Power Buy-In:** Major rising powers must be net beneficiaries in pilot implementations, demonstrating that the system rewards their actual contributions.

---

### Requirement 4: Anti-Free-Riding Enforcement Mechanism

**Derived from:** Constraint 4 (Free-Rider Incentive Asymmetry)
**The diagnosed limitation:** Actors who profit from instability face insufficient penalties for blocking solutions.
**The derived capability:** The system must be able to:
- Identify which actors benefit from specific instances of planetary instability (conflict, climate disruption, pandemic spread).
- Impose graduated costs on free-riders that exceed the benefits derived from instability.
- Create coalition structures that can act even when beneficiaries of instability object.

**Minimum specifications for viability:**
- **Transparency infrastructure:** Global sensor networks and AI-enabled analysis to track who gains from specific crises.
- **Graduated sanction capability:** Automatic, escalating penalties (e.g., trade restrictions → technology embargoes → financial isolation).
- **Coalition coordination:** Mechanisms for like-minded actors to enforce rules even without universal agreement.
- **Incentive reversal:** Structures that make cooperation with stabilization efforts more profitable than free-riding on chaos.

**Implementation considerations:** This is among the most difficult coordination problems in international relations. It requires:
- Monitoring capabilities that rising powers will likely view as intrusive.
- Enforcement mechanisms that do not depend solely on U.S. or Western financial dominance.
- Credible commitment that penalties will be applied to powerful actors, not just weaker states.

This cannot be implemented through the traditional UN veto structure. It requires new decision-making architecture.

**Feasibility Gates:**

1.  **Distributed Monitoring:** Surveillance infrastructure must be genuinely distributed, with no single nation controlling a dominant share of sensors or data processing.
2.  **Universal Enforcement:** The system must demonstrably apply sanctions to a Western-aligned actor for blocking coordination, proving it is not a tool for great-power competition.
3.  **Coalition Threshold:** A coalition representing a substantial portion of global trade volume commits to enforcing sanctions on identified free-riders, with binding obligations that survive changes in national leadership.

## Mapping to an Existing Proposal: The GGF as a Candidate Implementation

The Global Governance Frameworks (GGF) were developed independently but map directly to these derived requirements. We examine them here not as the only possible solution, but as a **concrete candidate implementation** that demonstrates how the abstract specifications might be realized.

### GGF Component 1: Bioregional Autonomous Zones (BAZ)

**Addresses:** Requirement 1 (Meta-Sovereign Guarantor)

**Proposed mechanism:**
- Territorial governance based on ecological boundaries (watersheds, biomes) rather than ethnic or national claims.
- Citizenship decoupled from state loyalty and anchored in "ecological custody."
- BAZ councils have authority granted by a meta-governance framework, not solely by national governments.
- Residents can hold multiple citizenship types (national, bioregional, or both).

**Assessment as a candidate solution:**
- **Potential strengths:** Grounds authority in ecological realities rather than state consent; provides a mechanism for administering contested zones without requiring one side to surrender sovereignty.
- **Critical dependencies:** Requires broad acceptance that ecological integrity can justify constraints on national sovereignty; needs enforcement capability independent of major power vetoes; must demonstrate it does not become permanent partition under a different name.
- **Feasibility assessment:** Conceptually coherent but faces significant legitimacy challenges. Would likely require demonstration projects in non-conflict zones to prove viability before application to active conflict areas.

---

### GGF Component 2: Global Commons Fund + Conduit Protocol

**Addresses:** Requirement 2 (Resource De-Weaponization)

**Proposed mechanism:**
- Strategic resources (energy, water, minerals) managed as "planetary commons."
- "Conduit Protocol" creates transparent, automated tracking of resource flows.
- Revenue from resource extraction captured via a coordination infrastructure levy.
- Funds redistributed according to planetary need and contribution to stability, not national power.

**Assessment as a candidate solution:**
- **Potential strengths:** Converts resources from sovereign instruments into managed global utilities; automated monitoring reduces opportunities for gaming; economic incentives reward contribution over control.
- **Critical dependencies:** Requires resource-rich nations to accept conditional rather than absolute control; needs tamper-proof metering technology that nations trust; must demonstrate that participants receive more value from fund participation than from weaponization.
- **Feasibility assessment:** Technically feasible but politically challenging. The simulation of Moscow's rejection demonstrates that even economically favorable resource arrangements face resistance when framed as loss of control. Would require pilot programs demonstrating tangible national benefits and governance structures where resource-rich nations have genuine influence.

---

### GGF Component 3: Planetary Stability Index (PSI)

**Addresses:** Requirement 3 (Absolute-Gain Optimization)

**Proposed mechanism:**
- Measurable metric of state contribution to planetary public goods (climate stability, pandemic prevention, conflict resolution).
- Benefits (technology access, market access, reconstruction contracts) allocated based on PSI scores.
- AI-enabled monitoring tracks actual contributions versus rhetorical commitments.
- Replaces power-based international hierarchy with contribution-based status.

**Assessment as a candidate solution:**
- **Potential strengths:** Creates concrete incentives for cooperation independent of relative military or economic position; makes "success" measurable through planetary outcomes; automatically rewards rising powers for climate and development contributions.
- **Critical dependencies:** Requires agreement on what counts as "contribution"; needs perceived legitimacy from both Western and non-Western powers; must have enforcement teeth—low PSI scores must actually affect market access.
- **Feasibility assessment:** Among the more politically viable components, as it offers status and benefits to rising powers. Key vulnerabilities include: risk of gaming (performative contributions for score-boosting), legitimacy of measurement (if controlled by Western institutions), and the enforcement question (who imposes costs for low scores).

---

### GGF Component 4: Transparency & Consequence Protocol (TCP)

**Addresses:** Requirement 4 (Anti-Free-Riding Enforcement)

**Proposed mechanism:**
- Global sensor networks and AI audit systems identify who benefits from specific instabilities.
- Graduated sanctions automatically triggered when free-riding is detected (e.g., blocking peace initiatives while profiting from war economies).
- Coalition enforcement allows like-minded actors to impose penalties without universal agreement.
- "Cognitive immunity protocols" aim to prevent narrative warfare from obscuring who benefits from chaos.

**Assessment as a candidate solution:**
- **Potential strengths:** Makes free-riding visible and costly; automated triggers remove some political discretion; coalition structure allows enforcement even when a major power objects.
- **Critical dependencies:** Requires surveillance infrastructure that some states will view as an intelligence operation; needs credible commitment that penalties apply to all actors; must have an enforcement coalition willing to impose actual economic costs on powerful free-riders.
- **Feasibility assessment:** This is the most challenging component. The simulation of Beijing's rejection was based in part on a preference for opacity regarding who benefits from instability. Implementation would require distributed monitoring infrastructure not controlled by any single nation, algorithmic rather than political determination of sanctions, and clear demonstrations that Western free-riding also triggers penalties.

## The Interdependence of the Requirements

The GGF components—and any alternative architecture aiming to satisfy the four derived requirements—are not separate solutions but an **integrated system**. Each component primarily addresses one constraint while supporting the others:

| Component | Primary Constraint Addressed | Enabling Function for Other Components |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **BAZ** | Sovereignty Rigidity | Creates neutral spaces where commons management and cooperation can occur. |
| **Commons Fund** | Resource Control | Reduces territorial resource conflicts and creates absolute-gain incentives. |
| **PSI** | Relative-Gain Dominance | Rewards contributions to the system and penalizes blocking behavior. |
| **TCP** | Free-Rider Asymmetry | Provides monitoring and enforcement for all other components. |

This systemic integration is critical: implementing only one or two components would likely fail because the constraints reinforce each other. Any viable successor architecture must be similarly comprehensive.

## The Fundamental Tension: Sovereignty vs. Coordination

Every one of these derived requirements shares a common feature: **they condition or constrain national sovereignty in favor of planetary coordination.**

- BAZ councils would override or condition national territorial claims.
- Commons protocols would constrain absolute resource sovereignty.
- A PSI would condition market access on planetary contribution, not national power.
- A TCP would impose costs for blocking coordination, regardless of perceived national interest.

This is not a flaw in the design. It is the logical consequence of the requirements derived from the diagnostic analysis.

**The core finding:** Any architecture capable of processing planetary coordination problems must possess authority that transcends the consent of individual nation-states. The four constraints identified in Section 4 cannot be addressed while preserving absolute, unconditional sovereignty.

This creates a significant political challenge: **states are unlikely to voluntarily adopt systems that constrain their sovereignty until the perceived costs of current-system failure exceed the perceived costs of sovereignty constraints.**

## Transition Pathways: A Brief Note

There are two broad pathways by which a successor architecture might emerge:

**Pathway 1: Voluntary Adoption (Assessed as Low Probability)**
States recognize the structural constraints, accept that sovereignty must become conditional, and voluntarily adopt new frameworks. This would require demonstration projects that prove the new architecture increases national wellbeing and a significant cultural shift in how sovereignty is understood. The simulation results presented in Section 3 suggest this pathway faces substantial barriers.

**Pathway 2: Crisis-Driven Adoption (Assessed as More Likely)**
The current system experiences sufficient stress (protracted conflict, climate disruption, financial instability) that the legitimacy of absolute sovereignty is weakened, and alternative architectures become politically viable. Historical precedent suggests this is the more common pattern for major institutional transitions.

**The implication for builders:** The low probability of voluntary transition does not reduce the importance of developing and demonstrating successor architectures. When a crisis creates an opening for change, the alternatives that are "ready" will have a significant advantage. The work of designing, testing, and refining these architectures is a form of preparation for a transition that may be driven by events rather than choice.
