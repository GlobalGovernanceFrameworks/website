---

**1. Informal and subsistence economies are invisible**

The entire system — Digital Passports, blockchain literacy, API integrations — is built for formalized trade. But a massive share of Global South economic activity is informal: smallholder farmers selling at local markets, artisanal miners, street vendors. If a subsistence farmer in rural Mali can't passport their grain, they're either excluded from the system or pressured into unwanted formalization. Neither outcome is decolonial. You need a "Graduated Legibility Protocol" — a way for informal economies to interface with the system at their own pace, perhaps through cooperative-level aggregation rather than individual product tracking. Community Resource Councils could act as collective passport holders for informal producers.

**2. The system's own ecological footprint is unaccounted for**

Distributed ledgers, AI monitoring, satellite surveillance, Whale-Song Monitoring, open-source TensorFlow models running continuously — this infrastructure has real energy and material costs. The framework governs everyone else's ecological impact but doesn't budget its own. An "Operational Footprint Cap" should ensure the monitoring apparatus never consumes resources disproportionate to the trade it governs. This is especially important because it's a credibility issue — critics will point to it immediately.

**3. Food security vs. planetary boundary tariffs — no override mechanism**

Embodied water tariffs on rice, biodiversity tariffs on fertilizer-dependent agriculture, resource depletion quotas on phosphorus — these could trigger food crises in import-dependent regions. The framework has no explicit food security exemption or emergency override within the tariff system. The Supply Chain Crisis Protocol handles supply shocks but not *policy-induced* scarcity from the framework's own mechanisms. You need a "Food Sovereignty Circuit Breaker" that automatically suspends tariffs on staple goods when food access indicators in a region cross a threshold.

**4. Data colonialism through the passport system itself**

DeepSeek's Digital Commons Trust governs the *code*, but the bigger risk is the *data*. Digital Passports will generate granular, real-time data about every Global South supply chain — production volumes, labor patterns, resource reserves, seasonal cycles. That's an intelligence goldmine. Without explicit data sovereignty provisions, Northern institutions (or the GTRC itself) could harvest this for competitive advantage, reproducing extraction in informational form. The Aurora Accord is referenced but not operationalized here. Each BTRH should own its own data, with aggregated/anonymized feeds going upward — not the reverse.

**5. The right to opacity**

Related but distinct: radical transparency as a universal principle has a James Scott "Seeing Like a State" problem. Indigenous communities may not want their sacred resource management practices legible to a global system. Small communities may not want their production patterns visible. The framework needs a "Right to Opacity" — a principled exception where communities can opt out of full transparency for culturally or strategically sensitive domains, with alternative verification methods (e.g., trusted-third-party attestation rather than full digital exposure).

**6. Transition support is too narrowly scoped**

The Just Transition Fund targets fossil fuel and extractive workers, but the tariff regime will also make industries non-competitive that aren't extractive — a textile factory in Bangladesh that can't afford Digital Passport compliance, a smallholder coffee operation that can't meet circularity scores. The framework's own rules will create losers beyond the extractive sector. The transition plan needs to cover *compliance-gap casualties*, not just fossil fuel workers.

**7. Inter-RTZ coordination costs scale non-linearly**

With 2-3 RTZs, the GTRC can arbitrate everything. With 20+, you'll get conflicting standards, overlapping resource claims, and competing bioregional interpretations. There's no RTZ-to-RTZ bilateral resolution mechanism below the GTRC level. You need a "Lateral Coordination Protocol" — think of it as a peer-resolution layer so not every disagreement escalates to the global council. This connects to Beer's recursion levels in VSM — you're missing a recursion level.

**8. Currency interface with national monetary systems**

The framework assumes Hearts/Leaves integration across RTZs but doesn't address how central banks will respond. Tariffs payable in Hearts, grants in Leaves — this is a parallel monetary system that will trigger sovereignty objections from every finance ministry involved. There's no mechanism for managing the fiat-Hearts exchange interface, no protocol for what happens when a central bank refuses to recognize Hearts-denominated obligations. Gemini's Bancor suggestion is adjacent but doesn't solve this — you need an explicit "Monetary Coexistence Protocol" that maps out how Hearts operate alongside (not against) national currencies during the transition.

---

If I had to rank these by architectural urgency, the informal economies gap (#1) and the food security circuit breaker (#3) are the most likely to blow up first in a real pilot. The data sovereignty (#4) and currency interface (#8) issues are the ones most likely to kill the framework politically before it even launches.
