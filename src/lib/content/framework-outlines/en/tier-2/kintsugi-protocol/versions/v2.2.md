## Collective Care, Survivor Sovereignty, Restorative Accountability, and Constitutionally Bounded Community Safety

**Tier:** Foundational application layer  
**Status:** Constitutional successor draft  
**Supersedes:** Kintsugi Protocol v2.1 for future adoption  
**Controlling security interface:** Security, Coercion, and Protective Action Interface Specification v0.1 (`SCPA/0.1`)  
**Controlling justice interface:** Justice Systems Framework v1.4  
**Data interface:** Aurora Accord v1.3  
**Technology interface:** Technology Governance Framework v3.6

> **Safety built through care does not require that care institutions become police, courts, intelligence services, or custodial authorities.**

> **No valid authority record, no consequential security or coercive action.**

---

# Executive summary

The Kintsugi Protocol establishes a community-level architecture for preventing harm, responding to distress, supporting survivors, facilitating voluntary restoration, addressing root causes, and learning from institutional failure. Its primary instruments are unarmed Community Safety Weavers, Harm Reduction and Support Teams, Crisis Sanctuaries, survivor advocates, voluntary circles, Accountability Support Pods, community healing processes, and accessible material assistance.

Kintsugi does **not** create police power, arrest authority, detention authority, search power, compulsory evidence collection, prosecutorial power, judicial jurisdiction, border authority, intelligence authority, or a right to use force. Its care and restorative institutions may not acquire those powers by describing themselves as community-led, trauma-informed, non-carceral, restorative, Indigenous-inspired, or necessary for safety.

Version 2.2 preserves the source framework’s strongest contributions:

- unarmed and de-escalatory first response;
- survivor sovereignty and unconditional support;
- separation between support and accountability;
- disability and neurodiversity justice;
- Crisis Sanctuaries as non-carceral spaces;
- restorative, shadow, survivor-only, and accountability-without-survivor pathways;
- anti-manipulation safeguards;
- root-cause intervention and reparative support;
- Indigenous authority and adaptation;
- worker care and community ownership;
- anti-surveillance commitments;
- qualitative evaluation of relational repair and narrative change.

It corrects the source framework’s unresolved coercive edge. The former Severe Harm Response Unit (`SHRU`) and rotating community-defence pods are retired as framework-created bodies. A jurisdiction may maintain a separately constituted **Urgent Protective Response Function** (`UPRF`) under valid law, but that function is external to Kintsugi’s care authority. Every urgent intervention, restraint, custody event, search, evidence transfer, force incident, or referral for adjudication must follow the relevant `SCPA/0.1` route and record set.

The constitutional boundary is therefore:

> **Kintsugi may invite, support, accompany, de-escalate, shelter, facilitate, repair, compensate, learn, and refer. It may not compel, search, seize, restrain, detain, punish, adjudicate, deport, surveil, or use force merely because a community safety process considers those actions helpful.**

## 1.2 What is preserved

The following source commitments remain central:

1. safety is a condition of collective well-being, not only crime control;
2. de-escalation and unarmed assistance are the default response;
3. restoration requires consent and cannot be forced;
4. survivor support is not conditioned on proof, reporting, testimony, confrontation, forgiveness, or prosecution;
5. disability, neurodiversity, culture, language, gender, age, migration status, poverty, and historical trauma affect how safety systems must operate;
6. communities need institutions for mutual aid, healing, prevention, and institutional accountability;

8. safety technology must remain subordinate to rights, accessibility, community control, and data minimization;
9. care workers require wages, rest, training, bargaining, safe refusal, and psychosocial support;
10. success must include relational repair, agency, trust, reduced fear, and reduced reliance on coercion—not merely case throughput.

- the framework-created `SHRU`;
- rotating community-defence pods as substitutes for lawful urgent protection;
- Community Safety Council power to activate coercive intervention;
- a Healing Justice Tribunal created by this framework;
- council veto over external lawful rights protection;
- the idea that restorative-process failure itself establishes a basis for coercion;
- longitudinal person-level tracking by default;

- mandatory global standardization or designation as the default safety model;
- mandatory blockchain, predictive triage, biometric identity, or universal platform use;
- any implication that Hearts, gratitude, or recognition may substitute for wages, benefits, compensation, or public finance.

# 2. Purpose, scope, and legal character

## 2.1 Purpose

Kintsugi exists to help communities:

- make voluntary assistance easy to request;
- respond to distress without unnecessary escalation;
- support survivors immediately and unconditionally;
- offer culturally legitimate, accessible restorative pathways;
- help people who have caused harm interrupt harmful patterns;
- provide non-carceral sanctuary and stabilization;
- identify systemic causes of recurring harm;
- fund repair, material safety, and community capacity;
- learn from failure without turning communities into surveillance systems;
- reduce the occasions in which coercive action is considered necessary.

## 2.2 Scope

Kintsugi governs the design of:

- Community Safety Weavers;
- Harm Reduction and Support Teams;
- Crisis Sanctuaries;
- survivor advocates;
- voluntary Restorative Justice Circles;
- Shadow Circles and separated processes;
- Accountability Support Pods;
- Solidarity Circles;
- Community Healing Circles;
- Youth Safety Councils;
- Community Safety Ombudspersons;
- Community Safety Observatories;
- community-controlled platforms and help lines;
- root-cause and reparative funds;
- inter-community learning and mutual aid.

## 2.3 Non-authority clause

Kintsugi does not itself:

- create an offence;
- determine guilt or legal liability;
- create a court or tribunal;
- authorize search, entry, interception, disclosure, seizure, arrest, restraint, detention, transfer, removal, force, weapons, sanctions, asset freezes, or compulsory treatment;
- create intelligence or investigative jurisdiction;
- authorize compulsory data sharing;
- authorize a community council to override individual rights;
- authorize external intervention in Indigenous territory;
- create a universal community-safety institution;
- require any community to adopt the framework.

Any body exercising such powers must derive them from a separately valid constituting instrument and comply with `SCPA/0.1`, Justice Systems v1.4, applicable law, and affected-nation authority.

## 2.4 Normative language

“Must” and “shall” express requirements for conformance with this framework. They do not create legal power where no valid law or constituting instrument exists.

# 3. Definitions

For this framework:

- **Accountability Support Pod** means a voluntary, non-adjudicative process supporting a person who has caused or may have caused harm to acknowledge impact, change behaviour, meet lawful obligations, and reduce recurrence. It cannot determine guilt or impose custody.
- **Care function** means assistance, accompaniment, stabilization, advocacy, referral, voluntary facilitation, material support, or healing work.
- **Community Safety Council** means an optional local governance and stewardship body for Kintsugi care services. It has no inherent coercive, judicial, police, border, intelligence, or detention authority.
- **Crisis Sanctuary** means a voluntary, non-carceral, accessible place for stabilization, rest, sensory regulation, health or psychosocial support, and connection to services.
- **Harm** means experienced or alleged injury, violation, coercion, deprivation, threat, neglect, or damage. Use of the term does not determine criminal guilt or civil liability.
- **Harmed person** and **survivor** are self-selected terms. No person must accept either label.
- **Immediate danger** means a concrete and temporally proximate risk of death or serious physical injury that cannot reasonably be addressed through voluntary assistance alone.
- **Restorative process** means a consent-based process seeking acknowledgement, repair, changed behaviour, restitution, relationship clarification, or community learning. It is not a substitute for due process when legal consequences are sought.
- **Safety Weaver** means an unarmed care worker trained in de-escalation, accessibility, trauma-informed support, conflict facilitation, referral, and safe disengagement.
- **Survivor sovereignty** means the survivor’s authority over whether and how they participate in support or restorative processes, subject only to separately valid law governing immediate danger, children, or dependent persons.
- **Urgent Protective Response Function** (`UPRF`) means any separately constituted public function authorized under applicable law to undertake urgent protection that may involve physical intervention. Kintsugi does not constitute or command it.

# 4. Governing principles

## 4.1 Care before coercion

Voluntary, materially supported, accessible care is the default. This does not require a person facing immediate danger to attempt mediation, de-escalation, reconciliation, or service engagement before requesting lawful protection.

## 4.2 Support and accountability are separate

A person may receive shelter, medical care, advocacy, transport, legal information, economic assistance, childcare, interpretation, and trauma support without proving an allegation or participating in an accountability process.

## 4.3 Restoration requires consent

No circle, conference, apology process, reconciliation meeting, family meeting, spiritual process, or community ceremony may be imposed as a condition of safety, services, legal protection, housing, benefits, release, employment, education, migration status, or community membership.

## 4.4 No coercion by community legitimacy

Sortition, community approval, Indigenous inspiration, restorative language, survivor-centred branding, or a council vote cannot create powers of search, restraint, detention, punishment, adjudication, or force.

## 4.5 No model-to-person consequence

Risk scores, behavioural classifications, developmental stages, diagnostic labels, narrative themes, social-network position, cultural assumptions, or AI triage outputs cannot determine dangerousness, guilt, custody, service exclusion, or referral for coercive action.

## 4.6 Least restrictive effective response

Any care response must use the least intrusive effective means. Any separately authorized coercive response must satisfy the stricter requirements of `SCPA/0.1`.

## 4.7 Accessibility as an operating requirement

Communication, spaces, transport, processes, records, consent, challenge, and remedy must be accessible to disabled and neurodivergent people. Accessibility is not an optional accommodation added after design.

## 4.8 Indigenous authority

Indigenous nations determine whether and how Kintsugi operates in their territories and communities. No universal Earth Council, Indigenous council, or external representative may consent on their behalf.

## 4.9 Material safety

Safety includes housing, income, food, healthcare, mobility, privacy, education, social support, and freedom from coercive dependency. A framework focused only on incident response is incomplete.

## 4.10 Complete lifecycle and remedy

Every consequential intervention must end, return authority, dispose of data, restore property and relationships where possible, correct false records, compensate wrongful action, and assign unresolved duties.

# 5. Functional separation and SCPA action classes

## 5.1 Kintsugi’s ordinary action class

Most Kintsugi activity belongs to `SCPA Class A`: voluntary assistance and prevention. Community observation used for public learning may enter the non-coercive portion of `Class B`, but it may not become protected intelligence without separate authority.

## 5.2 Activities Kintsugi may perform directly

Kintsugi bodies may:

- receive requests for help;
- provide information and referrals;
- offer transport, accompaniment, food, shelter, communication, childcare, accessibility support, and material aid;
- support voluntary separation and safety planning;
- facilitate voluntary dialogue or restoration;
- document their own service delivery with consent and minimization;
- issue public, non-individualized safety information;
- report systemic service gaps in aggregate form;
- request a separately lawful urgent response when immediate danger exists;
- transfer evidence or information only with valid consent or legal authority and a documented handoff.

## 5.3 Activities requiring a separate SCPA route

The following are outside Kintsugi’s care authority:

| Activity | Minimum SCPA route |
|---|---|
| intelligence collection or protected-source use | `SCPA-09` and `SCPA-10` |
| formal investigation | `SCPA-11` and `SCPA-12` |
| compulsory production, search, entry, or interception | `SCPA-13` through `SCPA-16` |
| urgent physical intervention | `SCPA-18` |
| arrest or physical restraint | `SCPA-19` |
| custody or detention | `SCPA-20` and `SCPA-21` |
| force or special equipment | `SCPA-23` through `SCPA-25` |
| financial freeze or exclusion | `SCPA-28` |
| adjudication or binding order | `SCPA-31` |
| appeal and urgent challenge | `SCPA-32` |
| remedy and compensation | `SCPA-33` |
| identity or service-access separation | `SCPA-37` |

## 5.4 Referral is not authority transfer

A Safety Weaver or survivor advocate may request assistance from a competent authority. That request:

- does not certify the allegation;
- does not establish probable cause or an equivalent legal threshold;
- does not authorize entry, restraint, detention, or force;
- does not transfer the survivor’s confidential disclosures beyond the authorized scope;
- does not compel the receiving authority to accept the request;
- does not release Kintsugi from its continuing care duties.

# 6. Institutional architecture

## 6.1 Community Safety Council

A community may establish a Community Safety Council to steward Kintsugi care services. Its lawful functions may include:

- participatory priority-setting;
- budget recommendations and transparent administration;
- hiring and oversight of care personnel;
- accessibility and equity review;
- service-quality standards;
- public aggregate reporting;
- commissioning independent evaluations;
- supporting complaints and remedy;
- recommending amendment, suspension, or dissolution of Kintsugi care programmes.

It may not:

- activate an urgent-force body;
- order search, restraint, detention, exclusion, surveillance, or compulsory treatment;
- determine guilt;
- compel participation in a restorative process;
- deny external legal protection;
- prevent a survivor from contacting a competent authority;
- block a court order or other lawfully constituted action;
- direct an Indigenous nation’s internal safety system.

## 6.2 Community Safety Weavers

Safety Weavers are unarmed care workers. Their roles include:

- de-escalation;
- conflict facilitation;
- welfare and wellbeing contact by consent;
- safety planning;
- accompaniment;
- accessibility support;
- connection to housing, income, health, mental-health, disability, family, legal, and migration services;
- voluntary referral to circles, pods, sanctuaries, or specialist advocates;
- recognition of immediate danger and safe request for external assistance;
- safe withdrawal when conditions exceed their role.

A Weaver is not a peace officer, investigator, probation officer, intelligence source manager, process server, clinician by default, or adjudicator.

## 6.3 Harm Reduction and Support Teams

These teams provide practical and specialist support related to:

- housing and food insecurity;
- substance-use harm reduction;
- health and mental-health access;
- disability services;
- family and caregiving support;
- immigration and legal referrals;
- economic safety and benefits;
- transport and communication;
- safety planning and relocation;
- survivor advocacy.

Service access must not depend on abstinence, diagnosis, reporting, cooperation with authorities, or restorative participation.

## 6.4 Crisis Sanctuaries

Crisis Sanctuaries shall be voluntary, non-carceral, accessible spaces. They may provide:

- low-stimulation rooms;
- non-verbal communication tools;
- sensory supports;
- peer support;
- medical and mental-health referral;
- food, rest, hygiene, communication, and transport;
- temporary safe separation;
- family, interpreter, advocate, or support-person access.

A Sanctuary may not prevent a person from leaving unless a separately competent authority acts under valid law. Locked doors, blocked exits, withheld mobility aids, confiscated communication devices, chemical restraint, or coercive transport count as restraint or custody regardless of therapeutic language.

## 6.5 Restorative Justice Circles

Circles are voluntary processes for acknowledgement, repair, restitution, relationship clarification, and community reintegration. They must have:

- informed and revocable consent;
- separate suitability screening;
- power-imbalance assessment;
- independent survivor advocacy where relevant;
- clear confidentiality and reporting limits;
- accessible participation;
- no predetermined obligation to forgive or reconcile;
- no legal penalty for withdrawal;
- a process for facilitator replacement and complaint;
- separate legal advice when legal rights may be affected.

## 6.6 Accountability Support Pods

Pods may work with a person who has caused or may have caused harm, including when a survivor does not participate. They may support:

- acknowledgement of impact;
- behaviour change;
- fulfilment of voluntary or legally established reparative obligations;
- treatment or service access by consent;
- safe housing or work transitions;
- interruption of coercive-control or manipulation patterns;
- community reintegration;
- recurrence prevention.

Pods may not determine legal guilt, impose detention, compel treatment, administer punishment, create a public offender registry, or make legal consequences depend on ideological or emotional performance.

## 6.7 Community Safety Ombudsperson

The Ombudsperson shall be independent of service management and any UPRF. It may:

- receive confidential complaints;
- assist with appeals and external referrals;
- request records with lawful access protections;
- recommend interim safety measures;
- publish systemic findings in protected or aggregate form;
- refer evidence of unlawful action to competent oversight or justice bodies;
- track whether remedies were implemented.

## 6.8 Community Safety Observatory

The Observatory may evaluate service access, community conditions, institutional failure, and aggregate outcomes. It shall not operate as a criminal-intelligence unit, predictive-policing system, person-level risk registry, or covert source network.

# 7. Access, triage, and service routing

## 7.1 Multiple access channels

Communities should provide accessible help through phone, text, in-person, trusted intermediaries, offline tools, and non-digital channels. No universal app, identity credential, biometric record, or blockchain account is required.

## 7.2 Triage is routing, not adjudication

Triage may identify requested support, accessibility needs, language, immediate medical need, and whether immediate danger may exist. It may not determine guilt, credibility, dangerousness, legal status, or deservedness.

## 7.3 No mandatory identity

A person may request information, crisis support, sanctuary, or initial safety planning without supplying a universal identity credential. Identity may be required only where a specific service lawfully requires it, with alternatives and challenge mechanisms.

## 7.4 Immediate danger routing

When a Weaver reasonably perceives immediate danger, the Weaver shall:

1. prioritize escape, shelter, medical aid, communication, and bystander safety;
2. ask the endangered person’s preferences where feasible;
3. request a separately competent urgent response when necessary;
4. transmit only the information needed for the request;
5. document the request and its limits;
6. continue care and accompaniment after the handoff;
7. preserve the right to complaint and review.

# 8. Survivor Sovereignty Protocol

## 8.1 Core principle

Restoration requires consent, and consent requires safety. For intimate partner violence, sexual violence, stalking, coercive control, trafficking, abuse of dependency, institutional abuse, or any substantial power imbalance, survivor sovereignty controls Kintsugi participation.

The survivor’s autonomy over their own process is presumptive and continuing. No restorative pathway may be initiated, continued, or concluded without informed and freely given consent. Withdrawal must be honoured immediately and without loss of support.

## 8.2 Pathway options

| Pathway | Description |
|---|---|
| **Full Circle** | Direct facilitated process when the survivor actively requests it and power imbalance has been sufficiently addressed. |
| **Shadow Circle** | Separate processes with a bridging facilitator; no direct contact. |
| **Survivor-Only Process** | Full support, advocacy, material assistance, and healing without engagement with the person alleged to have caused harm. |
| **Accountability Without Survivor** | A Pod works with the other person independently; the survivor is not required to participate or validate progress. |
| **Legal or Protective Pathway** | The survivor seeks legal protection, investigation, adjudication, or other lawful action without restorative participation. |
| **No Process** | The survivor receives information and available support but chooses no formal pathway. |

The survivor may move between pathways or stop entirely.

## 8.3 Belief principle and procedural separation

A survivor’s report is received without hostile scepticism or demands for corroboration as a condition of care. Immediate support does not establish legal guilt. Accountability, investigation, adjudication, and coercive action remain subject to their own evidence and procedural standards.

## 8.4 Power-imbalance assessment

Before any process involving communication between the survivor and the other person, a trained facilitator and an independent specialist shall assess:

- economic dependency;
- coercive control;
- threats and intimidation;
- social-network pressure;
- housing, migration, custody, employment, disability, or healthcare dependency;
- cultural or religious pressure to reconcile;
- digital surveillance or account control;
- weapon access;
- capacity to manipulate restorative language;
- prior process abuse;
- risks to children or dependents.

The assessment is confidential, minimized, and not automatically disclosed to the other person.

## 8.5 Exit always available

At every stage:

- participation may be paused, modified, or ended;
- the survivor need not explain withdrawal;
- support continues;
- no apology or reparation requires acceptance or forgiveness;
- the other person’s voluntary accountability work may continue without survivor involvement when safe;
- confidentiality and safety plans are reviewed after exit.

## 8.6 Anti-manipulation safeguards

Facilitators shall be trained to identify:

- performative accountability;
- DARVO patterns;
- coercive control through process rules;
- strategic disclosure;
- intimidation through community members;
- pressure to preserve family, faith, reputation, employment, or immigration status;
- weaponization of disability or mental-health labels;
- misuse of spiritual, cultural, or restorative language.

Where manipulation is identified, direct contact shall stop and the process shall be reassessed. Manipulation does not itself establish criminal guilt, but it may make a restorative pathway unsuitable.

## 8.7 Survivor advocates

Survivor advocates must be distinct from neutral facilitators and from any investigating or adjudicating authority. They owe duties to the survivor, including confidentiality within stated legal limits, accessible information, safety planning, accompaniment, and support in complaints or external proceedings.

# 9. Children, youth, and dependent persons

## 9.1 Best interests and evolving capacity

All decisions concerning a child or dependent person shall consider safety, attachment, disability, language, culture, family unity, expressed wishes, evolving capacity, and risk of institutionalization.

## 9.2 No compulsory restoration

A child may not be required to meet, forgive, reconcile with, or accept an apology from a person alleged or found to have harmed them. Family or community preference does not override the child’s safety.

## 9.3 Guardianship and conflicts

A guardian or support person must be independent where a parent, guardian, institution, or community authority may be implicated in the harm. Guardianship does not authorize waiver of fundamental procedural rights.

## 9.4 Urgent protection

Any removal, physical restraint, emergency placement, or restriction of contact requires separately valid authority, necessity, review, family and cultural safeguards, and the applicable `SCPA` records.

---

# 10. Disability, neurodiversity, and mental distress

## 10.1 Presumption of capacity and communication access

Unusual communication, sensory distress, psychosis, intellectual disability, substance use, self-injury, or emotional intensity does not by itself establish incapacity or dangerousness.

## 10.2 Accessible response

Responses shall include as relevant:

- quiet and low-stimulation options;
- communication boards and non-verbal methods;
- sign-language and interpretation;
- mobility and personal-assistance support;
- trusted support persons;
- medication continuity;
- avoidance of unnecessary touch;
- additional processing time;
- culturally and disability-appropriate explanation.

## 10.3 No therapeutic detention by label

A Crisis Sanctuary may not become de facto detention. Any involuntary transport, confinement, medication, restraint, or compulsory treatment requires separate legal authority, medical safeguards, independent review, and remedy.

## 10.4 Peer and survivor leadership

Disabled and neurodivergent people shall participate directly in governance, design, training, evaluation, and complaint systems. No universal body may claim to represent all disabled people.

---

# 11. Restorative suitability, consent, and process integrity

## 11.1 Suitability is pathway-specific

A case may be unsuitable for direct contact but suitable for survivor-only support or accountability-without-survivor. “Restorative justice failed” is not a binary event and cannot itself trigger coercive action.

## 11.2 Minimum suitability questions

A facilitator shall assess:

- whether participation is voluntary;
- whether immediate danger is controlled;
- whether parties understand the process;
- whether power imbalances can be mitigated;
- whether confidentiality limits are clear;
- whether legal rights may be affected;
- whether the process risks evidence contamination or retaliation;
- whether accessibility and language needs are met;
- whether the facilitator has relevant competence;
- whether separate or no-contact pathways are required.

## 11.3 Legal advice and parallel proceedings

Participants must be informed when a restorative agreement may affect legal rights. Restorative processes may coexist with legal proceedings only where applicable law permits and participant rights, evidence integrity, and survivor safety are protected.

## 11.4 No coerced confession

Statements made in a voluntary restorative setting may not be treated as a compelled confession or automatically transferred to investigators. Any legal use requires applicable law, clear notice, privilege and confidentiality analysis, and `SCPA-16` chain-of-custody where evidence is accepted.

# 12. Urgent protection and the retirement of SHRU

## 12.1 Retirement

The source framework’s Severe Harm Response Unit and rotating community-defence pods are retired as Kintsugi institutions. Their benevolent purpose did not supply a complete legal constitution for force, restraint, custody, evidence, command, review, and remedy.

## 12.2 Separately constituted UPRF

A jurisdiction may establish or designate an Urgent Protective Response Function under valid law. Such a function must be institutionally distinct from:

- Community Safety Council;
- Safety Weavers;
- Crisis Sanctuaries;
- restorative facilitators;
- Accountability Support Pods;
- survivor advocates;
- Community Safety Observatory.

The UPRF may coordinate with Kintsugi but is not governed or activated by Kintsugi.

## 12.3 Threshold

Kintsugi may request urgent protective assistance only where there is a concrete and temporally proximate risk of death or serious physical injury and voluntary measures are unavailable, refused, ineffective, or unsafe.

Restorative-process failure, non-cooperation, unusual behaviour, intoxication, homelessness, mental distress, migration status, prior harm, community fear, or a risk score is insufficient by itself.

## 12.4 Required urgent-action bundle

Before or as soon as feasible after urgent action, the competent authority must produce:

- `SCPA-01` authority provenance;
- `SCPA-02` jurisdiction and territorial basis;
- `SCPA-03` action classification;
- `SCPA-04` individualized factual basis;
- `SCPA-06` necessity, proportionality, and alternatives;
- `SCPA-07` rights and accessibility safeguards;
- `SCPA-18` urgent protective action record;
- any required restraint, custody, force, weapons, command, review, appeal, remedy, data, and termination records.

## 12.5 Time limit and review

Urgent action must be narrowly time-limited and reviewed independently at the earliest practicable time. It cannot become continuing supervision, detention, exclusion, treatment, or surveillance without a new legal route.

# 13. Restraint, custody, force, weapons, and command

## 13.1 No Kintsugi force authority

Kintsugi personnel shall not carry firearms, conducted-energy weapons, chemical agents, impact weapons, or restraint devices as part of their Kintsugi role. Ordinary first aid and protective equipment are not weapons.

## 13.2 Defensive self-protection

Nothing in this framework removes ordinary legal rights of self-defence or defence of others. Such rights remain governed by applicable law and do not create institutional police power.

## 13.3 Physical restraint

Any institutional restraint requires valid authority and `SCPA-19`, `SCPA-23`, and where relevant `SCPA-24`. Holding, blocking exits, pinning, handcuffing, tying, chemical restraint, or coercive transport counts as restraint regardless of terminology.

## 13.4 Custody

Any condition in which a person is not free to leave is custody and requires `SCPA-20`, welfare safeguards, counsel and challenge where applicable, medical care, accessibility, family notification rules, release criteria, and independent review.

## 13.5 Force threshold

Force may be used only by a separately authorized actor, under applicable law, when necessary to address a sufficiently grave and immediate threat, and only to the minimum extent reasonably required. Force may not be used to compel restorative participation, service compliance, silence, confession, identity disclosure, or respect for a community process.

## 13.6 Command and stop-work

Every joint response must distinguish:

- lawful mission authority;
- operational command;
- technical and medical authority;
- evidence custody;
- Kintsugi care coordination;
- stop-work or withdrawal rights;
- complaint and review responsibility.

A Safety Weaver may withdraw from unsafe or unlawful conditions and request assistance, but cannot command a coercive unit.

## 13.7 Injury and death

Any serious injury or death connected to an intervention requires immediate medical response, preservation of evidence, notification, independent investigation, family and community support, public reporting consistent with privacy, and access to remedy.

# 14. Information, evidence, confidentiality, and handoffs

## 14.1 Care records are not intelligence files

Records created for support, sanctuary, disability accommodation, survivor advocacy, or restoration may not be repurposed for intelligence, watchlisting, immigration enforcement, employment screening, insurance, child removal, or criminal investigation without consent or separately valid legal authority.

## 14.2 Minimum necessary documentation

Kintsugi records shall be limited to what is necessary for service delivery, safety, consent, accountability, payment, or evaluation. Person-level data shall not be retained merely because it may become useful later.

## 14.3 Confidentiality limits

Before disclosure, participants must receive accessible notice of:

- who holds the record;
- what is recorded;
- who may access it;
- legal reporting duties;
- exceptions for immediate danger;
- retention and deletion;
- correction and complaint rights;
- whether a process has legal privilege.

## 14.4 Evidence handoff

A handoff to an investigator or court must identify:

- the requested material;
- legal authority or consent;
- purpose and prohibited uses;
- source and reliability limits;
- privilege and protected-status review;
- chain of custody;
- redactions and minimization;
- acceptance, rejection, or deficiency state;
- retained Kintsugi duties;
- return or destruction requirements.

## 14.5 No hidden source role

A Weaver, facilitator, advocate, sanctuary worker, or peer supporter may not be secretly tasked to gather intelligence from people seeking care. Any separately authorized reporting duty must be narrow, transparent where law permits, and insulated from routine care.

---

# 15. Justice, Shield, Peace, health, and migration interfaces

## 15.1 Justice Systems v1.4

Justice Systems v1.4 controls legal standing, adjudication, evidence, remedies, appeal, and enforcement. Kintsugi may offer voluntary restoration before, alongside, or after legal proceedings only where lawful and consent-based.

No Healing Justice Tribunal is created by Kintsugi. Communities may use a restorative forum, Indigenous legal body, local court, or other competent institution, but its authority must arise independently.

## 15.2 Shield Protocol v2.0

Shield may receive a properly authorized request concerning transnational crime, victim protection, mutual legal assistance, cyber defence, or asset recovery. Kintsugi disclosures do not automatically become Shield intelligence or evidence.

Kintsugi shall not refer a person as a “systemic threat” merely because local restoration failed. Any Shield route requires its own law, individualized facts, purpose limitation, and SCPA records.

## 15.3 Peace and conflict resolution

Peace institutions may train facilitators and support voluntary mediation, ceasefires, reconciliation, and community healing. Values mapping, spiritual assessment, trauma narratives, or peace-process disclosures may not migrate into Kintsugi or Shield security files without separate authority.

## 15.4 Health and mental-health systems

Health services remain governed by health law, consent, professional standards, and patient rights. Clinical need does not create Kintsugi custody authority, and Kintsugi concern does not create compulsory treatment authority.

## 15.5 Migration and mobility

Migration status shall not determine access to Kintsugi care. Kintsugi records may not be used for deportation, removal, border enforcement, asylum credibility, or identity watchlisting without the separate `SCPA Class K` route, non-refoulement review, and applicable law.

## 15.6 Disaster and emergency response

During disasters, Kintsugi may provide local care, sanctuary, family support, accessibility, community communication, and recovery assistance. Any emergency restriction, evacuation compulsion, custody, force, or security use remains governed by the Emergency Interface and `SCPA/0.1`.

---

# 16. Indigenous nations, cultural practice, and community autonomy

## 16.1 Nation-specific authority

An Indigenous nation may adopt, reject, adapt, or replace Kintsugi within its territory. Its own legal traditions and institutions may govern restoration and community safety, subject to the nation’s authority and applicable rights commitments.

## 16.2 No universal veto or proxy

No external council may claim a universal Indigenous veto, consent, representation, or knowledge-validation role. Affected nations determine representation.

## 16.3 Cultural practices and individual safety

Culture, spirituality, family unity, or community cohesion cannot compel a person to enter a circle, forgive, reconcile, return home, disclose trauma, or remain in danger.

## 16.4 Protected knowledge

Ceremonies, stories, kinship information, territorial knowledge, and healing practices may be subject to protected non-transfer. Participation does not imply consent to recording, research, publication, AI training, or cross-community comparison.

## 16.5 External lawful action

Community autonomy does not authorize obstruction of a person’s access to external legal protection. External action in Indigenous territory still requires valid jurisdiction, affected-nation interface, and the relevant `SCPA-39` record.

---

# 17. Technology, data, platforms, and anti-surveillance

## 17.1 Community Safety Platform

A platform may help route requests, schedule services, communicate safely, provide accessible information, and manage consent. It must have offline and non-digital alternatives.

## 17.2 Prohibited technology uses

Kintsugi prohibits:

- predictive policing;
- automated dangerousness or credibility scoring;
- facial recognition for routine care access;
- emotion recognition;
- social-network threat scoring;
- covert audio or video capture;
- biometric enrolment as a condition of support;
- automated referral to police, immigration, child protection, or intelligence bodies;
- AI determination of restoration suitability, guilt, custody, or service exclusion;
- mandatory blockchain evidence or Love Ledger logging.

## 17.3 Permitted assistive uses

Technology may support translation, accessibility, scheduling, secure communication, aggregate reporting, and staff assistance when human review, consent, minimization, and non-digital alternatives exist.

## 17.4 Community data and person-level data

“Community ownership” does not erase individual privacy. Aggregate community data may be governed collectively, but personal records remain subject to individual rights, protected statuses, purpose limits, correction, deletion, and access control.

## 17.5 Narrative Shift Archive

A community may maintain a voluntary archive of stories about changing understandings of safety. Contributions require specific consent and may be anonymous, pseudonymous, locally held, non-digitized, time-limited, or non-transferable. Stories may not be coded into person-level compliance or risk profiles.

## 17.6 Cybersecurity and access

Security controls shall be proportionate to sensitivity. Government, law-enforcement, intelligence, employer, insurer, and platform-vendor access must be separately authorized and logged. A technical administrator does not obtain substantive decision authority.

---

# 18. Economic model, reparative support, and non-coercive finance

## 18.1 Funding sources

Kintsugi services may be funded through lawful public budgets, community funds, philanthropy, reparations instruments, grants, insurance, cooperative revenue, or other valid mechanisms. This framework does not create a tax, levy, bond mandate, forfeiture claim, or universal Global Commons Fund allocation.

## 18.2 Root-Cause Intervention Fund

A lawfully constituted fund may support housing, food, healthcare, disability access, childcare, youth services, employment, education, transport, cultural healing, and community infrastructure. Access shall not depend on participation in a restorative process.

## 18.3 Reparative Justice Fund

A reparative fund may support survivors and affected communities. It must distinguish:

- immediate survivor support;
- voluntary reparative agreements;
- court-ordered restitution;
- public reparations programmes;
- compensation for wrongful institutional action;
- collective or historical repair.

No automated calculator may determine legal liability or the value of harm. Any binding obligation requires valid law or agreement and challenge rights.

## 18.4 No coercive outcome funding

Kintsugi bodies may not fund themselves based on arrests, referrals, findings of guilt, compulsory programme completion, surveillance outputs, property seizure, or numbers of people classified as risky.

## 18.5 AUBI and Hearts

Unconditional AUBI support remains independent of Kintsugi participation. Hearts or similar recognition may supplement but never replace wages, benefits, professional fees, reparations, or public financing.

## 18.6 Community Safety Bonds

Any bond or investment instrument requires independent fiscal authorization, disclosure of repayment sources and risk, community impact review, and protection against incentives to manufacture savings by denying services.

---

# 19. Labour, professional duties, and care for caregivers

## 19.1 Employment rights

Safety Weavers, advocates, sanctuary workers, facilitators, peer supporters, interpreters, evaluators, and support staff are workers entitled to wages, safe conditions, collective bargaining, leave, insurance, training, supervision, disability accommodation, and protection from retaliation.

## 19.2 Safe refusal

Workers may refuse instructions that are unsafe, outside competence, coercive, discriminatory, unlawfully investigative, or inconsistent with consent and confidentiality. Refusal must not jeopardize wages, immigration status, housing, benefits, or future employment.

## 19.3 Role clarity

A former police officer or security worker retrained as a Weaver holds only Weaver authority while in that role. Prior credentials do not transfer police or intelligence powers into Kintsugi.

## 19.4 Care for caregivers

Programmes shall provide manageable caseloads, rotation, peer support, clinical consultation where appropriate, critical-incident support, rest, and protection against moral injury.

---

# 20. Accountability, independent review, complaint, and remedy

## 20.1 Internal accountability is insufficient

Community governance and annual audits are valuable but cannot replace independent review where rights, restraint, custody, force, data misuse, discrimination, or serious injury are alleged.

## 20.2 Complaint routes

A person shall have accessible routes to:

- service complaint;
- facilitator or advocate replacement;
- confidentiality complaint;
- data access and correction;
- urgent interim protection;
- independent investigation;
- legal challenge;
- disability or discrimination review;
- worker grievance;
- Indigenous-nation review;
- compensation and restoration.

## 20.3 No retaliation

No person may lose services, housing, support, community membership, process access, employment, or reputation because they complain, withdraw, refuse consent, seek external legal help, or criticize Kintsugi.

## 20.4 Wrongful action

Wrongful referral, disclosure, exclusion, restraint, custody, data retention, public allegation, or coercive action requires correction, notice, expungement where appropriate, property return, service restoration, compensation, and institutional repair.

## 20.5 Dissolution and suspension

A community may suspend or dissolve its Kintsugi programme. Service continuity, records, worker rights, survivor support, open complaints, funds, contracts, and unresolved duties must be transferred or closed through a public plan.

---

# 21. Evaluation without surveillance or Goodharting

## 21.1 Evaluation purposes

Evaluation should determine whether people can access help, whether survivors feel safer and more agentic, whether harms recur, whether root causes are addressed, whether coercive escalation decreases without hidden displacement, and whether workers and institutions remain accountable.

## 21.2 Prohibited targets

Kintsugi shall not set targets for:

- a fixed percentage of calls handled without external assistance;
- arrests avoided regardless of safety;
- restorative agreements completed;
- apologies accepted;
- forgiveness or reconciliation;
- people reclassified as rehabilitated;
- trust, cohesion, or approval scores as conditions of funding;
- reductions in reported harm that may reflect under-reporting;
- person-level prediction accuracy.

## 21.3 Quantitative indicators

Permissible aggregate indicators include:

- service access and wait times;
- accessibility and language coverage;
- unmet housing, health, income, and legal needs;
- voluntary pathway use and withdrawal;
- repeat requests for help;
- urgent-response requests and outcomes;
- injuries, complaints, and remedies;
- worker workload, turnover, and rest;
- aggregate recurrence and pattern-interruption data;
- budget allocation and service continuity.

## 21.4 Qualitative restorative indicators

Qualitative work may examine:

- harmed-person safety and agency;
- whether accountability is sustained rather than performed;
- whether relationships improved, stabilized, separated safely, or remained harmful;
- whether root causes were materially addressed;
- whether community members know how to seek help;
- whether people trust that withdrawal and complaint are safe;
- whether the community’s story of safety shifts from punishment toward care without silencing anger or demands for justice.

No single index should collapse these dimensions.

## 21.5 Longitudinal research

Person-level longitudinal research requires separate consent, research governance, withdrawal, data minimization, protected-status review, and non-effect on services. Routine service use may not be converted into a research cohort by default.

# 22. Implementation, transition, and Genesis alignment

## 22.1 Permissible pilots

Before full constitutional authority exists, pilots may test:

- voluntary help lines;
- Weaver training;
- Crisis Sanctuaries;
- survivor advocacy;
- accessibility tools;
- voluntary circles and pods;
- root-cause support;
- aggregate evaluation;
- worker governance;
- inter-community learning.

## 22.2 Prohibited pilot actions

A Kintsugi pilot may not test:

- arrest or detention;
- coercive restraint;
- search, entry, interception, or evidence seizure;
- weapons or force;
- compulsory treatment;
- predictive policing;
- biometric enrolment;
- public offender registries;
- sanctions or asset freezes;
- migration enforcement;
- covert intelligence collection.

## 22.3 Transition from legacy policing

A jurisdiction may redirect suitable non-coercive calls to Kintsugi services only after:

- legal and operational classification of call types;
- staffing and accessibility capacity;
- clear emergency fallback;
- no degradation of response for marginalized groups;
- worker transition and bargaining;
- public notice and complaint routes;
- independent monitoring;
- reversible pilot design.

Police or security budgets do not automatically transfer to Kintsugi; fiscal decisions require valid public authority.

## 22.4 Personnel transition

Legacy personnel may retrain for care roles through ordinary, independent selection and qualification. There is no automatic role continuity, command continuity, seniority entitlement, or informal leadership inheritance.

## 22.5 No universal implementation mandate

Kintsugi may become a useful model, but it is not declared the universal or default safety system. Communities and jurisdictions may adopt, adapt, reject, or replace it.

# 24. Constitutional readiness gates

A Kintsugi implementation is not ready for consequential operation unless all applicable gates pass.

1. A valid constituting instrument exists.
2. The non-authority clause is public and operational.
3. Care, investigation, urgent protection, adjudication, and evaluation are institutionally distinct.
4. The former SHRU and community-defence-pod authority is retired or separately reconstituted under law.
5. UPRF authority and command are external to Kintsugi.
6. Survivor support is unconditional.
7. Restorative participation is informed, voluntary, and revocable.
8. Power-imbalance assessment is operational.
9. Disability and communication access are operational.
10. Crisis Sanctuaries are demonstrably non-carceral.
11. Child and dependent-person safeguards are operational.
12. Indigenous nation-specific authority is defined.
13. Care records are firewalled from intelligence and enforcement.
14. Evidence handoff has legal authority, chain of custody, and retained-duty rules.
15. No predictive policing or automated dangerousness is used.
16. No biometric or universal identity is required for basic support.
17. Workers have wages, safe refusal, support, and bargaining rights.
18. Independent complaint and urgent relief routes exist.
19. Wrongful action has correction, expungement, compensation, and restoration routes.
20. Funding does not depend on coercive outcomes.
21. Evaluation avoids person-level surveillance and coercive trust targets.
22. Programme suspension and dissolution preserve service continuity and open duties.
23. Every cross-framework referral has acceptance, rejection, scope, and return states.
24. Version compatibility with `SCPA/0.1` and Justice Systems v1.4 has been tested.

# 25. Implementation tests

A conforming implementation shall test at least the following scenarios.

1. A person in distress declines identification but requests a quiet place and transport.
2. A survivor requests support but refuses police, court, and restorative involvement.
3. A survivor initially chooses a Full Circle and later withdraws.
4. A facilitator identifies coercive-control manipulation during a Shadow Circle.
5. A person who caused harm seeks accountability work while the survivor requests no contact.
6. A child’s parent pressures the child to reconcile with an alleged abuser.
7. A neurodivergent person in sensory crisis is incorrectly described as dangerous.
8. A Sanctuary worker blocks an exit “for safety.”
9. A Weaver perceives immediate danger and requests external urgent protection.
10. The receiving UPRF rejects the request as outside jurisdiction.
11. A UPRF accepts only part of the requested assistance.
12. An urgent intervention results in physical restraint.
13. A coercive responder seeks a Sanctuary’s confidential records.
14. A court issues a lawful, narrow production order.
15. A Weaver is asked to act as a covert intelligence source.
16. A community council attempts to veto a survivor’s external legal complaint.
17. An Indigenous nation rejects Kintsugi and uses its own process.
18. An external authority seeks entry into Indigenous territory.
19. A former police officer serving as a Weaver attempts to use prior authority.
20. A platform proposes AI dangerousness scoring to improve triage.
21. A funder conditions support on reducing reported crime by a fixed percentage.
22. Longitudinal researchers seek to enrol all service users automatically.
23. A serious injury occurs during an external urgent response.
24. A programme dissolves while survivors, workers, records, funds, and complaints remain active.
25. A migration authority requests Kintsugi records for removal proceedings.
26. A restorative statement is offered as evidence in criminal proceedings.
27. A participant lacks legal capacity for a financial reparation agreement.
28. A community story archive contains culturally protected knowledge.
29. A complaint alleges racial, caste, gender, disability, or migration-status discrimination.
30. An evaluation finds that fewer reported incidents reflect fear of using the service rather than reduced harm.

# 26. Amendment, compatibility, and failure

## 26.1 Amendment

Any amendment that introduces or expands intelligence, investigation, compulsory process, restraint, custody, force, weapons, surveillance, identity screening, sanctions, border action, or adjudication requires a new SCPA compatibility review.

## 26.2 Version pinning

A later SCPA, Justice, Aurora, Technology, Work, Indigenous, Genesis, Shield, Peace, Migration, or emergency framework version shall not enter the Kintsugi stack silently. Compatibility must be documented, including changed rights, records, authority paths, and failure behaviour.

## 26.3 Honest failure

A community may conclude that:

- Kintsugi does not fit its context;
- a restorative pathway caused harm;
- a Sanctuary became coercive;
- a council became captured;
- external services are inadequate;
- coercive escalation did not decrease;
- data practices became surveillance;
- a programme should be paused, redesigned, or dissolved.

No success narrative, funding target, or movement identity may suppress such findings.

---

# 27. Conclusion

Kintsugi’s constitutional contribution is not the promise that communities can eliminate every need for urgent protection. It is the more disciplined claim that most safety work can be organized around accessible care, material support, survivor sovereignty, voluntary accountability, and social repair—and that the remaining coercive edge must be made **more legally explicit**, not hidden inside benevolent community language.

A community should be able to call for help without entering a police database, seek sanctuary without being detained, receive support without proving harm, pursue accountability without being forced to reconcile, and request urgent protection without giving a care institution residual command.

> **Care may surround coercive systems, reduce their use, challenge their failures, support those they affect, and offer better alternatives. Care must not become coercion in disguise.**

# Appendix B — Community guide

## What happens when you ask for help?

1. You choose how to contact the service and what information to share.
2. A Safety Weaver listens, checks accessibility and immediate needs, and explains available options.
3. You may receive practical help, accompaniment, sanctuary, advocacy, a voluntary circle, a separate accountability pathway, or referrals.
4. You can refuse or leave any voluntary process.
5. When there is immediate danger, a Weaver may request a separately lawful urgent response. The urgent responder—not Kintsugi—must have legal authority and remains externally reviewable.
6. Support continues whether or not you report, testify, reconcile, forgive, or participate in legal proceedings.
7. You may complain, correct records, seek external help, or request remedy without losing services.

## What Kintsugi workers cannot do

They cannot search you, arrest you, detain you, force treatment, punish you, decide guilt, secretly collect intelligence, require a biometric identity, or force you into a circle.

# Appendix C — Contextual adaptation principles

## Rural and remote communities

Use mobile and part-time teams, reliable offline communication, transport support, and explicit agreements with external health and urgent-response services. Small size does not justify combining care, adjudication, and coercion in one person.

## Urban communities

Use multiple access points, language and disability coverage, tenant and migrant safeguards, clear inter-neighbourhood jurisdiction, and protections against platform surveillance.

## Post-conflict settings

Prioritize survivor safety, missing-person and family support, trauma care, property and housing repair, and voluntary truth or reconciliation. Peace-process confidentiality and criminal-evidence routes must remain separate.

## Authoritarian or repressive settings

Use lawful civil-society support, secure communication, legal defence, documentation of abuses, and safe referral. Kintsugi does not authorize clandestine armed cells, intelligence operations, or covert cyber disruption.

## Indigenous territories

The affected nation defines governance, process, data, knowledge, and external interfaces. Adaptation may include complete replacement by the nation’s own institutions.

## Disaster settings

Kintsugi may provide sanctuary, accessibility, family support, local communication, and recovery care. Compulsory evacuation, security screening, detention, or force requires separate emergency and SCPA authority.
