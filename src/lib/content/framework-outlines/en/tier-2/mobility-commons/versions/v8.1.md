## 1. Executive Summary

The Mobility Commons reimagines transportation as a regenerative, equitable, and shared global resource. It establishes a fossil-free, universally accessible mobility system that respects ecosystems, prioritizes community sovereignty, and ensures that movement serves human flourishing rather than the other way around.

Transportation accounts for approximately 14% of global greenhouse gas emissions (IPCC, 2022), with freight alone representing nearly half of transport-related carbon. The 20th-century model — car-centric, fossil-fueled, and extractive — has driven climate change, displaced communities, and degraded ecosystems.

This framework offers an alternative. It is built on four pillars:

| Pillar | Focus |
|--------|-------|
| **Governance** | Democratic, multi-stakeholder coordination through the Global Mobility Council |
| **Infrastructure** | Regenerative, bioregional networks that restore ecosystems while connecting communities |
| **Economy** | Just transition funding, Hearts/Leaves incentives, and community ownership models |
| **Justice** | Universal access, mobility reparations, and the right to stay |

The framework is designed for phased implementation, beginning with pilot projects in aligned BAZs and scaling through the pull effect of superior outcomes — cleaner air, shorter commutes, stronger communities.

## 2. Core Principles

| Principle | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Mobility as a Commons** | The freedom to move is a public good, governed transparently by multi-stakeholder councils, prioritizing community benefit over private gain |
| **Fossil-Free by Default** | All new infrastructure and vehicles align with net-zero targets by 2030; temporary emergency fuel exemptions require strict sunset clauses |
| **Proximity Priority** | The highest success metric is not ridership but the reduction in forced mobility; communities should be designed so daily needs are within walking distance |
| **Right to Movement & Access** | Safe, affordable, and accessible mobility for all, regardless of location, income, or ability |
| **Universal Design** | Systems designed for universal access, adhering to disability justice standards |
| **Bioregional Integration** | Corridors respect ecosystems, watershed boundaries, wildlife migration, and BAZ-led plans |
| **Community-Led & Owned** | Projects co-designed with communities; clear pathways from private/state to cooperative ownership |
| **Data Sovereignty** | Mobility data is a community-controlled resource, protected against surveillance under the Aurora Accord |
| **Freight as Commons** | The movement of goods is governed by the same regenerative principles as passenger movement, with transparency across supply chains |
| **Indigenous Transportation Sovereignty** | BAZ-level authority over transportation in traditional territories, with Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC 2.0) |

## 3. Governance Architecture

### 3.1 The Global Mobility Council (GMC)

**Role:** Sets global standards for decarbonization, equity, interoperability, and safety. Coordinates transnational corridors. Resolves disputes.

**Composition:**

| Constituency | Representation |
|--------------|----------------|
| **BAZs** | Elected representatives from regional BAZ federations |
| **Indigenous Nations** | Appointed by the Earth Council, with veto over matters affecting traditional territories |
| **Youth** | Permanent seats with decision-making authority; suspensive veto over projects with 50+ year lifespans |
| **Disability Advocates** | Appointed by the Global Disability Alliance |
| **Informal Transport Workers** | Elected by cooperatives and unions (boda bodas, taxi associations, etc.) |
| **Freight & Logistics** | Representatives from port workers, rail unions, and supply chain cooperatives |
| **Technical Experts** | Appointed by the Global Technology Council for term-limited advisory roles |

**Decision-Making:**

- **Standards:** Consensus among BAZ representatives; supermajority (2/3) for major changes
- **Transnational Corridors:** Affected BAZs must approve; Indigenous veto applies
- **Disputes:** Values-Based Conflict Transformation protocol, with the Digital Justice Tribunal as final arbiter

**Standing Committees:**

| Committee | Mandate |
|-----------|---------|
| **Freight & Logistics Committee** | Oversees freight decarbonization targets, green corridor standards, port transition, and supply chain transparency. Co-chaired by port worker and rail union representatives. Sets freight-specific metrics and reports to full GMC quarterly. |
| **Infrastructure & Design Committee** | Reviews Planetary Health and Community & Cultural Impact Assessments. Coordinates the Global Pathways Initiative. |
| **Ceremonial Governance Committee** | Co-chaired by Indigenous representative. Manages Phase 1 scoping processes and Red Lines registry. |
| **Justice & Access Committee** | Oversees universal access audits, reparations fund, and gender/kinship equity compliance. |

### 3.2 Subsidiarity Bridge: How the GMC Relates to Existing Authorities

| Level | Authority | Relationship to GMC |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Global** | GMC sets decarbonization, equity, and interoperability standards | Primary standard-setting body |
| **Transnational** | Regional bodies coordinate cross-border corridors (e.g., EU, ASEAN, African Union) | Implement GMC standards regionally |
| **National** | DOTs, aviation authorities, port authorities regulate safety and licensing | Must comply with GMC standards to access BAZ markets |
| **BAZ** | Control land use, infrastructure siting, local mobility plans | Adopt GMC standards; use procurement power to enforce compliance |
| **Local** | Operate transit systems, manage streets | Implement BAZ plans |

**Enforcement Mechanism:** BAZs control land use and procurement. If a national DOT or corporation wants to operate within a BAZ, they must meet BAZ-adopted GMC standards. This creates bottom-up enforcement without requiring the GMC to replace existing authorities.

### 3.3 Ceremonial Governance: Operational Protocol

Ceremonial governance is not vague aspiration. It is the binding Phase 1 scoping process for any major infrastructure project affecting Indigenous territories or sacred sites.

**Three-Phase Process:**

| Phase | Activity | Output |
|-------|----------|--------|
| **Phase 1: Ceremonial Scoping** | Indigenous-led talking circles, elder testimony, community consultations. Facilitated by the BAZ, with resources provided for full participation. Duration: 3-12 months depending on complexity | Recorded "Red Lines" — places that cannot be crossed, routes that must be honored, timing restrictions for ceremonial seasons |
| **Phase 2: Technical Planning** | Engineers and planners design within Red Line constraints. Any proposed deviation requires returning to Phase 1 | Infrastructure plan that respects Red Lines, with documented consultation |
| **Phase 3: Binding Resolution** | If consensus cannot be reached, the Digital Justice Tribunal resolves with a presumption in favor of the community with the deepest documented cultural connection to the land. All decisions are appealable only on procedural grounds | Final binding decision; implementation proceeds or halts accordingly |

**What Ceremonial Governance Does:**
- Establishes constraints that engineers must work within
- Gives communities binding authority over what cannot be done
- Creates a clear, predictable process for developers

**What Ceremonial Governance Does Not Do:**
- Replace engineering with ritual
- Require consensus on technical specifications
- Delay projects indefinitely without resolution

## 4. Regenerative Infrastructure

### 4.1 The Global Pathways Initiative

Funded by the Global Commons Fund, with mandates for long-term maintenance, 5-year climate resilience audits, and redundant pathway networks for crisis resilience.

| Infrastructure Type | Description | Priority |
|---------------------|-------------|----------|
| **Global High-Speed Rail** | Connects population centers, replaces short-haul aviation. Wildlife corridors and permaculture-integrated designs | High |
| **Regional Rail & Transit** | Electrified rail networks connecting BAZs and rural areas | High |
| **Non-Motorized Networks** | Pedestrian pathways and cycle networks in all BAZs; separated from motor traffic | High |
| **EV & Hydrogen Infrastructure** | Universal charging/refueling networks with permeable, pollution-filtering materials | Medium |
| **Sustainable Maritime Corridors** | Green corridors for ammonia/hydrogen/battery-powered ships; supports Just Transition for port workers | Medium |
| **Living Infrastructure** | Bridges as wildlife corridors, terraced permaculture walls, aquifer-recharging road surfaces. Projects earn Leaves in the Love Ledger | High where applicable |

### 4.2 Bioregional Design Protocols

All infrastructure projects undergo two assessments:

| Assessment | Purpose | Approval Body |
|------------|---------|---------------|
| **Planetary Health Impact Assessment** | Evaluates carbon footprint, biodiversity impacts, water use, and alignment with Biosphere Health Index | BAZ Environmental Council |
| **Community & Cultural Impact Assessment** | Evaluates impacts on communities, including Indigenous consultation (FPIC 2.0), accessibility, and cultural heritage | BAZ Community Council + affected communities |

**Design Requirements:**
- Corridors follow watershed boundaries where possible, not arbitrary political borders
- Wildlife crossings integrated every 5 km in ecologically sensitive areas
- Seasonal adaptation protocols for weather extremes
- Ancestral route protection: where traditional travel routes exist, they are preserved or incorporated

### 4.3 Emergency Mobility Activation

For climate disasters and humanitarian crises:

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Disaster Response Mobile Units** | Pre-positioned fleets for rapid deployment |
| **Climate Migration Transportation Corridors** | Pre-planned routes for safe relocation |
| **Coordination** | Integrated with Crisis Command Protocol and Migration & Human Mobility Framework |

## 5. Freight & Logistics Protocol

The movement of goods is governed by the same regenerative principles as passenger movement. This protocol fills the gap left by frameworks that focus only on moving people.

### 5.1 Principles

| Principle | Meaning |
|-----------|---------|
| **Freight as Commons** | Supply chains are transparent, accountable, and serve community needs, not just corporate efficiency |
| **Shortest Path First** | Where possible, goods move through the shortest, lowest-emissions route; long-haul trucking replaced by rail and ship |
| **Last-Mile Regeneration** | Urban freight shifts to cargo bikes, electric micro-vehicles, and micro-distribution hubs |

### 5.2 Infrastructure Transition

| Mode | Current | Target (2035) |
|------|---------|---------------|
| **Ocean Shipping** | Heavy fuel oil | Sail-freight, wind-assisted propulsion, green ammonia/hydrogen |
| **Rail Freight** | Diesel | 100% electrified |
| **Long-Haul Trucking** | Diesel | Replaced by rail where possible; remaining fleet electric or hydrogen |
| **Last-Mile Delivery** | Diesel vans | Cargo bikes, electric micro-vehicles, micro-distribution hubs |
| **Air Freight** | Jet fuel | Sustainable aviation fuel for essential goods; reduced volume |

### 5.3 Supply Chain Transparency

- **Digital Product Passports:** (from Gaian Trade Framework) track transportation emissions per good, from origin to destination
- **Consumers and BAZs** can choose lower-emissions supply chains
- **Carbon Accounting:** Freight emissions are visible, accountable, and subject to reduction targets

### 5.4 Worker Transition

- **Port Workers:** Transition to green shipping infrastructure, with retraining and AUBI support
- **Long-Haul Truckers:** Retraining for rail operations, local delivery, or regenerative logistics roles
- **Logistics Cooperatives:** Ownership transitions where communities take control of last-mile delivery

## 6. The Mobility Economy

### 6.1 Integration with AUBI & Hearts/Leaves

| Mechanism | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Hearts for Sustainable Mobility** | Users earn Hearts for choosing low-emissions transport (trains, e-bikes, walking). Redeemable for reduced fares, local currency, or community projects |
| **Leaves for Infrastructure** | Projects that incorporate living infrastructure (wildlife corridors, permeable surfaces) earn Leaves |
| **Governance** | Social Resilience Council oversees Hearts/Leaves distribution; transparent, audited |

### 6.2 Mobility as a Service (MaaS) Commons

An open-source platform for integrated mobility planning:

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Journey Planning** | Combines all transport options (rail, bus, bike-share, ride-share) into single interface |
| **Emissions Tracking** | Users see carbon impact of each trip option |
| **Algorithmic Transparency** | Audited by Office of Algorithmic Accountability; AI optimizes for Biosphere Health Index, not corporate profit |
| **Data Sovereignty** | User data controlled per Aurora Accord; no surveillance or sale |
| **Interoperability Mandate** | Proprietary platforms (ride-share, navigation, journey planning) operating within BAZs must publish open APIs and feed real-time data into the MaaS Commons. Non-compliant platforms lose BAZ operating licenses. |

**Adoption Strategy Against Incumbents**

The MaaS Commons faces entrenched proprietary platforms (Google Maps, Uber, Citymapper) with powerful network effects. The adoption strategy does not attempt to outcompete these platforms on convenience from day one. Instead:

- **Phase 1:** BAZ-operated transit systems (buses, rail, bike-share) are *exclusively* bookable through the MaaS Commons. Proprietary platforms may display schedules but cannot process fares for BAZ transit. This creates a baseline user base.
- **Phase 2:** The interoperability mandate forces proprietary platforms to feed data into the Commons, making it the most comprehensive planning tool. Users migrate because it has the most complete information, not because they are forced to.
- **Phase 3:** As community-owned transit expands, the share of journeys only available through the Commons grows organically.

The goal is not to ban proprietary platforms but to ensure they operate within the Commons data ecosystem rather than outside it.

### 6.3 Transportation Reparations Fund

A Global Commons Fund portfolio addressing historical harms:

| Priority | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Highway Displacement** | Communities divided by 20th-century highway construction receive priority funding for reconnection (caps, boulevards, greenways) |
| **Transit Deserts** | Communities historically excluded from transit investment receive first access to new infrastructure |
| **Governance** | Co-chaired by GMC and Gender & Kinship Justice Council |

### 6.4 Community Ownership Models

| Model | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Cooperative Transit** | Local bus, rail, and bike-share systems owned by workers and riders |
| **Municipal Ownership** | Cities and BAZs own transit infrastructure, operating as public good |
| **Transition Pathways** | Clear legal and financial mechanisms for converting private/state systems to community ownership |

## 8. Implementation & Metrics

### 8.1 Implementation Roadmap

| Phase | Timeframe | Activities |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Phase 1: Pilots** | 2026-2028 | Establish GMC and standing committees; launch 5-10 BAZ pilots (including Indigenous-led, rural, urban, and freight-focused); test ceremonial governance protocol; develop MaaS Commons prototype; pilot cargo bike hubs and freight-to-rail transfer terminals; negotiate interoperability agreements with proprietary platforms |
| **Phase 2: Scaling** | 2028-2032 | Expand to 50 BAZs; build first transnational passenger and freight corridors; electrify pilot rail freight routes; scale freight transition with green shipping corridors; implement reparations fund; MaaS Commons live in all pilot BAZs |
| **Phase 3: Global** | 2032-2040 | Full network; fossil-free by default; 50% of transit under community ownership; 70% of freight ton-km on zero-emissions modes; MaaS Commons as standard platform |

### 8.2 Metrics for Success

| Category | Metric | Target (2040) |
|----------|--------|---------------|
| **Decarbonization** | Transport emissions reduction (vs. 2025) | 80% reduction |
| **Proximity** | Population within 15-minute walk of daily needs | 70% |
| **Access Equity** | Population within 15-minute walk of affordable transit | 95% |
| **Universal Access** | Disability Impact Assessment (DIA) scores | 90% compliant |
| **Freight Decarbonization** | Ton-kilometers moved by zero-emissions modes | 70% |
| **Freight Transparency** | Goods with Digital Product Passport tracking transport emissions | 80% |
| **Community Ownership** | Transit systems under cooperative/municipal control | 50% |
| **Indigenous Sovereignty** | Projects with FPIC 2.0 completed | 100% of affected projects |
| **Worker Transition** | Fossil fuel transport workers transitioned to regenerative roles | 100% |
| **MaaS Adoption** | BAZ transit journeys bookable through MaaS Commons | 90% |

**Metric Interpretation Note: Proximity and Transit**

The Proximity and Access Equity metrics interact. If Proximity Priority succeeds — communities redesigned so daily needs are walkable — then transit ridership in those areas should *decline*. This is a success signal, not a failure. Transit metrics should be evaluated alongside proximity metrics: declining ridership in high-proximity BAZs indicates that forced mobility is being eliminated. Rising ridership should only be celebrated in areas where it replaces car dependence, not where it indicates persistent poor urban design. The Transparency Dashboard should display these metrics in tandem, with contextual interpretation.

### 8.3 Freight-Specific Intermediate Targets

| Mode | Phase 1 (2028) | Phase 2 (2032) | Phase 3 (2040) |
|------|----------------|----------------|----------------|
| **Ocean Shipping** | 5 green corridors operational (wind-assisted, green ammonia) | 30% of major routes using zero-emissions propulsion | 70% zero-emissions |
| **Rail Freight** | 3 pilot BAZ corridors fully electrified | 50% of freight rail electrified | 100% electrified |
| **Last-Mile Delivery** | Cargo bike hubs in 10 BAZs | Cargo bike/electric micro-vehicle hubs in 50 BAZs | Standard last-mile mode in all BAZs |
| **Long-Haul Trucking** | Freight-to-rail transfer terminals in 5 pilot corridors | 30% of long-haul ton-km shifted to rail | 70% shifted; remaining fleet electric/hydrogen |
| **Supply Chain Transparency** | Digital Product Passport piloted for 100 goods | 50% of BAZ-traded goods tracked | 80% of all goods tracked |

### Appendix D: Relationship to Existing Transportation Authorities

*This framework does not replace existing transportation authorities. It creates coordination and accountability.*

| Authority | Relationship |
|-----------|-------------|
| **National DOTs** | Retain safety, licensing, and operational authority. GMC standards apply for access to BAZ markets. |
| **FAA / EASA / ICAO** | Retain aviation safety authority. GMC sets decarbonization and equity standards for access to BAZ airports. |
| **IMO (International Maritime)** | Retains shipping safety authority. GMC sets green corridor standards; port authorities adopt. |
| **Port Authorities** | Retain operational authority. Must meet GMC decarbonization standards to receive Global Commons Fund support. |
| **BAZs** | Gain new authority: procurement standards can require GMC compliance from operators seeking to use BAZ land or funds. |

**Principle:** The GMC does not replace. It sets standards that BAZs adopt, and BAZs use their existing authority (land use, procurement) to enforce them. This creates a pathway from current reality to the commons without requiring immediate global agreement.
