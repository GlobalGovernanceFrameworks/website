## Executive constitutional summary

The Living Shield supports communities, Indigenous nations, municipalities, public agencies, infrastructure operators, civil-society organizations, and mutual-aid networks in reducing disaster risk before emergencies occur. It provides common interfaces for multi-hazard assessment, accessible early warning, pre-arranged finance, evacuation support, infrastructure continuity, ecosystem-based protection, mutual aid, and just recovery.

The strongest insight of v4.3 remains: disaster governance should invest before loss, not only compensate after it. Early warning, community sensors, safe shelters, resilient infrastructure, ecological restoration, accessible communication, local knowledge, and forecast-based finance can prevent routine hazards from becoming cascading crises.

This framework does **not** create a planetary disaster authority. It does not require every Bioregional Autonomous Zone to establish a council, grant a council a land-use veto, impose a universal duty of care, levy a reinsurance tax, change sovereign debt terms, order evacuation, command responders, declare an emergency, activate the Planetary Immune System, or adjudicate negligence. Consequential actions require an independently valid source of authority and the records required by the Emergency, Catastrophic-Risk, and Continuity Interface Specification.

> **Preparedness should make lawful action faster. It should not make constitutional boundaries disappear.**

The successor separates eight functions that v4.3 partly combined:

1. hazard observation and plural state estimation;
2. ordinary planning and infrastructure regulation;
3. accessible warning and public information;
4. pre-authorized forecast finance and social protection;
5. requested mutual aid and operational assistance;
6. lawful emergency declaration and power activation;
7. adjudication, liability, and remedy;
8. recovery, authority return, and institutional learning.

No body controls the full chain.

# 1. Purpose, status, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

The Living Shield provides interoperable design and operating interfaces for:

- multi-hazard risk assessment;
- local, Indigenous, regional, and cross-border preparedness;
- early warning and accessible communication;
- infrastructure and essential-service continuity;
- forecast-based financing and anticipatory action;
- evacuation, shelter, and livelihood protection;
- ecosystem-based disaster-risk reduction;
- humanitarian logistics and mutual aid;
- just recovery, reconstruction, and restoration;
- cascading-failure analysis;
- after-action learning and ordinary-capacity reform.

## 1.2 Status

This document is a governance framework and constitutional interface. It may be adopted through domestic law, Indigenous law, municipal or regional charter, Treaty protocol, compact, contract, professional rule, infrastructure licence, fiscal instrument, mutual-aid agreement, or another valid source.

Its templates do not become binding merely because a GGF institution, technical body, funder, platform, insurer, or participating community publishes or endorses them.

## 1.3 Non-authority clause

This framework does not itself:

- create disaster-management jurisdiction;
- declare an emergency or disaster;
- order evacuation, shelter-in-place, exclusion, demolition, seizure, requisition, or use of force;
- establish land-use powers, building codes, industrial duties, offences, civil liability, fines, or debt penalties;
- establish a mandatory BAZ Resilience Council or universal representation formula;
- impose a tax, levy, bond, insurance obligation, procurement rule, or AUBI payment;
- authorize a Planetary Health Council, Living Shield body, model, dashboard, or fund to command public institutions;
- transfer authority from a state, municipality, Indigenous nation, professional regulator, infrastructure operator, court, or humanitarian organization;
- activate the Planetary Immune System;
- condition essential aid, AUBI Layer 1, justice, housing, healthcare, or rescue on political participation or GGF alignment.

> **No valid authority record, no consequential disaster action.**

## 1.4 No performance-to-jurisdiction conversion

A body does not acquire authority because it predicts hazards accurately, funds useful projects, responds rapidly, earns public trust, operates across borders, or coordinates many institutions.

Demonstrated competence may justify voluntary replication, procurement, proposed legislation, or delegated functions. It does not itself transfer jurisdiction.

# 2. Relationship to the emergency constitutional interface

## 2.1 Controlling sequence

All emergency-capable functions follow the common sequence:

1. ordinary prevention and preparedness;
2. non-binding alert;
3. plural state estimation;
4. standing and jurisdiction review;
5. declaration by a competent authority where required;
6. action-specific power activation;
7. bounded operational coordination;
8. review at the applicable 72-hour, 30-day, and 90-day points;
9. termination, authority return, restoration, and compensation;
10. after-action learning and transition to ordinary governance.

## 2.2 Severity does not create authority

The number of jurisdictions affected, percentage of population exposed, estimated economic loss, hazard category, probability, or catastrophic potential may affect urgency and coordination. It does not determine who may declare, command, regulate, spend, evacuate, adjudicate, or enforce.

## 2.3 Declaration does not activate every power

A declaration must be followed by a **Power Activation and Prohibition Schedule** identifying each power activated, its operator, territory, population, objective, limits, review, expiry, and excluded powers.

## 2.4 No direct PIS escalation

The Living Shield may transmit a Hazard Alert or Catastrophic-Risk Review Request to PIS v2.2. The PIS may support foresight, dependency mapping, exercises, or coordination. It cannot take over disaster governance or inherit unassigned authority.

## 2.5 Ordinary governance remains primary

Building standards, zoning, ecosystem management, drainage, fire prevention, public health, infrastructure maintenance, social protection, insurance, occupational safety, and emergency services belong primarily to ordinary law and administration. Emergency governance must not become a substitute for long-term investment or democratic planning.

# 3. Governing principles

1. **Prevention before emergency:** Avoidable exposure, fragile infrastructure, ecological degradation, and exclusion should be reduced through ordinary governance.
2. **Subsidiarity with mutual aid:** Response remains at the lowest competent level while higher levels provide requested support, interoperability, finance, and surge capacity.
3. **Rights-preserving preparedness:** Accessibility, bodily integrity, mobility, housing, livelihood, privacy, culture, and due process are preparedness requirements, not post-crisis additions.
4. **Plural observation:** No model, sensor network, agency, company, insurer, dashboard, or knowledge system determines hazard status alone.
5. **Action-specific authority:** Warning, finance, evacuation, land-use control, infrastructure operation, relief, and enforcement require different mandates.
6. **Forecast support without punishment:** Good-faith anticipatory action remains legitimate when a forecasted event does not occur.
7. **Community capability without manufactured sovereignty:** Communities should shape preparedness, but a framework cannot create local constitutional authority by naming a council.
8. **Indigenous authority and protected non-transfer:** Indigenous peoples determine territorial, knowledge, data, cultural, and emergency interfaces affecting them.
9. **Accessible warning and safe response:** Information, transport, shelter, care, and recovery must work for disabled people, children, older people, migrants, people without digital access, and people with animals or caregiving duties.
10. **Worker and responder protection:** Preparedness and response do not justify unpaid labour, unsafe deployment, or retaliation for refusal and whistleblowing.
11. **Ecological and social continuity:** Risk reduction should protect ecosystems, livelihoods, community relations, and future options rather than merely move risk elsewhere.
12. **Failure may stop escalation:** The lawful result may be continued observation, voluntary measures, redesigned plans, non-scaling, or dissolution.

# 4. Scope and hazard architecture

## 4.1 Hazard domains

The framework applies to:

- climate and weather hazards;
- geophysical hazards;
- wildfire and landscape hazards;
- technological and industrial accidents;
- infrastructure and utility failures;
- biological hazards through the Global Health and Aethelred interfaces;
- environmental contamination;
- food, water, housing, logistics, and supply-chain disruption;
- compound and cascading events;
- cross-border disasters;
- slow-onset emergencies where ordinary institutions remain the primary response.

## 4.2 Event classes

Each event should be described without prematurely converting uncertainty into emergency status:

1. background condition;
2. anomaly or watch condition;
3. hazard alert;
4. credible near-term threat;
5. realized local event;
6. multi-jurisdiction event;
7. declared emergency under valid law;
8. cascading or potentially catastrophic event;
9. recovery and residual-risk phase;
10. unresolved or disputed event.

## 4.3 Cascading risk

Assessment should identify dependencies among:

- power, water, sanitation, communications, transport, and health;
- food, fuel, medicine, finance, housing, and care;
- dams, industrial facilities, ports, data centres, and hazardous-material sites;
- ecosystems and protective landscapes;
- public institutions, vendors, and critical labour;
- neighbouring jurisdictions and cross-border systems.

A cascading-failure estimate is not itself an instruction to centralize command.

## 4.4 Slow-onset conditions

Drought, sea-level rise, land degradation, heat, displacement pressure, and infrastructure decay may require long-term planning, investment, migration policy, and social protection. Repeated emergency declarations should not replace ordinary law.

# 5. Separation of functions

| Function | Permitted role | Must remain separate from |
|---|---|---|
| Hazard observation | collect signals, monitor conditions, issue non-binding alerts | declaration, coercive action, liability findings |
| State estimation | compare models, local observations, and uncertainty | command, finance authorization, adjudication |
| Planning and regulation | adopt zoning, building, safety, and continuity duties under valid law | judicial determination and emergency command |
| Warning | communicate risks and recommended protective action | covert persuasion, political narrative control |
| Forecast finance | release pre-authorized support under a valid instrument | emergency declaration and debt punishment |
| Operational assistance | perform requested rescue, logistics, shelter, or restoration | residual jurisdiction over the host territory |
| Declaration and activation | activate legally defined powers | self-triggering by the observing body without safeguards |
| Justice and remedy | decide legality, liability, compensation, and appeal | operational deployment and investigation |
| Evaluation | assess outcomes and learning | automatic continuation, funding, or sanction |

Where one institution lawfully performs more than one function, it shall disclose the combination, conflicts, safeguards, review, and reasons separation is impracticable.

# 6. Federated institutional architecture

## 6.1 The Living Shield network

The Living Shield is a federated network of participating public authorities, Indigenous authorities, mutual-aid organizations, professional bodies, infrastructure operators, research institutions, community organizations, funders, and humanitarian actors.

The network may:

- maintain templates and interoperability standards;
- support training, exercises, and peer learning;
- match voluntary assistance offers and requests;
- host non-binding hazard and readiness research;
- support accessible communication resources;
- document implementation experience;
- maintain a public catalogue of separately constituted finance facilities and mutual-aid agreements.

It may not declare emergencies, command participants, impose standards with legal effect, allocate public funds without mandate, adjudicate disputes, or represent non-participating communities.

## 6.2 Territorial resilience bodies

A state, municipality, BAZ, Indigenous nation, or other lawful territorial authority may create, recognize, or contract with a resilience body.

Its charter should state:

- constituting authority;
- territory and jurisdiction;
- composition and selection;
- advisory, administrative, regulatory, or operational powers;
- relation to Indigenous jurisdiction and existing emergency services;
- conflicts of interest;
- funding and audit;
- appeal and complaint;
- continuity, transfer, and dissolution.

No universal composition formula or land-use veto arises from this framework.

## 6.3 Planetary Health interface

Planetary Health institutions may contribute ecological observations, cross-boundary risk analysis, and system-health context. They do not become disaster declarers, land-use authorities, arbitrators, or PIS activation authorities unless a valid instrument separately assigns such a function.

## 6.4 Finance facilities

Resilience pools, public funds, insurers, development banks, mutual-aid reserves, and community funds remain separately constituted fiscal actors. Their mandates, contribution rules, investment policies, payout triggers, conflicts, audit, and dissolution must be established independently.

## 6.5 Justice and enforcement

Courts and tribunals operate under Justice Systems v1.4 and the applicable legal order. A Living Shield alert, conditions profile, or technical recommendation is evidence to be assessed—not a judgment, warrant, fine, or enforcement order.

# 7. Multi-Hazard Conditions Profile

## 7.1 Replacement of the Community Resilience Score

The composite Community Resilience Score is retired as a universal control instrument. Resilience cannot be reduced to one number that simultaneously determines credit, fiscal eligibility, governance quality, emergency status, and community worth.

The successor uses a **Multi-Hazard Conditions Profile** with separately reported dimensions.

## 7.2 Minimum dimensions

A profile may include:

- hazard exposure;
- warning coverage and accessibility;
- shelter and evacuation capacity;
- critical-infrastructure condition;
- ecosystem protection and degradation;
- health and care continuity;
- food, water, energy, transport, and communications dependencies;
- household financial and livelihood vulnerability;
- disability and accessibility gaps;
- institutional and workforce capacity;
- insurance and fiscal space;
- data, model, and observation limitations;
- unresolved jurisdictional and Indigenous interfaces.

## 7.3 No social-cohesion credit score

Social cohesion, trust, political agreement, cultural conformity, or institutional approval shall not determine bond pricing, emergency status, public benefit eligibility, or the legal rights of a community.

Qualitative community conditions may inform support design where voluntarily and lawfully collected, but they must not become a generalized governance score.

## 7.4 Profile use

A profile may support planning, funding proposals, mutual-aid design, training priorities, and public explanation. It does not automatically:

- trigger an emergency;
- activate finance unless a valid instrument pre-authorizes that trigger;
- authorize land-use restrictions;
- create negligence;
- reduce future assistance;
- determine insurance or debt terms;
- establish jurisdiction.

# 8. Multi-hazard risk registers and planning

## 8.1 Ordinary planning instrument

A competent territorial authority may require or maintain a Multi-Hazard Risk Register. The register should identify locally material hazards rather than satisfy a universal quota.

## 8.2 Minimum fields

The register should record:

- responsible authority;
- territory and affected populations;
- hazards, exposures, and dependencies;
- observation sources and uncertainty;
- critical facilities and services;
- historical and projected conditions;
- infrastructure, ecological, social, and occupational risks;
- disability and accessibility implications;
- Indigenous, local, and cross-border interfaces;
- prevention and preparedness measures;
- residual risks and contested assumptions;
- review and amendment schedule.

## 8.3 Public and protected layers

Public risk information should be accessible. Sensitive infrastructure details, personal data, security vulnerabilities, protected Indigenous knowledge, and confidential operational information require layered access under Aurora v1.3.

## 8.4 Participation

Planning should include affected residents, workers, disabled people, local service providers, land and infrastructure operators, and affected Indigenous nations. Participation does not require public disclosure of protected knowledge or universal consensus.

# 9. Observation, early warning, and plural state estimation

## 9.1 Observation sources

Hazard observation may include:

- meteorological, hydrological, seismic, geological, oceanic, ecological, and air-quality systems;
- community and occupational reports;
- infrastructure telemetry;
- remote sensing;
- public-health and biological signals;
- local and Indigenous observation where invited and authorized;
- scientific models and historical records.

## 9.2 Independence map

Material decisions should identify common dependencies among models and observations. Two products derived from the same sensor network, vendor, dataset, or model family are not necessarily independent confirmations.

## 9.3 State estimate

A Multi-Hazard State Estimate should state:

- plausible event and affected area;
- time horizon;
- probability, confidence, or qualitative uncertainty;
- severity and reversibility;
- observation sources and disagreement;
- model limitations;
- possible action and inaction harms;
- vulnerable populations and essential dependencies;
- competent authorities;
- next review time.

## 9.4 Alerts are not declarations

An alert may recommend attention or voluntary protective action. It does not itself create legal emergency status, compulsory evacuation, facility control, fiscal authority, or PIS activation.

# 10. Forecast-based financing and anticipatory action

## 10.1 Purpose

Forecast-based finance releases resources before or at the earliest stage of an event so people can protect life, health, housing, livelihoods, animals, infrastructure, and essential supplies.

## 10.2 Pre-authorization

Automatic or accelerated disbursement is valid only where a separately lawful fiscal instrument has already defined:

- funding source and custodian;
- eligible territory and population;
- covered hazards and actions;
- trigger model or decision process;
- required independence and confidence;
- geographic precision;
- accessibility and vulnerability rules;
- authorized recipients and payment channels;
- fraud and error processes;
- public reporting and audit;
- correction, suspension, and appeal;
- expiry and review.

## 10.3 Trigger design

A universal 75-percent probability threshold is not required. Appropriate triggers depend on forecast horizon, model calibration, hazard type, action cost, reversibility, false-positive consequences, and vulnerable populations.

Triggers may combine models, observations, predefined forecast windows, and responsible human review. Indigenous and local evidence may inform the decision where authorized without being forced into one numerical weight.

## 10.4 Good-faith false alarms

A forecasted event that does not occur is not presumptively a failure. Preparatory spending may still protect people, test systems, replenish supplies, reduce exposure, or reveal readiness gaps.

Future support shall not be reduced, recouped, or penalized merely because a properly authorized forecast did not materialize. Recovery may occur for fraud, intentional misuse, or a defined operational breach through due process.

## 10.5 Eligible support

Pre-authorized support may include:

- cash or in-kind household assistance;
- evacuation transport and accommodation;
- animal and livelihood protection;
- medicine, cooling, food, water, fuel, and communications;
- worker mobilisation and safety;
- pre-positioning and supply-chain action;
- temporary accessibility measures;
- local mutual-aid grants;
- infrastructure stabilization.

## 10.6 No political conditionality

Forecast support cannot depend on Treaty accession, GGF membership, public approval, surveillance consent unrelated to the payment, adoption of one institutional model, or surrender of Indigenous authority.

# 11. Community sensor and observation networks

## 11.1 Local capability

Community-owned or locally governed sensors can improve warning, accountability, and maintenance. Networks should be designed around local usefulness, repairability, offline operation, accessible interfaces, and clear responsibility.

## 11.2 Authority and ownership

The network charter should identify:

- equipment ownership;
- installation and land authority;
- maintenance responsibility;
- data categories and custodians;
- access and sharing rules;
- validation and calibration;
- public and protected outputs;
- complaint and correction;
- funding and replacement;
- shutdown and data disposition.

## 11.3 No compulsory dashboard feed

Participation in a community network does not require all raw data to flow to a global dashboard. Aggregation, local custody, delayed publication, protected attestation, or non-transfer may be appropriate.

## 11.4 Evidentiary status

Community observations may be important evidence. Their legal weight is determined by the competent forum under applicable evidence rules, not by automatic framework preference or dismissal.

# 12. Indigenous forecasting, knowledge, and territorial authority

## 12.1 Affected-nation authority

Where preparedness, monitoring, warning, land management, evacuation, shelter, ecological intervention, or reconstruction affects Indigenous territory, knowledge, data, culture, or sacred relations, the affected people or peoples identify their own authorities and applicable law.

## 12.2 No universal proxy

No Earth Council, quota, global Indigenous panel, BAZ body, or GGF-appointed representative may act for an affected people without that people’s authority.

## 12.3 Knowledge invitation and protected attestation

Relevant Indigenous knowledge may be invited through nation-specific interfaces. A people may choose:

- direct participation;
- protected attestation without disclosure of underlying knowledge;
- local-only custody;
- conditions on use and attribution;
- non-publication;
- non-training;
- refusal or non-engagement.

## 12.4 No forced equal weighting

Scientific models, local observations, and Indigenous knowledge may answer different questions and use non-comparable forms of evidence. The State Estimate should disclose how each source influenced the decision and preserve disagreement rather than force one weighting formula.

## 12.5 Immediate rescue

Narrow immediate rescue may occur under the Emergency Interface where delay would expose persons to imminent grave harm and no authorized representative can be reached. It does not authorize permanent monitoring, land control, knowledge extraction, relocation, or institutional precedent.

# 13. Accessibility, disability, and differentiated needs

## 13.1 Accessibility as core infrastructure

Preparedness and warning systems should support:

- multiple languages and plain language;
- sign-language, captioned, audio, visual, tactile, and easy-read formats;
- offline and low-bandwidth access;
- screen readers and accessible devices;
- cognitive accessibility;
- trusted intermediaries without forced disclosure;
- accessible transport, shelters, toilets, medicine storage, and communication.

## 13.2 Personal assistance and continuity

Plans should address personal assistants, caregivers, service animals, mobility devices, medical equipment, medication, refrigeration, charging, communication supports, and family unity.

## 13.3 No vulnerability ranking as lesser worth

Risk data may identify differentiated support needs. It shall not reduce rescue priority, legal standing, autonomy, or the value assigned to a person’s life.

## 13.4 Children and older people

Plans should include guardianship, family tracing, safeguarding, education continuity, elder care, consent, privacy, and protection from institutional abandonment.

# 14. Infrastructure resilience and essential-service continuity

## 14.1 Ordinary duties

Infrastructure hardening, redundancy, maintenance, interoperable backup, and safe failure belong primarily to ordinary regulation, licences, procurement, professional standards, contracts, and operator duties.

## 14.2 Critical-service continuity profile

Each material system should identify:

- essential function;
- operator and legal duty;
- critical dependencies;
- single points of failure;
- backup duration and substitution;
- workforce and vendor dependencies;
- cyber and physical risks;
- accessibility and household impacts;
- restoration priorities;
- manual and offline modes;
- authority for emergency operation or shutdown.

## 14.3 Conduit interface

The Conduit Protocol may provide infrastructure interoperability, distributed capacity, repair, and community-resilience interfaces. It does not receive emergency command or land-use authority through this framework.

## 14.4 Essential-service protection

Preparedness and response should protect water, sanitation, power, communications, healthcare, food, shelter, transport, finance, care, and public information. Suspension or rationing requires a valid basis, published criteria, accessibility, review, and restoration.

# 15. Ecosystem-based disaster-risk reduction

## 15.1 Scope

Ecosystem-based measures may include wetlands, mangroves, floodplains, forests, prescribed or cultural burning, watershed restoration, dunes, reefs, urban green infrastructure, and soil restoration.

## 15.2 Legal and ecological basis

Projects require valid land and resource authority, ecological evidence, affected-rights review, Indigenous interfaces, labour protections, long-term stewardship, and monitoring.

## 15.3 No knowledge appropriation

Indigenous and local practices are not automatically public techniques or global commons. Use requires the authority, attribution, benefit, custody, and non-transfer conditions set by knowledge holders.

## 15.4 Risk transfer

A measure should assess whether it transfers flood, fire, displacement, ecological, insurance, or livelihood risk to another place or population.

## 15.5 Compensation and transition

Where ordinary risk reduction restricts lawful activities, requires relocation, changes land use, or creates concentrated burdens, the constituting legal instrument should address transition, acquisition, compensation, livelihood protection, and appeal.

# 16. Technological and industrial hazards

## 16.1 Operator responsibility

Industrial facilities, dams, mines, chemical sites, nuclear and radiological installations, data centres, transport systems, and other hazardous operators may be subject to duties under valid law for:

- risk assessment;
- maintenance and containment;
- worker and community warning;
- emergency plans;
- monitoring and disclosure;
- financial assurance;
- independent inspection;
- incident reporting;
- remediation and compensation.

## 16.2 Technology-neutral safety outcomes

The framework does not universally mandate one kill switch, sensor, software system, blockchain, model, or containment method. Requirements should be risk-based, independently assessed, maintainable, and appropriate to the operator and jurisdiction.

## 16.3 Community right to know

Public information duties should balance health and safety with personal privacy, security-sensitive details, protected knowledge, and legitimate confidentiality. Communities need actionable risk information, not necessarily publication of every technical vulnerability.

## 16.4 Incident handoff

Technological incidents use the Emergency Interface and applicable Technology Governance, Aurora, Justice, Shield, health, environmental, and infrastructure protocols. Observation does not establish operator fault.

---

# 17. Biological hazard interface

Biological events are governed through Global Health v2.2 and, where biotechnology is involved, Aethelred v1.2.

Living Shield functions may support:

- shelter, logistics, continuity, and accessible communication;
- cross-hazard dependency mapping;
- water, sanitation, food, power, transport, and facility continuity;
- evacuation or movement support where lawfully authorized;
- recovery and livelihood protection.

Living Shield does not declare public-health emergencies, classify laboratory incidents with legal effect, order health measures, or activate the PIS.

# 18. Cascading failure and system dependency

## 18.1 Dependency map

A System Dependency and Cascade Map should identify:

- initiating hazards;
- affected functions;
- directional dependencies;
- thresholds and time-to-failure;
- substitutes and manual fallbacks;
- common vendors and infrastructure;
- geographic concentration;
- workforce and care dependencies;
- cross-border effects;
- restoration sequences;
- authorities responsible for each intervention.

## 18.2 Correlated observations

Risk models, insurance models, credit models, infrastructure dashboards, and emergency estimates may rely on the same underlying data. Correlation should be disclosed before treating multiple outputs as confirmation.

## 18.3 Option preservation

Preparedness should preserve multiple supply, communication, transport, shelter, and governance pathways. Centralized resilience infrastructure can itself create systemic fragility.

## 18.4 Exercise requirements

Exercises should include:

- false alarms;
- model disagreement;
- failure of digital systems;
- inaccessible shelters;
- host refusal of assistance;
- worker refusal and fatigue;
- Indigenous non-engagement;
- conflicting authorities;
- finance failure;
- recovery beyond ninety days;
- lawful non-escalation.

# 19. Land use, building safety, and planned risk reduction

## 19.1 Competent authority

Zoning, building codes, development restrictions, acquisition, managed retreat, retrofit mandates, and industrial siting require valid territorial and sectoral authority.

## 19.2 Evidence and procedure

A consequential planning measure should include:

- hazard and exposure evidence;
- uncertainty and alternatives;
- notice and accessible participation;
- affected Indigenous and property rights;
- worker and livelihood effects;
- housing and displacement consequences;
- proportionality and transition;
- review and appeal;
- duration and amendment.

## 19.3 Resilience body recommendations

A local resilience body may advise, investigate, or recommend within its charter. It may exercise binding land-use power only where a valid law or constituting authority grants that power.

## 19.4 No technical arbitration

A Planetary Health body or technical panel may provide evidence and recommendations. It does not issue binding arbitration between territorial authorities unless a valid agreement has constituted an arbitral function and preserved judicial review.

# 20. Disaster Risk and Continuity Duty options

## 20.1 Model-law status

The former “Planetary Duty of Care” is reframed as a family of **Disaster Risk and Continuity Duty** options for adoption through Treaty, domestic, Indigenous, municipal, sectoral, professional, or contractual instruments.

## 20.2 Possible duty elements

A valid instrument may require an operator or authority to:

- identify reasonably foreseeable hazards;
- maintain proportionate prevention and continuity plans;
- protect workers and affected communities;
- warn and disclose material risk;
- maintain safe systems and emergency capability;
- cooperate with lawful inspections and response;
- update plans after material changes and incidents;
- fund remediation or financial assurance;
- preserve evidence and report failures.

## 20.3 Standard of care

The standard should consider:

- magnitude and foreseeability of harm;
- operator knowledge and control;
- legal mandate and available resources;
- industry and professional standards;
- reasonable alternatives;
- accessibility and distributional impacts;
- public-authority constraints;
- emergency and post-emergency conduct.

## 20.4 Liability and defences

Negligence, causation, defences, immunities, contribution, public-authority liability, and remedies are determined through valid law and Justice Systems v1.4. The framework does not prescribe a universal fine or sovereign-debt penalty.

## 20.5 Standing and remedies

Adopting instruments should address standing for affected persons, workers, communities, Indigenous nations, public-interest bodies, and ecological representatives where applicable, together with injunction, repair, compensation, disclosure, and transition support.

# 21. Warning, evacuation, shelter, and protective movement

## 21.1 Accessible warning

Warnings should state:

- hazard and affected area;
- confidence and uncertainty;
- recommended action;
- timing and review;
- transport, shelter, and assistance;
- accessible and multilingual formats;
- official source and correction channel.

## 21.2 Voluntary evacuation support

Where time permits, authorities should make voluntary evacuation feasible through transport, income continuity, accessible shelter, medicine, caregiving support, animal and livelihood protection, and safe return planning.

## 21.3 Compulsory evacuation

Compulsory evacuation requires valid law and an Isolation, Evacuation, and Shelter Record stating:

- authority and factual predicate;
- area and population;
- necessity and alternatives;
- transport and accessible shelter;
- family and animal arrangements;
- property security and livelihood support;
- exceptions and professional duties;
- review, expiry, return, and compensation.

## 21.4 Shelter standards

Shelters should provide safety, accessibility, privacy, sanitation, healthcare, communications, safeguarding, gender-responsive arrangements, cultural and religious accommodation, family unity, service-animal access, complaint routes, and protection from exploitation.

## 21.5 Return and relocation

Authorities should distinguish temporary evacuation, delayed return, voluntary relocation, acquisition, and managed retreat. Emergency displacement does not itself create permanent land transfer or extinguish rights.

# 22. Mutual aid, humanitarian assistance, and operational coordination

## 22.1 Pre-negotiated mutual aid

Participants should establish agreements before emergencies covering:

- requests and acceptance;
- mission and geography;
- credentials and professional authority;
- command and coordination;
- worker safety and insurance;
- equipment and procurement;
- data and communication;
- liability and claims;
- security and neutrality;
- termination and handback.

## 22.2 Assistance without takeover

An assisting body operates within the host mandate and mission scope. It does not acquire general authority over the host territory, infrastructure, population, or recovery.

## 22.3 Immediate unsolicited rescue

Narrow life-saving assistance may be offered or performed where permitted by law and the Emergency Interface. It must be proportionate, non-extractive, reported promptly, and terminated or regularized when the immediate need ends.

## 22.4 Humanitarian neutrality

Aid may not be conditioned on political allegiance, military cooperation, surveillance unrelated to delivery, Treaty accession, or acceptance of permanent GGF institutions.

## 22.5 Multiple commands

Fire, health, infrastructure, humanitarian, policing, environmental, and military actors may retain separate lawful commands. Coordination does not require one supreme commander.

---

# 23. Social protection and anticipatory household support

## 23.1 AUBI interface

AUBI Layer 1 remains unconditional. Emergency or forecast support supplements rather than converts the baseline into a compliance instrument.

## 23.2 Pre-authorized surges

A fiscal authority may establish temporary surges through a valid instrument defining:

- eligible persons and territory;
- amount and form;
- trigger and duration;
- non-digital access;
- migration and residency treatment;
- caregiving and disability supplements;
- appeal and correction;
- non-recovery for good-faith receipt;
- transition at expiry.

This framework does not prescribe a universal amount such as 500 dollars per household.

## 23.3 Livelihood continuity

Support may cover paid leave, replacement income, food, housing, transport, childcare, livestock, tools, small enterprises, informal workers, displaced workers, and essential household debt relief.

## 23.4 No survival conditionality

Basic support cannot depend on Hearts, Leaves, volunteer service, preparedness scores, political behaviour, biometric identity, or use of one payment platform.

---

# 24. Just recovery and reconstruction

## 24.1 Recovery begins before impact

Plans should identify recovery authority, finance, procurement, debris and hazardous waste, housing, documentation, claims, worker needs, ecological restoration, cultural continuity, and public participation before disaster occurs.

## 24.2 Build safer, not simply “back better”

Reconstruction should reduce future risk without using disaster as a pretext for displacement, privatization, land enclosure, cultural erasure, labour exploitation, or environmentally harmful megaprojects.

## 24.3 Housing and land

Recovery should address tenants, informal residents, undocumented people, collective landholders, customary tenure, displaced communities, and people lacking conventional title. Emergency absence does not prove abandonment.

## 24.4 Local economic continuity

Procurement and finance should protect local enterprises, workers, care systems, cooperatives, and essential services while preventing corruption and capture.

## 24.5 Cultural and ecological restoration

Recovery may include archives, sacred sites, language and community spaces, ecosystems, livelihoods, and memorial practices under the authority of affected communities and peoples.

---

# 25. Resilience finance, bonds, insurance, and debt

## 25.1 No self-authorized levy

This framework does not impose a 0.5-percent reinsurance levy or any other tax. Contributions to a resilience facility require a valid fiscal, contractual, insurance, Treaty, or voluntary instrument.

## 25.2 Resilience finance network

Separately constituted facilities may coordinate grants, reserves, insurance, guarantees, contingent credit, anticipatory finance, debt relief, community funds, and reconstruction finance while retaining independent mandates.

## 25.3 Resilience bonds

A bond or guarantee should disclose:

- issuer and legal basis;
- project and beneficiaries;
- risk and return;
- public guarantees and contingent liabilities;
- performance measures;
- model and data assumptions;
- worker, community, environmental, and Indigenous safeguards;
- default and restructuring;
- conflicts and fees;
- audit and remedy.

Bond terms should not rely on one composite resilience score.

## 25.4 Insurance and parametric products

Parametric triggers may enable rapid payment but can create basis risk. Products should disclose trigger gaps, geographic precision, excluded losses, data dependencies, dispute resolution, accessibility, and treatment of model failure.

## 25.5 Debt protection

Preparedness gaps, low conditions-profile values, failed forecasts, or disaster occurrence shall not automatically increase sovereign, municipal, Indigenous, or community debt burdens.

Debt-service suspension, restructuring, grants, or contingent relief may be created through valid financial instruments. Relief should protect populations and essential services rather than reward creditors for public failure.

## 25.6 Finance independence

A funder or insurer may not convert financing conditions into emergency command, Indigenous authority, land control, public narrative control, or immunity from justice.

---

# 26. Data, privacy, records, and protected knowledge

## 26.1 Aurora interface

All personal, relational, location, disability, health, infrastructure, environmental, insurance, community, and Indigenous-restricted data follow Aurora Accord v1.3.

## 26.2 Data minimization

Preparedness and response should collect only data necessary for a defined purpose, authority, duration, and access group. Emergency convenience does not justify indefinite household, movement, disability, or vulnerability profiles.

## 26.3 Dashboard limits

Public dashboards should use aggregation, uncertainty, correction, accessibility, and layered disclosure. They should not expose household vulnerability, critical-system weaknesses, protected locations, or Indigenous knowledge.

## 26.4 Record separation

Planning data, emergency operational records, benefit records, insurance records, justice evidence, and research data should remain separated unless a lawful purpose and access basis supports linkage.

## 26.5 Post-emergency disposition

Termination requires deletion, return, sealing, archival transfer, or continued lawful retention for each category, together with notice and challenge where appropriate.

---

# 27. Technology, models, and automation

## 27.1 Technology Governance interface

Technology Governance v3.6 applies to sensors, warning platforms, digital twins, AI models, drones, automated decision systems, communication tools, and infrastructure controls.

## 27.2 Human and institutional responsibility

Automation may support detection, translation, routing, modelling, logistics, and anomaly review. It does not independently declare emergencies, order evacuation, deny benefits, determine negligence, or activate the PIS.

## 27.3 Model documentation

Material models should document:

- intended use and excluded use;
- training and observation sources;
- calibration and geographic limits;
- uncertainty and failure modes;
- correlated dependencies;
- accessibility and distributional impacts;
- operator and review responsibility;
- override, correction, and retirement.

## 27.4 Low-tech continuity

Essential warning, payment, shelter, and coordination should have offline or manual fallbacks appropriate to local conditions.

## 27.5 Cybersecurity

Security measures should be risk-based and technology-neutral. Incident response must preserve safety, evidence, service continuity, privacy, and lawful authority.

---

# 28. Public information, media, and correction

## 28.1 Synoptic interface

Public disaster communication follows Synoptic v4.2. Authorities and media may warn, authenticate, translate, explain, correct, disclose uncertainty, and preserve access.

## 28.2 No propaganda function

Living Shield institutions may not manufacture political narratives, covertly segment audiences, suppress lawful criticism, or use fear to secure institutional expansion.

## 28.3 Correction and uncertainty

Warnings and public estimates should have visible timestamps, sources, uncertainty, update channels, correction history, and contact routes.

## 28.4 Protected communication

Public transparency does not require publication of personal data, security vulnerabilities, protected knowledge, confidential humanitarian routes, or information likely to increase immediate harm.

## 28.5 Independent journalism

Public-interest media and journalists retain the right to investigate preparedness failures, government conduct, funders, operators, Living Shield participants, and GGF institutions.

---

# 29. Lawful escalation and the PIS interface

## 29.1 Escalation request

A competent local, Indigenous, national, regional, or sectoral authority may request broader review or assistance. A technical or coordination body may transmit a non-binding alert within its mandate.

## 29.2 No universal numerical trigger

The fact that an event affects more than ten BAZs, five percent of the world’s population, or another numerical threshold does not automatically create a Level 2 crisis or PIS authority.

## 29.3 Independent catastrophic-risk review

PIS v2.2 may prepare a Catastrophic-Risk State Estimate, dependency map, or coordination proposal. Declaration and activation remain with competent authorities under the Emergency Interface.

## 29.4 Action modules

Broader response may assemble separately authorized modules for:

- rescue and humanitarian logistics;
- infrastructure stabilization;
- health and biological response;
- environmental containment;
- cyber assistance;
- nuclear and radiological incidents;
- space and natural hazards;
- unarmed international assistance;
- separately lawful defensive deployment.

No referral activates all modules.

## 29.5 Time architecture

Provisional extraordinary action follows the applicable 72-hour review. Continued action requires renewal no longer than 30 days at a time. Action continuing beyond 90 cumulative days requires extraordinary review and a plan to transition into ordinary law or terminate.

---

# 30. Justice, liability, enforcement, and remedy

## 30.1 Justice remains separate

Courts and tribunals determine jurisdiction, evidence, legality, liability, remedy, appeal, and enforcement under Justice Systems v1.4 and applicable law.

## 30.2 Technical findings are not judgments

A hazard alert, conditions profile, audit, model, community report, insurer estimate, or Living Shield recommendation does not establish negligence, causation, offence, debt default, or entitlement to seize assets.

## 30.3 Protective and interim orders

A competent court or regulator may issue lawful interim orders where the required evidence, urgency, rights protection, duration, and review standards are met.

## 30.4 Remedies

Available remedies may include:

- prevention and infrastructure orders;
- warning and disclosure;
- repair and remediation;
- accessible services;
- compensation;
- restitution and rehabilitation;
- procurement or licence correction;
- worker and whistleblower protection;
- ecological restoration;
- public-law review;
- institutional redesign.

## 30.5 Wrongful disaster action

People harmed by wrongful evacuation, exclusion, demolition, requisition, benefit denial, data use, surveillance, shelter conditions, land transfer, or delayed return require an accessible restoration and compensation route.

---

# 31. Personnel, labour, volunteers, and responder protection

## 31.1 Emergency work record

Each mobilisation should identify employer or responsible authority, role, command, credentials, compensation, hours, safety, equipment, accommodation, insurance, family support, data duties, refusal, complaint, and demobilisation.

## 31.2 No unpaid moral conscription

Hearts, Leaves, civic honour, volunteer recognition, or community obligation do not replace wages and labour rights where work is directed, hazardous, regular, or institutionally necessary.

## 31.3 Volunteers

Volunteers need informed role choice, training, supervision, safety, expenses, insurance, privacy, and the right to stop. Volunteer status must not be used to evade employment obligations.

## 31.4 Professional autonomy and refusal

Workers and professionals may refuse unsafe, unlawful, discriminatory, or outside-scope tasks without retaliation, subject to valid and proportionate professional duties.

## 31.5 Demobilisation

After response, personnel should receive safe release, rest, mental-health and occupational support, credential closure, pay settlement, claims access, and transition to ordinary work.

---

# 32. Termination, authority restoration, and continuity

## 32.1 Termination triggers

Each activated power or mission must end when:

- its expiry arrives without lawful renewal;
- the factual predicate no longer exists;
- the objective is achieved or no longer attainable;
- a competent authority withdraws the mandate;
- a review body finds the action unlawful, unnecessary, or disproportionate;
- ordinary institutions can resume the function;
- continued action would create greater harm.

## 32.2 Termination certificate

Termination should record ended powers, residual risks, remaining services, personnel, assets, data, contracts, claims, public communication, and responsible successor authorities.

## 32.3 Authority and asset return

Temporary facilities, access rights, equipment, land, credentials, records, funds, and command relationships return to lawful holders or are disposed of under a valid plan.

## 32.4 Service continuity

Termination should not abruptly end shelter, health, food, water, income, accessibility, or protection where people still rely on them. Transitional service authority and finance must be explicit.

## 32.5 No emergency acquisition by inertia

Repeated assistance, successful operation, or prolonged occupation does not transfer permanent authority, property, land, data, infrastructure, or institutional control.

---

# 33. Monitoring, evaluation, learning, and anti-Goodhart safeguards

## 33.1 Evaluation domains

Evaluation should examine:

- preventable death and injury;
- warning reach and accessibility;
- evacuation and shelter safety;
- essential-service continuity;
- household and livelihood protection;
- ecological and cultural outcomes;
- worker safety;
- distribution of support and burden;
- model error and observation independence;
- rights impacts and wrongful action;
- speed of authority return;
- affected-community accounts;
- institutional learning and correction.

## 33.2 Prohibited control metrics

The following shall not automatically create power or determine rights:

- one composite resilience score;
- social cohesion or public trust;
- number of emergency declarations;
- number of land-use vetoes;
- PIS activation avoidance as an absolute target;
- forecast hit rate without action context;
- adoption of Living Shield institutions;
- public approval of authorities;
- debt or insurance pricing based on community worth.

## 33.3 False-alarm evaluation

Forecast performance should distinguish model error, prudent precaution, event avoidance caused by intervention, geographic miss, timing miss, communication failure, and implementation failure.

## 33.4 Failure reports

Independent after-action review should include affected people, workers, disabled people, Indigenous nations, technical experts, rights advocates, operators, and funders where relevant.

The review may conclude that:

- escalation was unnecessary;
- a warning was late or excessive;
- finance triggers were poorly designed;
- local action was sufficient;
- an institution should narrow, transfer, or dissolve;
- no common metric can validly compare the cases.

## 33.5 Learning does not transfer authority

Successful performance may justify proposed adoption or investment. It does not confer new regulatory, fiscal, judicial, territorial, or emergency power.

---

# 34. Implementation, pilots, amendment, and dissolution

## 34.1 Pre-entry use

Before Treaty or statutory adoption, participants may use voluntary templates for risk mapping, accessibility audits, mutual aid, exercises, forecast-finance design, community sensors, and recovery planning.

They may not use pilots to exercise unconstituted compulsory powers.

## 34.2 Pilot Constitutional Contract

Each pilot should state:

- participating authorities and communities;
- legal and voluntary basis;
- hazard and geography;
- tested functions;
- excluded powers;
- data and knowledge rules;
- worker and accessibility safeguards;
- finance and procurement;
- Indigenous interfaces;
- review and complaint;
- stop conditions;
- asset, data, and institutional disposition.

## 34.3 Suggested pilot sequence

1. accessible warning and household support;
2. multi-hazard conditions profiles without composite scoring;
3. community sensor and offline communication systems;
4. forecast-finance trigger simulation;
5. shelter and evacuation accessibility;
6. infrastructure dependency mapping;
7. ecosystem-based risk reduction;
8. mutual-aid and authority-return exercise;
9. false-alarm and non-escalation test;
10. recovery and wrongful-action claims exercise.

## 34.4 No required success or scale

A pilot may conclude that a model, financial product, sensor network, council, or intervention should remain local, be redesigned, not scale, or close.

## 34.5 Amendment

Amendments that alter legal powers require the same class of authority as the powers affected. Technical appendices may be updated under delegated procedures that preserve transparency, challenge, and versioning.

## 34.6 Withdrawal and dissolution

Participants may withdraw according to the constituting instrument, subject to safe continuity of active services, workers, records, contracts, claims, and protected knowledge.

Living Shield institutions are not presumed permanent and may dissolve without a successor where their functions are no longer needed or are better held elsewhere.

# 35. Constitutional readiness gates

A binding or consequential component is not ready unless all applicable gates pass.

1. **Authority gate:** the valid source for every consequential action is identified.
2. **Function-separation gate:** observation, planning, finance, declaration, command, justice, and evaluation are distinguished.
3. **No mandatory-council gate:** the framework does not manufacture territorial institutions or powers.
4. **Conditions-profile gate:** separate dimensions replace a composite governance score.
5. **No metric-to-power gate:** no index, threshold, or dashboard self-executes authority.
6. **Forecast-finance gate:** triggers, custodians, recipients, audit, correction, and expiry are pre-authorized.
7. **False-alarm gate:** good-faith recipients and communities are not punished for a forecast miss.
8. **No debt-punishment gate:** disaster exposure and preparedness gaps do not automatically worsen debt.
9. **Land-use gate:** zoning, building, retreat, and development controls rest on valid territorial authority.
10. **Duty-of-care gate:** any binding duty is adopted through valid law with standard, causation, defences, and remedies.
11. **Indigenous-authority gate:** affected peoples control their interfaces and protected knowledge.
12. **Accessibility gate:** warning, transport, shelter, benefits, and recovery are operationally accessible.
13. **Data gate:** Aurora-compliant authority, minimization, access, correction, and disposition exist.
14. **Observation-independence gate:** common model and sensor dependencies are disclosed.
15. **Infrastructure gate:** essential-service continuity and lawful operator authority are clear.
16. **Worker gate:** wages, safety, refusal, insurance, and demobilisation are protected.
17. **Evacuation gate:** compulsory movement has valid law, support, review, return, and compensation.
18. **Finance gate:** levies, bonds, insurance, AUBI surges, and debt relief use valid instruments.
19. **Communication gate:** warning and correction are separated from propaganda and political persuasion.
20. **PIS gate:** escalation is a request for independent review, never automatic takeover.
21. **Time gate:** the applicable 72-hour, 30-day, and 90-day reviews are implemented.
22. **Justice gate:** technical findings do not substitute for adjudication, liability, or enforcement.
23. **Restoration gate:** authority, assets, data, services, and rights have a return plan.
24. **Failure gate:** the system can lawfully choose non-escalation, redesign, non-scaling, or dissolution.

# 38. Conclusion

The Living Shield is strongest when it is not a hidden emergency constitution.

Its proper role is to help people see hazards sooner, reduce exposure, maintain essential systems, release support before loss, coordinate assistance, protect workers and disabled people, respect Indigenous authority, and recover without reproducing the conditions that created vulnerability.

A resilient society does not rely on a single score, council, dashboard, fund, or planetary commander. It builds many competent institutions with clear authority, interoperable records, mutual aid, accessible infrastructure, and the capacity to return extraordinary powers when the danger passes.

> **The shield is living because it learns, adapts, and remains distributed—not because it absorbs every authority around it.**
