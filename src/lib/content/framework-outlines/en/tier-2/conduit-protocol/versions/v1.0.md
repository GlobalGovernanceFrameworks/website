## 1. Executive Summary

Infrastructure is the circulatory system of civilization. Energy grids, data networks, and water systems determine who thrives and who is left behind. Today, this infrastructure is fragmented, privately controlled, fossil-fueled, and vulnerable — designed for extraction, not resilience.

The Conduit Protocol transforms infrastructure into a commons: publicly governed, community-owned, interoperable, and regenerative.

### The Core Problem

Essential infrastructure is controlled by monopolies and states that optimize for profit or power, not for universal access or ecological limits. Aging grids are vulnerable to climate shocks, cyber-attacks, and geopolitical disruption. Billions lack reliable access to clean energy, water, and connectivity.

### The Core Solution

| Pillar | What It Does |
|--------|-------------|
| **Transnational Grid Standards** | Open-source protocols ensuring all infrastructure is interoperable, resilient, and built to open standards — no vendor lock-in, no proprietary control |
| **Community Infrastructure Governance** | Bioregional Grid Authorities give communities co-governance over the infrastructure they depend on, with pathways to cooperative ownership |
| **Just Transition** | Fossil fuel infrastructure is decommissioned equitably — workers retrained, communities repaired, legacy harms addressed |
| **Climate Criticality Accelerator** | Climate-essential infrastructure (renewable grids, flood-resistant water systems, resilient networks) is fast-tracked through priority funding and streamlined approval |

### What This Framework Creates

A world where:
- Every community has reliable access to clean energy, secure data, and clean water
- Infrastructure is governed by those who depend on it, not distant corporations
- Fossil fuel systems are decommissioned justly — no worker abandoned, no community left polluted
- Grids are resilient enough to survive climate shocks, cyber-attacks, and geopolitical disruption
- Infrastructure enhances ecosystems rather than degrading them

### The Relationship with Existing Systems

This framework does not replace existing utilities, grid operators, or national regulators. It creates standards that participating BAZs adopt, and BAZs use their existing authority (land use, procurement, permitting) to require compliance from operators serving their territories. Existing utilities can transition into Bioregional Grid Authorities or operate within their standards. The goal is transformation, not demolition.

## 2. Core Principles

| Principle | Meaning |

## 3. Governance Architecture

### 3.1 Global Infrastructure Council (GIC)

**Role:** Sets global standards for grid interoperability, resilience, and security. Coordinates transnational infrastructure.

**Key Functions:**
- Maintain and update the Open Grid Protocol (open-source standards for energy, data, water infrastructure)
- Coordinate with Crisis Infrastructure Command during emergencies
- Oversee conflict-of-interest disclosures for all members (enforceable via Digital Justice Tribunal)
- Publish Annual State of the Grid Report

**Accountability:** Sub-council of the MGCC. Annual public audit. All members submit financial disclosures.

### 3.2 Bioregional Grid Authorities (BGAs)

**Role:** BAZ-level bodies managing local infrastructure — energy, data, water — with community co-governance.

**Composition:** Minimum 40% community representatives, plus engineers, ecological stewards, and Indigenous representatives where applicable.

**Powers:**
- Implement GIC standards locally
- License and regulate operators within their territory
- Allocate 10% of funding to community-led innovation
- Host annual binding community feedback forums
- Enforce procurement standards: operators must comply with Open Grid Protocol to access BAZ territory

**Community Ownership Pathway:**
- Year 1-10: Communities gain 25% ownership stake in new infrastructure through cooperatives
- Year 10-25: Pathway to 51% ownership, contingent on stewardship capacity (training funded by Global Commons Fund)
- Communities may opt out of ownership and designate a trusted BGA or cooperative to manage their stake

**Accountability:** All financial decisions publicly logged. Persistent underperformance (>50% metric failure for 2 consecutive years) triggers temporary stewardship by the Office of Commons Transition with mandatory community-led restructuring within 12 months.

### 3.3 What About Existing Utilities?

| Existing Entity | Relationship to the Conduit Protocol |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------|
| **National utilities** | May continue operating within BGA territories if they comply with Open Grid Protocol standards. Non-compliant utilities lose access to BAZ procurement and permitting. |
| **Private grid operators** | May transition into BGAs, become cooperative partners, or operate as licensed providers under BGA oversight. |
| **National regulators** | Retain safety and licensing authority. GIC standards apply for access to BAZ markets (same enforcement mechanism as other GGF frameworks). |
| **International bodies (IEA, IRENA)** | GIC coordinates with existing bodies; does not replace them. Technical expertise is welcomed and integrated. |

## 4. Four Pillars

### 4.1 Transnational Grid Standards: The Open Grid Protocol

**What It Is:** Open-source technical standards for energy, data, and water infrastructure ensuring interoperability, resilience, and security.

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Open-Source Hardware & Software** | All infrastructure designs published openly. No vendor lock-in. Communities can build, modify, and maintain their own systems. |
| **Interoperability** | Standards ensure energy, data, and water systems connect seamlessly across borders and technologies. |
| **Quantum-Resistant Security** | Cybersecurity standards certified by the Quantum Governance Lab, updated every 24 months. |
| **Analog Fallback** | All grids must maintain manual override and mechanical systems capable of 50% service capacity during total digital failure. Priority: hospitals, emergency services, water, food distribution. |
| **Neutrality** | Infrastructure may not be weaponized. Use for military aggression is prohibited. Violations enforced by Digital Justice Tribunal. |
| **Cultural Interoperability** | Interfaces and emergency protocols in the top 3 languages per BAZ. Minority language preservation funded by Global Commons Fund. |

### 4.2 Community Infrastructure Governance

**What It Is:** Making infrastructure accountable to the communities it serves.

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Participatory Planning** | All infrastructure projects require community co-design, Intergenerational Impact Assessments, Justice Impact Assessments, and FPIC 2.0 for Indigenous territories. |
| **Community Feedback** | Annual binding community forums hosted by BGAs. Outcomes influence performance metrics and funding allocations. |
| **Anti-Gentrification** | Justice Impact Assessment models displacement risk. Projects causing significant harm to existing communities can be halted. |
| **Ecological Design** | Minimum 30% of new projects incorporate ecosystem services — wetlands for water management, biomimetic grid design, regenerative redundancy. |
| **Sacred Site Protection** | Sacred sites designated by the Earth Council are inviolable. Infrastructure routes around them, not through them. |

### 4.3 Just Transition & Decommissioning

**What It Is:** Ensuring the shift from fossil fuel infrastructure is equitable — no worker abandoned, no community left polluted.

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Fossil Fuel Decommissioning Trust** | Funded by Reparations Levy on fossil fuel corporations and Global Commons Fund. Decommissions legacy infrastructure and manages reparations for historically harmed communities. |
| **Just Transition Service Corps** | Retrains and employs former fossil fuel workers in decommissioning, grid modernization, and ecosystem restoration. Union-equivalent wages. Work rewarded via AUBI. |
| **Infrastructure Reparations Zones** | Designated areas where historical harm (pollution, displacement, extraction) is addressed through targeted investment, land restoration, and community wealth building. |
| **Stranded Asset Compensation** | Fair, time-bound compensation for asset holders transitioning infrastructure into Stewardship Trusts. Valuation based on regenerative potential. |
| **No Transition Without Pathway** | No decommissioning activates without corresponding retraining and employment pathways operational in the affected region. |

### 4.4 Climate Criticality Accelerator

**What It Is:** Fast-tracking climate-essential infrastructure through priority funding and streamlined approval.

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Identification** | Planetary Health Council identifies critical infrastructure needs for climate mitigation and adaptation (renewable energy lines, flood-resistant water systems, resilient networks). |
| **Prioritization** | Matrix: 30% climate vulnerability, 30% Indigenous co-design, 20% adaptation impact, 20% deployment speed. |
| **Funding** | Priority access to Global Commons Fund. Commons Conversion Bonds for large-scale projects. |
| **Streamlined Approval** | Accelerated permitting for high-scoring projects while maintaining FPIC 2.0 and justice protocols. Speed does not override consent. |
| **Review** | Mandatory 5-year performance review. Projects failing regenerative and climate targets lose priority funding. |

## 5. Crisis Response

### Crisis Infrastructure Command

Activated by the Meta-Governance Crisis Command during infrastructure emergencies.

**Activation Levels:**

| Level | Trigger | Response |
|-------|---------|----------|
| **Level 1** | Regional grid disruption affecting >500,000 people | 72-hour rapid response; mutual aid from neighboring BGAs |
| **Level 2** | Multi-bioregional infrastructure failure | Cross-regional coordination; Crisis Resource Pools (15% of grid capacity reserved for emergencies) |
| **Level 3** | Planetary-scale threat (solar storms, coordinated cyber warfare, climate tipping points) | Full activation; all BGAs shift to emergency protocols |

**Safeguards:**
- Emergency powers automatically sunset after 72 hours unless renewed
- All decisions logged publicly within 24 hours
- FPIC 2.0 mandatory for Indigenous territories even under emergency timelines
- Community override: simple majority for non-critical directives, 2/3 for life-support systems
- Post-crisis public hearings within 30 days, with minimum 40% representation from affected communities

**Mutual Aid:** Grid Brotherhood Agreements mandate reciprocal energy sharing up to 15% of BGA capacity during Level 1-2 crises. Pre-negotiated, default terms.

**Analog Resilience:** All critical systems must function during total digital failure. Analog Fallback Protocols maintain 50% service capacity through manual overrides and mechanical systems.

## 6. Implementation Pathway

### Phase 0: Bootstrap (Now)

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Without GIC** | Existing open-source energy communities (Open Source Ecology, Open Energy Monitor) serve as interim standards bodies |
| **Without BGAs** | Municipal utilities, energy cooperatives, and community-owned ISPs serve as proto-BGAs |
| **Without Global Commons Fund** | Philanthropic grants, municipal budgets, existing climate finance (Green Climate Fund, GEF) |
| **Without Treaty Ratification** | BAZs adopt Open Grid Protocol using existing municipal authority over permitting and procurement |
| **Without Full Decommissioning Trust** | Pilot with existing just-transition funds; partner with labor unions for worker retraining |

### Phase 1: Pilots (Years 1-5)

- Release Open Grid Protocol v1.0
- 8 pilot programs across diverse contexts (see Appendix D)
- Establish first Bioregional Grid Authorities with community co-governance
- Launch Just Transition Service Corps in 2 fossil fuel-dependent regions
- Test Crisis Infrastructure Command in disaster-prone and geopolitically complex pilots
- Target: 75% community approval on Infrastructure Legitimacy Index in all pilots

### Phase 2: Regional Integration (Years 6-12)

- 50% of BAZs adopt Open Grid Protocol
- Seamless energy/data sharing across 3+ bioregional boundaries
- 25% global coverage for Universal Access
- 80% of displaced fossil fuel workers retrained and employed
- 25% community ownership of new infrastructure in pilot regions
- Historical Harm Assessments completed in 50% of regions

### Phase 3: Global Adoption (Years 13-25)

- 60% of nations voluntarily adopt Open Grid Protocol
- 75% Universal Access for clean energy, water, and data
- 40% of global infrastructure under community governance
- Full circular economy standards in all projects
- Sovereignty Respect Protocols for non-participating nations to interface with the system

## 7. What Success Looks Like

*Metrics tell us whether we are meeting targets. But success is also felt.*

**A village in the Sahel** has reliable electricity for the first time. The solar microgrid was co-designed with local pastoralist communities. The hardware is open-source — when a panel fails, a local technician repairs it using publicly available blueprints. The community owns the grid. The power is theirs.

**A coal town in Appalachia** is decommissioning its last power plant. The workers are not unemployed — they are employed by the Just Transition Service Corps, building the solar array that replaces the plant. They earn union-equivalent wages. Their pensions are intact. The plant site is being restored to wetland. The community is not a casualty of transition; it is an architect of it.

**A cyclone hits Bangladesh.** The regional grid fails. Within hours, the Crisis Infrastructure Command activates Level 1 response. Neighboring BGAs share 15% of their capacity through pre-negotiated Grid Brotherhood Agreements. Analog fallbacks keep hospitals running. The grid is restored in 72 hours. No one dies because the power went out.

**A Pacific Island nation** runs its entire water system on open-source hardware. When sea levels threaten the desalination plant, the community downloads updated designs from the Open Grid Protocol repository, adapted for their conditions by engineers in another island nation. They rebuild. The knowledge is free. The water flows.

**A city in Europe** converts its privately-owned district heating system to a community cooperative. The Bioregional Grid Authority manages the transition. After 10 years, residents own 25% of the infrastructure. They vote on pricing, maintenance priorities, and expansion plans. The heating works better than before. It also costs less.

**A sacred river in the Andes** is protected. The proposed hydroelectric dam was redesigned to route around the sacred site. The Earth Council exercised its veto. The engineers found an alternative that generates 85% of the original capacity without touching the river. The infrastructure serves the community without violating what the community holds sacred.

**The question is no longer "who owns the grid?"** It is "how do we ensure the grid serves everyone — reliably, equitably, and within planetary boundaries?"

## 8. Integration with Other Frameworks

| Framework | Role |
|-----------|------|
| **Treaty for Our Only Home** | Provides legal authority for transnational infrastructure agreements and dispute resolution |
| **Hearthstone Protocol** | Provides legal tools (Stewardship Trusts) for transitioning private infrastructure to commons ownership |
| **Climate & Energy Framework** | Sets decarbonization targets and renewable energy standards |
| **Mobility Commons** | Coordinates shared rights-of-way and energy needs for transportation |
| **Aurora Accord** | Provides data governance, cybersecurity standards, and transparency dashboards |
| **Indigenous Framework** | Ensures FPIC 2.0 for all infrastructure on Indigenous territories |
| **Phoenix Protocol** | Informs circular economy standards for materials and waste in infrastructure projects |
| **Disaster Risk Reduction** | Co-develops resilience standards and crisis response protocols |
| **Work in Liberation** | Provides framework for Just Transition Service Corps labor standards |
| **Water & Sanitation** | Defines water governance principles that the Conduit Protocol implements physically |

**A Note on Boundaries:** The Conduit Protocol manages the physical systems — grids, pipes, cables, towers. It does not set energy policy (Climate & Energy), data governance (Aurora Accord), or water rights (Water & Sanitation). It builds and maintains the infrastructure those frameworks depend on.

### Appendix B: Financial Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Description | Phase |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **Global Commons Fund** | Primary infrastructure funding | All phases |
| **Reparations Levy** | Levy on fossil fuel corporations funding decommissioning | Phase 1-3 |
| **Extractive Industry Transition Tax** | Funds worker retraining and grid modernization | Phase 1-3 |
| **Infrastructure Carbon Dividend** | Carbon pricing revenue directed to grid decarbonization | Phase 2-3 |
| **Commons Conversion Bonds** | Investment vehicles for large-scale infrastructure transition | Phase 2-3 |
| **Regenerative Infrastructure Credits** | Non-transferable community equity instruments tied to project outcomes | Phase 2-3 |
| **Microgrid Incentives** | Communities developing microgrids earn Leaves via Love Ledger | Phase 1-3 |

**Honest Note on Funding:** The Reparations Levy and Transition Tax assume political power to tax entrenched fossil fuel industries. This power does not currently exist at global scale. The framework therefore sequences funding: Phase 0-1 uses existing climate finance and philanthropic grants; Phase 2 introduces levies in jurisdictions where political conditions allow; Phase 3 scales through demonstrated success and treaty-level authority. If the levies prove politically unavailable, the framework falls back to commons-based funding (Community Conversion Bonds, microgrid incentives, GCF allocations) — slower but not dependent on taxing hostile industries.
