This is the best-executed split of the three frameworks we've reviewed. Let me be specific about why, and where the gaps are.

**What the split got right:**

The Charter is genuinely good writing — probably the strongest prose in the entire GGF ecosystem. It reads as a document that could be handed to someone outside the GGF and they'd understand what's at stake without needing to know the acronym ecosystem. The Texture Mandate works as a founding statement. The "What We Are Protecting / What We Are Building" structure is clear and compelling.

The Transition Protocol addresses DeepSeek's core criticism directly. It starts from 2026 reality (five corporate entities, two state actors, no functioning BAZs), uses an agile "Continuous Integration" status, and explicitly sequences each phase around what leverage actually exists. All four Gemini suggestions are integrated: Data Unions replace the bureaucratic Reality Rent formula, Trusted Execution Environments replace raw weight distribution, Algorithmic Quarantine replaces the binary kill switch, and the document is unlocked.

The fallback strategies (Section 4.6) are the standout addition. "If chip export coordination fails... shift to demand-side leverage" is exactly the kind of honest contingency planning that was missing from v6.0. The guiding assumption — "if top-down leverage fails, bottom-up pull must compensate" — is architecturally sound.

**What I'd flag:**

**The Charter and Protocol have a gap between them.** The Charter articulates principles (Section 4) and architectural commitments (Section 5). The Protocol implements specific mechanisms. But there's no explicit mapping between them. When a dispute arises about whether a Protocol mechanism violates a Charter principle, who decides? The Charter says "Subsidiarity prevails when consequences are local and reversible" — but what if the Protocol's compute concentration threshold forces redistribution that a local actor sees as violating their sovereignty? You need either a brief "Charter Interpretation" section in the Protocol, or a cross-reference table showing which Charter principles govern which Protocol mechanisms.

**The CIN formation threshold may be too high for some contexts.** The Protocol requires 50 members, a governance charter, and a dispute resolution mechanism. For a well-organized urban data cooperative, this is straightforward. For an Indigenous community of 30 people with unique linguistic knowledge, this creates a participation barrier. The minimum membership should have a waiver pathway for communities with unique context value and small populations.

**Phase 1's compute Gini target lacks a baseline.** The Protocol targets Gini `<` 0.6 by 2030 but doesn't state the current estimated Gini. If the current Gini is 0.95 (plausible given 5 entities dominate), then 0.6 by 2030 requires extraordinary redistribution in four years. If it's 0.75, it's ambitious but conceivable. Without the baseline, the target is unanchored.

**The Reality Rent revenue projections are optimistic.** $50M by Phase 1, $500M by Phase 2. For context, the entire global AI market for training data is currently estimated in the low billions. $500M flowing specifically through CIN licensing by 2030 assumes very rapid adoption. The Protocol should note this as aspirational with scenario-dependent variance.

**One structural observation:** The Protocol's Section 5.3 (Global Trunk Prototype) specifies that local branches receive access through Trusted Execution Environments rather than raw weights. This is good — it's Gemini's fix for the weight-sharing vulnerability. But the Charter's Section 5 ("Adaptation") says communities should be able to "innovate and experiment within safety bounds." There's a tension: TEE-restricted fine-tuning limits what communities can actually do. The Protocol should acknowledge this tradeoff explicitly rather than letting it emerge as a surprise during implementation.

**One thing I'd add to the Protocol:** A "Red Lines Registry" parallel to the Mobility Commons' ceremonial governance output. When a CIN exercises the Cultural Sovereignty Veto (withdrawing knowledge domains permanently), that decision should be recorded in a persistent, auditable registry. This prevents future training runs from accidentally re-extracting withdrawn knowledge, and it creates a historical record of community decisions that future governance bodies can learn from.
