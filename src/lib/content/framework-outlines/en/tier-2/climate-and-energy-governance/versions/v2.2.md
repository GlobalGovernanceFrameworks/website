# Climate & Energy Governance Framework v2.2

## A Charter for Planetary Stewardship

We, the stewards of Earth, commit to a sacred covenant: to restore and sustain planetary stability. Guided by justice, equity, and the wisdom of Indigenous and traditional knowledge, we pledge to recalibrate humanity's relationship with energy — from extraction to regeneration — while making sure that no household is left cold, in the dark, or unable to cook because the transition, a market shock, or an institutional gap put essential energy out of reach. This framework is our collective blueprint to secure a thriving, resilient, and equitable future for all life on our only home.

## Plain Language Summary

*This framework uses technical terms because precision matters. But we also want it to be readable. Here is what the key terms mean:*

- **Regenerative Trade Zone (RTZ):** A coalition of countries, regions, and communities that agree to trade on terms that reward clean energy and ecological restoration. Being in the zone creates economic advantages that draw others in — not through enforcement, but through attraction. RTZs are governed by the Gaian Trade Framework.

- **Bioregional Autonomous Zone (BAZ):** A self-governing region defined by ecological boundaries rather than political borders. BAZs are the building blocks of the regenerative economy.

- **Hearts and Leaves:** Currencies that reward care work (Hearts) and ecological restoration (Leaves). They circulate alongside money; they do not replace it. Essential energy bills are usually paid in ordinary money, which matters for household protection (§6).

- **Sundown Protocol:** The framework for phasing out fossil fuels through a managed transition that protects workers and communities.

- **Community energy sovereignty:** Community ownership and governance of renewable generation and local grids, so that the benefits of energy stay with the people who use it.

- **Essential energy minimum:** The amount and quality of energy a household needs for health and dignity — warmth or cooling, cooking, light, refrigeration, power for medical and assistive devices, and communication (§6.1).

- **Price shock versus physical shortage:** In a price shock, energy is still available but suddenly costs much more. In a physical shortage, there is not enough energy to deliver. They call for different responses (§7).

For a full glossary, see Appendix G.

## 1. What This Framework Creates

Imagine a community that generates its own clean power — owned not by a distant utility, but by the people who live there. Imagine energy bills replaced by energy dividends. Imagine fossil fuel infrastructure not as a battleground of protests and arrests, but as decommissioned sites being restored to marshland, forest, or community garden — the workers who once extracted carbon now restoring ecosystems, their livelihoods secured through a just transition.

Imagine, too, that when a gas price triples in a cold winter, no household has to choose between heating and eating, nobody is disconnected for being unable to pay, and the people who decide on support already have the authority and the money to act within days rather than months.

This is not a dream. Pieces of it already exist in communities from Germany to Costa Rica, from Indigenous-led territories to progressive cities. What is missing is not the possibility, but the architecture to scale it — to connect these islands of regeneration into a self-reinforcing system that outcompetes the extractive economy, and to protect households while it does so.

**This framework is that architecture.**

It is built around two strategic insights:

1. **The transition will not be enforced from above; it will be pulled from the side.** The Regenerative Trade Zone is the engine of that pull.
2. **A transition that leaves people cold will not survive politically.** Energy security for households is a condition of the transition, not a cost to be absorbed later.

## 2. Constitutional Position and Non-Authority

### 2.1 Status

This framework is a governance architecture and interface. It may be adopted through domestic law, Indigenous law, municipal or regional charter, Treaty protocol, compact, contract, regulatory instrument, licence condition, or another valid source. Its templates and targets do not become binding merely because a GGF institution publishes or endorses them.

### 2.2 Non-authority clause

This framework does not itself:

- create energy-regulatory, price-setting, licensing, or tariff-setting jurisdiction;
- impose a tax, levy, tariff, sanction, fine, or contribution;
- declare an emergency, activate emergency powers, or ration energy;
- order disconnection, reconnection, curtailment, or allocation;
- create a global entitlement to a specified quantity of energy;
- create a court, prosecute, or adjudicate liability;
- transfer authority from a state, municipality, Indigenous nation, regulator, utility, court, or community;
- allocate strategic reserves, Hearts, Leaves, or public funds.

> **No valid authority record, no consequential energy action.**

Consequential actions require an independently valid source of authority and the records required by GMEAIA and, for emergencies, the Emergency Interface and `ECRC/0.1`.

### 2.3 What the framework does do

It names the functions a just energy transition needs; supplies model standards, options, and record templates; identifies the competent-authority pathway for each function; and requires that unaccepted or unanswered duties stay visible under GMEAIA rather than disappearing.

### 2.4 No performance-to-jurisdiction conversion

A body does not acquire authority because it forecasts accurately, funds useful projects, coordinates many actors, or earns public trust. Demonstrated competence may justify voluntary replication, procurement, delegation, or proposed legislation. It does not itself transfer jurisdiction.

## 4. Governance Structure

### 4.1 Global Oversight Body

**Role:** Coordinate climate and energy policy among participating authorities, maintain shared standards and reporting methods, monitor progress, and integrate with UNFCCC processes.

**Constituting source:** The body exists only where validly constituted by the Treaty, a compact, or participating authorities. Its charter states its powers, prohibited powers, membership and selection, decision rules, funding, challenge, review, and dissolution.

**Instruments requiring separate authority:** Trade penalties, climate tariffs, restricted access to green technology funds, and other consequential measures may be used only where a competent trade, fiscal, funding, or regulatory authority has enacted them under its own law, with notice, reasons, proportionality, review, and appeal. Trade measures route through Gaian Trade and the competent trade authorities; fiscal measures through Adaptive Tax. Alignment with WTO exemptions (GATT Article XX) is a design consideration for those authorities, not a power of this body.

**Dispute resolution:** Disputes go to the competent court or tribunal under Justice Systems v1.4.1 and the applicable legal order. An International Climate Chamber may be established where a valid instrument constitutes it; until then any climate panel is advisory.

**Biosphere Health Index thresholds:** The Planetary Health Council or another competent observer may report Biosphere Health Index (BHI) observations, including:

- global average temperature above 1.5°C for 12 consecutive months;
- methane release exceeding 50 gigatons CO2 equivalent annually;
- critical ecological tipping points reached (e.g., Amazon rainforest collapse).

A threshold crossing triggers a published state estimate, review, and escalation to the competent authorities. It does not activate emergency powers. A persistent condition is not an emergency under CIS §13.1; it goes to ordinary law and treaty processes, and, where the criteria are met, it is recorded as a CIS §7.7 standing preventable-harm condition, whose record cannot go dormant while the condition persists. A time-bounded emergency that independently meets CIS §13.1 follows CIS §13 and the Emergency Interface alone.

**Safeguards:** Independent audits, conflict-of-interest rules, rotation where the charter adopts it, and published reasons prevent capture by powerful interests.

### 4.2 Regional Hubs

**Role:** Adapt shared goals to regional contexts, support subnational actors — cities, regions, Indigenous territories — and build local capacity.

**Focus:** Capacity building, resource coordination, and resilience to political backsliding through multi-stakeholder coalitions. Regional hubs hold only the powers their constituting instruments grant. Political change in a member state does not transfer that state's authority to a hub or any other body.

### 4.3 National Implementation Units

**Role:** Execute policies and report progress, aligned with Paris Agreement Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). Sovereignty is respected.

**Continuity during political shifts:** When a national government withdraws or backslides, cities, regions, Indigenous nations, utilities, cooperatives, and other actors may continue within their own lawful competence. Their action does not substitute for national authority, and no regional or GGF body inherits national powers.

### 4.4 Competent energy authority (household continuity)

Each adopting jurisdiction designates the authority or authorities responsible for the household continuity functions in §6 — typically the energy regulator, the energy ministry, social-protection authorities, or municipal utilities acting under their own law. The designation is recorded in the Household Energy Continuity Record (Appendix E). Where no authority accepts the function, the matter is recorded under GMEAIA (§6.6).

### 4.5 Governance of Controversial Technologies

The framework recognizes that certain technologies — nuclear energy, carbon capture and storage, and solar radiation management (geoengineering) — are deeply contested. It does not resolve these debates but sets out conditions that competent authorities should require before any deployment.

**Nuclear Energy:** Case-by-case consideration, with:
- consent from affected communities, and consent or refusal by affected Indigenous nations under their own law;
- demonstrated long-term waste solutions with intergenerational accountability;
- safety standards and independent oversight under Technology Governance v3.7 and competent nuclear regulators;
- a preference for small modular reactors over large centralized facilities where they meet safety standards.

**Carbon Capture and Storage:** Only as a complement to, not a replacement for, emissions reduction. Criteria include:
- net-negative outcomes verified by independent monitoring;
- no use for enhanced oil recovery;
- community consent for storage sites;
- funding from the fossil fuel industry, not diverted from renewable investment.

**Solar Radiation Management (Geoengineering):** Subject to the highest scrutiny:
- moratorium on deployment without global consensus;
- research only under a Seven-Generation Impact Assessment;
- binding prohibition on unilateral deployment by any nation or corporation, to be enacted through valid treaty or domestic law;
- youth and future-generation review, and Indigenous review, carried by whatever binding effect the competent constituting instrument grants. A Global Youth Assembly or Indigenous body holds a veto only where such an instrument grants it; affected Indigenous peoples' consent and refusal rights arise from their own law and the competent Indigenous framework.

For all controversial technologies, the principle is the same: **when the risks are intergenerational, the consent must be intergenerational** — and the authority to give or withhold it must be real, not asserted.

## 5. Core Pillars of Transformation

### Pillar I: Radical Mitigation & Ecosystem Restoration

**Targets:** Net-zero emissions by 2050, with interim milestones (55% reduction by 2035). These are benchmarks for adopting authorities, aligned with IPCC pathways.

**Strategies:**
- Carbon pricing that makes fossil fuels more expensive than renewables. Instrument design, rates, incidence, and revenue use follow Adaptive Tax v0.1.5. Every carbon-pricing instrument publishes a household-incidence assessment (Adaptive Tax §13) and the compensating measures that protect the essential energy minimum (§6.4).
- Nature-based solutions — restoring forests, wetlands, and mangroves that sequester carbon.
- Industrial decarbonization through circular economy practices (closed-loop manufacturing, waste reduction).

Funding and tracking for nature-based solutions may use the AUBI Framework's Leaves currency under Financial Systems.

### Pillar II: The Just & Sovereign Energy Transition

**Goal:** Shift to 90–100% clean energy globally by 2050 (aligned with IPCC pathways), with 80% community-owned renewable energy systems.

**How It Happens:**
- Phase out fossil fuel subsidies and redirect funds to renewable energy. Subsidies that keep essential energy affordable for low-income households are replaced by targeted protection (§6.4) before they are withdrawn, not after.
- Modernize grids for decentralized, resilient energy systems (physical operation under Conduit Protocol v1.0).
- Scale Community Energy Cooperatives (§5.2b).
- Ensure a just transition for fossil fuel workers (Sundown Protocol v2.2, §5.2a).
- Build sustainable supply chains for renewable technologies (ethical sourcing of critical minerals).
- Create circular energy systems (battery recycling, modular solar panel designs).

#### 5.2a The Sundown Protocol: Managing the Phase-Out

Sundown Protocol v2.2 governs the managed phase-out of fossil fuel infrastructure: voluntary commitments through the Pathfinder Compact, transition standards, worker and community protection, ecological remediation, and ceremonial and cultural closure. This framework does not restate Sundown's instruments. Any levy, bond, or contribution that finances the transition requires a valid fiscal or legal instrument under Adaptive Tax or the competent authority.

Sundown's sector guidance requires that replacement energy be operational before decommissioning completes. This framework adds the household test: decommissioning schedules state their effect on household energy prices and supply in the affected region, and the §6 protections that apply.

The Sundown Protocol is not punitive. Its goal is that those who powered the extractive era are among the first to benefit from the regenerative one.

#### 5.2b Community Energy Sovereignty

Community energy sovereignty is built through three linked functions, each with its own home:

- **Cooperative development:** technical assistance, financing models (seed capital from the Global Commons Fund where authorized, matched by Hearts contributions under Financial Systems), and governance templates for cooperative formation. Held in this framework; see Appendix B.
- **Ownership transitions:** transferring existing energy infrastructure to community ownership — through voluntary sale, public-interest purchase, worker or multi-stakeholder cooperatives, municipalization, or other lawful routes. Governed by Hearthstone Protocol v2.0.3, including its claims mapping, essential-service continuity, worker transition, and stewardship-charter requirements.
- **Grid interoperability:** technical protocols that let community grids connect, share power, and trade energy without surrendering local control. Physical systems under Conduit Protocol v1.0; data and AI standards under Technology Governance v3.7 and the Aurora Accord.

Community energy is the hearth — the ancient centre of home, warmth, and gathering. Its purpose is to return energy to what it has always been: the capacity to keep each other warm, to power our lives, to build together. A community energy cooperative is also a natural first responder in a price shock: it can protect members' essential minimum and keep prices tied to local costs rather than volatile fuel markets.

### Pillar III: Traditional Knowledge-Led Adaptation & Resilience

**Focus:** Resilience for vulnerable populations and ecosystems, guided by Indigenous and local knowledge under the authority of the knowledge holders.

**Actions:**
- Infrastructure upgrades that protect communities from climate impacts.
- Disaster preparedness through The Living Shield v4.4.2.
- Nature-based solutions (mangrove restoration, watershed protection) aligned with biodiversity targets.
- Climate finance for developing nations, with funds reaching communities directly.
- **Heat and cold resilience:** cooling and heating access is part of adaptation. Extreme-heat and extreme-cold plans protect the essential energy minimum (§6.1) and identify households dependent on powered medical or assistive devices.

**Health-Resilient Adaptation:** Major energy and adaptation projects include Health Co-Benefits Assessments. Results — reduced air pollution, improved health outcomes — are reported and may inform a BAZ's Love, Meaning, and Connection Index (LMCI-M) evidence as an independent outcome channel, not as a score that determines rights or funding.

### Pillar IV: Building the Regenerative Energy Commons

**The Narrative:** Promoting the vision of community-owned, resilient, abundant clean energy. Public information follows Synoptic Protocol v4.2: authorities and participants may explain, correct, and disclose uncertainty, but no institution under this framework may covertly segment audiences, suppress lawful criticism, or run political persuasion campaigns.

**The Lived Experience:** Community Energy Cooperatives provide clean power, local wealth, stronger community bonds, and better quality of life. People experience the transition as liberation, not sacrifice — which requires that their energy bills, warmth, and security improve, not only their emissions.

**The Economic Advantage:** The Regenerative Trade Zone creates incentives for nations to join. As the zone grows, being outside it becomes costlier — through lost opportunity, not punishment.

## 6. Household Energy Continuity and Affordability

Energy is the only essential service in the GGF corpus that, before this revision, had no household continuity or affordability provisions. This section names the function, sets model standards, and identifies the pathway. Each adopting jurisdiction decides which competent authority exercises each function (§4.4). Nothing in this section creates a global entitlement, a GGF energy regulator, or a price-setting power.

### 6.1 Essential energy minimum

Each adopting jurisdiction should publish an essential-energy minimum schedule. It should cover:

- space heating to a health-protective indoor temperature in cold periods, and cooling or heat relief in hot periods;
- cooking and safe food storage;
- lighting;
- refrigeration of medicines;
- power for medical, assistive, and life-sustaining devices;
- hot water for hygiene;
- communication and access to warnings;
- accessibility needs, disability-related energy costs, and household composition.

The schedule distinguishes:

- the **protected minimum**, which may not be cut for inability to pay;
- ordinary household service above the minimum;
- essential institutional service (healthcare, shelters, care homes, water and sanitation pumping, schools used as refuges);
- livelihood and food-system uses;
- discretionary and commercial use.

The schedule states its basis — climate, housing stock, local fuel types — and is reviewed at least every three years and after any major shock.

### 6.2 Affordability options

A jurisdiction may use any combination of:

- lifeline or social tariffs for the protected minimum;
- income-based energy support;
- an energy component within social protection, including AUBI Layer 3 where adopted;
- targeted efficiency retrofits and heating-system replacement for low-income and poorly insulated homes;
- arrears relief, debt forgiveness, and payment plans;
- cross-subsidy within a tariff structure;
- community energy cooperative pricing that passes through local generation costs;
- public finance.

A model energy-burden threshold may inform policy but must be tested against actual housing, disability, family-size, climate, and local cost conditions. An affordability measure that is mainly paid in Hearts is inadmissible where essential energy is payable only in fiat (AUBI §5.1.2).

### 6.3 Protection against disconnection

The protected minimum shall not be disconnected for inability to pay. Specifically:

- no disconnection of households with members dependent on powered medical or life-sustaining devices, or during declared cold or heat periods set by the competent authority;
- prepayment arrangements that cut supply automatically when credit runs out are treated as disconnection; the competent authority provides emergency credit, friendly-hours rules, or an equivalent safeguard;
- any lawful restriction for non-payment above the minimum requires notice, an accessible challenge route, a payment-plan offer, and an assessment of vulnerability;
- tenants are not disconnected because a landlord fails to pay a supplier;
- lack of a formal tenancy, address, identity document, or migration status does not bar access to the protected minimum.

Disconnection is never used as debt-collection leverage against the protected minimum or as a compliance tool for any other GGF process.

### 6.4 Transition incidence and protection before price rises

Carbon pricing, fossil-subsidy removal, network-cost reallocation, and Sundown decommissioning can raise household energy costs. Before such a measure takes effect, the adopting authority publishes:

- the expected change in household energy costs by income group, region, housing type, and heating fuel;
- the households that cannot reduce consumption quickly (renters, people in poorly insulated homes, people dependent on a single fuel);
- the compensating measures from §6.2 and when they start;
- the independent outcome measures that will show whether the protected minimum held.

Fiscal design and revenue recycling follow Adaptive Tax. Income measures follow AUBI and social-protection law. The rule is sequence: protection is in place before the price rises, not promised afterwards.

### 6.6 Unaccepted duties

Where the household-continuity function has no accepting authority, or the accepting authority records a capacity limitation, the referring body records the state under GMEAIA-08 (`capacity_limited`, `refused`, `deferred`, `timed_out`, `misrouted`, or `constitutional_gap`), with expected harm during delay and named custody of the unresolved duty.

Where cold- or heat-related deaths or serious irreversible harm are ongoing or recurring, a known intervention exists, its cost is small relative to the harm, and no authority has accepted the duty, the condition is recorded as a CIS §7.7 standing preventable-harm condition. Its record may not close or go dormant while the condition persists; it carries a published cost-of-delay estimate and re-escalates each review cycle. The record creates no authority, levy, or individual duty.

## 7. Energy Shocks: Price Shocks and Physical Shortages

### 7.1 Two different conditions

A sudden rise in energy costs can come from two different conditions, which call for different responses:

| | Price shock with adequate supply | Physical shortage |
|---|---|---|
| **What happens** | Energy remains deliverable but costs much more | Not enough energy can be delivered to meet demand |
| **Main harm** | Households and essential services cannot afford the protected minimum; windfall profits accrue upstream | Some users cannot be supplied at any price; essential services and production are at risk |
| **Primary response** | Affordability protection (§6.2–6.3), income support, incidence correction, market-conduct review | Lawful allocation that protects households and essential services, demand reduction, supply restoration |
| **Usual pathway** | Ordinary law and ordinary coordination (Meta-Governance §5) | Emergency-supply law where it exists; Emergency Interface and CIS §13 when its criteria are met |
| **What it is not** | Not an emergency merely because prices rose | Not a reason to suspend rights beyond what the emergency authorization specifies |

A threefold price rise is not by itself evidence of unlawful market conduct. A competition referral asks the competent authority to examine conduct; that authority may legitimately find none.

### 7.2 Pre-authorized protective instruments

Legitimate protection must be fast, and fast protection must be lawful before the shock arrives. Adopting jurisdictions should establish, in ordinary times:

- a **disconnection-moratorium trigger**: the indicator, the authority that activates it, its scope, and its maximum duration;
- a **temporary household energy support** instrument through social protection, AUBI, or a forecast-finance instrument under The Living Shield §10 where the shock is tied to a supply disruption within its hazard scope, with defined funding source, eligibility, payment channel, non-digital access, expiry, and review;
- a **market-monitoring referral route** to the competent competition or energy-market authority, with a response clock;
- a **shortage-allocation plan** under the competent authority, naming protected customer categories and essential services, consistent with the Emergency Interface and `ECRC/0.1`.

Each instrument states its trigger, the authority that activates it, what it may and may not do, its duration, review, expiry, independent outcome measures, and challenge route. An indicator may activate an already authorized, reversible protective measure; it may not create new authority (GMEAIA §3.8).

### 7.3 Coordination during a shock

Any participating authority, affected community, worker institution, authorized sentinel, or valid petition may open ordinary coordination under Meta-Governance §5.1 without an emergency declaration. Coordination documents a shared problem formulation (§5.3), sets response clocks and handoffs (§5.4), and leaves authorization with each competent institution (§5.6). Where CIS §13.1 is independently met, Meta-Governance §13 provides emergency convening; convening is not command.

Coordination is judged by outcomes, not records: whether households kept their protected minimum, how long the first protective action took, and who was missed. Procedural conformance is not material protection.

### 7.4 Strategic reserves

Strategic reserves are released only by the authority that holds them, under its own rules. Where a reserve serves both humanitarian availability and price stabilization, its governing instrument states which objective takes priority and how the conflict is resolved. This framework allocates no reserves.

### 7.5 After the shock

Temporary measures expire as authorized. An after-action review reports the first protective action and its delay, households that lost supply, subgroup effects, windfall profits and their treatment, what pre-authorized instruments were missing, and whether ordinary capacity should be strengthened. Lessons go to the competent authorities as recommendations, not as new powers.

## 8. Policy Mechanisms

Each mechanism requires a competent authority acting under its own law. This framework supplies design standards.

| Mechanism | Description | Competent pathway |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Treaties** | Strengthen Paris Agreement commitments with clear implementation pathways | Parties under the Treaty for Our Only Home and UNFCCC |
| **Carbon Markets** | Interoperable, high-integrity markets including biodiversity and community co-benefits, aligned with Article 6 of the Paris Agreement | Gaian Trade and competent market regulators |
| **Carbon pricing** | Taxes or charges on emissions, with published incidence and household protection | Adaptive Tax; §6.4 |
| **Green Bonds & Subsidies** | Public and private finance for clean technology and circular systems | Competent fiscal and financial authorities |
| **Mandatory Corporate Disclosure** | Climate reporting per ISSB requirements | Competent securities and reporting regulators |

## 9. Financing the Transition

### Funding sources (aspirational benchmarks)

| Source | Target | Mechanism |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| **Multilateral Funds** | $500B annually by 2030 | Green Climate Fund and equivalent institutions |
| **Private Capital** | $1T annually by 2040 | Blended finance, green bonds, impact investment |
| **Reparative Contributions** | 0.5–1% of GDP from high-emission nations | Through the Treaty's contribution provisions and Parties' own fiscal law |
| **Loss & Damage Fund** | $100B annually by 2030 | Separate from adaptation funding, addressing irreversible climate impacts |

**Total target:** $500 billion annually by 2030 in public finance, scaling to $1 trillion by 2040 through blended instruments, with priority for adaptation and loss and damage in the Global South.

**Status:** These figures are aspirational benchmarks, not forecasts, appropriations, or assessed contributions. Current multilateral climate funds have mobilized a fraction of these levels. Contributions arise only through valid treaty, fiscal, or voluntary instruments.

**Household protection finance:** Adopting jurisdictions should identify, before carbon pricing or subsidy removal takes effect, the funding for §6.2 measures — for example a share of carbon-pricing revenue, set through Adaptive Tax. Windfall profits from energy price shocks may be addressed through Adaptive Tax's excess-returns and sector-specific instrument families where a competent jurisdiction enacts them.

## 10. Implementation Roadmap

### Milestone 1: Governance Activation (target: 18 months)

**Trigger:** Valid constitution of the Global Oversight Body and adoption of the framework by an initial RTZ coalition (15+ nations and BAZs).

**Activities:**
- Recruit founding RTZ members (Appendix C).
- Each founding member designates its household-continuity authority and publishes its essential-energy minimum schedule (§6.1).

**Trigger:** Reparative contributions flowing through valid instruments; first Community Energy Cooperatives operational in pilot BAZs.

**Activities:**
- First nature-based funding rounds via Leaves under Financial Systems.

### Milestone 3: Systemic Shift (target: Year 8)

**Trigger:** BHI reported alongside GDP in public reporting within RTZ member states; 50% of RTZ energy community-owned.

**Activities:**
- Hearts and Leaves integrated into RTZ economies under Financial Systems.
- Majority of fossil fuel infrastructure in RTZ members under active decommissioning, with household price effects assessed (§5.2a).
- Worker transition programmes show measurable success.

### Milestone 4: Global Tipping Point (target: Year 15)

**Trigger:** RTZ covers over 60% of the global economy; 90–100% clean energy achieved internally.

**Activities:**
- Non-RTZ nations face the opportunity costs of non-membership.
- Global emissions on a downward trajectory aligned with 1.5°C pathways.
- Framework becomes a foundation for post-2030 climate architecture.

## 11. How the Pull Effect Works: A Concrete Example

Consider a mid-sized industrial nation hesitant to join the RTZ. It is not ideologically opposed to climate action, but it fears economic disadvantage — and it remembers the political backlash when a fuel-tax rise hit rural households without compensation.

**Year 1:** Costa Rica — an RTZ member — exports renewable energy components to other RTZ members under preferential terms. Its energy costs drop. Its manufacturers gain price advantages in RTZ markets.

**Year 2:** The hesitant nation's exporters lose market share in RTZ countries. Their goods face Gaian Trade tariffs that RTZ members' goods do not. Their supply chains lack Digital Product Passports.

**Year 3:** Business leaders and union representatives visit a former coal region in Germany that is now an RTZ member. They see reclaimed land, thriving cooperatives, retrained workers — and a lifeline tariff and retrofit programme that cut energy poverty while carbon prices rose. They ask: why not us?

**Year 4:** The nation applies for RTZ candidacy and enters the Trade Transition Tiering process (Appendix C). Its first commitment is the household-continuity package — because that is what makes the rest politically possible.

**The pull effect does not require enforcement.** It requires that the alternative become so obviously superior — for exporters and for households — that staying out becomes the harder choice.

## 12. Metrics for Success

- Limit warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels
- Net-zero emissions by 2050

### Energy
- 90–100% clean energy by 2050
- 80% community-owned renewable energy systems

### Household energy security
- Share of households unable to keep their home adequately warm or cool, by income, region, tenure, and disability

- 90% of nations meet development and resilience benchmarks

- 75% of vulnerable communities (low-lying coastal zones, arid regions, small island states) climate-resilient by 2035; 100% by 2040

- 30x30 targets achieved (30% of land and sea protected)

### Well-Being
- Love, Meaning, and Connection Index (LMCI-M) shows measurable improvement in RTZ communities
- Health Co-Benefits Assessments demonstrate improved public health outcomes

## 14. Integration with the Global Governance Framework Ecosystem

### 14.1 Implementation sequencing

Initial adoption is driven by a voluntary coalition of nations and BAZs forming a Regenerative Trade Zone under Gaian Trade. Binding obligations arise as the Treaty for Our Only Home is ratified and as Parties and competent authorities enact them. Consequences escalate from incentives to sanctions only through valid instruments with adjudication under Justice Systems.

### 14.2 Technology and data governance

Technology deployed under this framework (smart grids, energy trading platforms, smart meters) adheres to Technology Governance v3.7. Energy data follows the Aurora Accord, including community data sovereignty and Indigenous data protocols. Household energy-consumption and payment data are purpose-limited: they may support protection (§6) but may not be used for unrelated enforcement, credit scoring, or profiling.

**Net-Zero AI Standards:** Competent technology authorities should require that large-scale AI operations (data centres, model training) be powered by verifiable, additional renewable energy and undergo efficiency audits. Their demand on grids is assessed for effects on household prices and supply. AI used for grid management undergoes bias audits and follows open-source standards where feasible.

### 14.3 Crisis coordination

- **Price shocks and cross-sector stress:** ordinary coordination under Meta-Governance §5 (§7.3).
- **Time-bounded emergencies:** CIS §13, the Emergency Interface, `ECRC/0.1`, and The Living Shield v4.4.2. Meta-Governance §13 provides convening, not command.
- **Catastrophic-risk foresight:** Planetary Immune System v2.2.2 may prepare state estimates and dependency maps. It does not take over energy governance.
- **Strategic reserves and emergency finance:** released only by the reserve's own authority (Gaian Trade's Global Strategic Resource Reserve, national reserves) and, for Hearts and Leaves, under Financial Systems v3.5 §10.
- **Cascading failures:** dependency mapping under The Living Shield Appendix G (System Dependency and Cascade Map), including energy-to-food and energy-to-water links.

### 14.4 Local-to-global coordination

Local energy cooperatives may form Community Energy Federations. Where a federation adopts Work in Liberation arrangements, it may operate as a Community Work Team under that framework. A federation may send delegates to the Fractal Labor Parliament or the Global Oversight Body only where those bodies' constituting instruments provide for such representation.

Indigenous knowledge integration follows the competent Indigenous framework (Indigenous Sovereignty Pathways v1.1) and the protocols of the affected peoples themselves.

### 14.5 Just transition via the Sundown Protocol

This framework sets targets and household protections. Sundown Protocol v2.2 provides the phase-out pathway. Hearthstone v2.0.3 governs ownership transitions of energy assets. Conduit v1.0 operates physical infrastructure.

## 16. Framework Falsification and Narrowing Conditions

Under GMEAIA G20, this framework states what would show it should narrow, change, or stop.

**Theory of change:** A trade-based pull effect plus community energy ownership and protected household energy security can deliver the transition faster and more durably than enforcement-led approaches, while preventing the political backlash that higher fossil prices otherwise cause.

**Disconfirming evidence:**
- RTZ membership does not produce measurable trade or investment advantages after the Milestone 2 period.
- Community-owned energy shares stall or reverse in pilot BAZs despite available finance.
- Energy poverty, disconnections, or cold- and heat-related deaths rise in adopting jurisdictions after transition measures, relative to comparable non-adopting jurisdictions.
- Pre-authorized protective instruments are never activated in real shocks, or activate too slowly to reach households before harm occurs.
- Coordination adds delay without improving household outcomes.

**Narrowing condition:** If the pull effect fails but household protections work, the framework narrows to its household-continuity and community-energy functions.

**Suspension condition:** If adoption measurably worsens household energy security without compensating benefits, new adoption pauses pending independent review.

**Replacement condition:** If competent national and regional authorities deliver equivalent outcomes without this framework, it may be retired in favour of those arrangements.

**Review:** Every three years, and after any major energy price shock in an adopting jurisdiction.

## 17. Conclusion

Together, we can realize a coordinated, equitable, and regenerative transformation of our global climate and energy systems — one rooted in justice, powered by cooperation, and inspired by possibility.

A transition that makes energy cleaner but leaves people cold will not hold. This framework therefore asks two things of every participant: build the regenerative energy commons, and make sure that while it is built, every household keeps its warmth, its light, and its power — through protections that are lawful, funded, and ready before they are needed.
