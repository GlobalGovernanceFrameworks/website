## Executive constitutional summary

The Aethelred Accord supports the responsible development, containment, assessment, use, transfer, release, monitoring, recall, and retirement of advanced biotechnology. It protects biological liberty, genetic privacy, Indigenous and collective authority, worker safety, ecological integrity, equitable access to essential biotechnologies, and the freedom to refuse participation in biological intervention.

The Accord does **not** create a global biotechnology sovereign. It does not itself enact criminal offences, issue licences, approve field releases, impose compulsory treatment, declare emergencies, override Indigenous law, seize biological materials, revoke patents, compel licensing, impose taxes, or shut facilities. Those actions require an independently valid legal instrument and a competent authority operating within its jurisdiction.

The Accord replaces the former model of one Global Biosafety Council, one public genetic registry, universal biosafety levels, automatic metric-triggered pauses, and a tribunal override of Indigenous refusal. In their place it establishes:

1. a multidimensional **Biotechnology Activity, Dependency, and Impact Profile**;
2. separated standards, assessment, accreditation, regulation, incident coordination, access, justice, and enforcement functions;
3. federated provenance and authority records compatible with protected non-transfer;
4. lawful, activity-specific licensing and field-release decisions;
5. a shared biological-risk incident interface with Global Health v2.2.1;
6. the 72-hour, 30-day, and 90-day emergency review architecture;
7. independent evidence, appeal, remedy, and wrongful-action compensation;
8. public-interest access pathways grounded in valid health, patent, procurement, trade, or emergency law.

Three rules govern the whole Accord:

> **Scientific severity does not create legal authority.**

> **Classification identifies risks and claims; it does not itself authorize access, release, prohibition, seizure, or intervention.**

> **No valid authority record, no consequential biotechnology action.**

# 1. Purpose, scope, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

The Accord provides a common governance grammar for biotechnology activities whose effects may cross bodies, generations, species, ecosystems, territories, supply chains, or jurisdictions. It is designed to make risk visible without turning risk assessment into command, and to make beneficial biotechnology accessible without treating life, bodies, genetic material, or knowledge as an automatically global commons.

Its purposes are to:

- prevent accidental, negligent, reckless, or deliberate biological harm;
- improve laboratory, manufacturing, clinical, agricultural, ecological, and field-release safety;
- preserve human biological liberty, bodily integrity, reproductive freedom, disability rights, and informed consent;
- recognize Indigenous, community, familial, and relational claims in genetic material and knowledge;
- protect workers, whistleblowers, research participants, patients, communities, and ecosystems;
- support equitable access to safe and effective essential therapies and public-interest biotechnologies;
- preserve scientific inquiry while governing dual-use capability and dangerous disclosure;
- maintain incident readiness, continuity, recall, decommissioning, and restoration capacity;
- provide interfaces among health, environmental, labour, technology, data, Indigenous, justice, and emergency authorities.

## 1.2 Scope

The Accord may be used for governance of:

- synthetic biology;
- gene editing and gene regulation;
- engineered organisms and biological systems;
- gene drives and self-propagating constructs;
- advanced diagnostics, vaccines, therapeutics, and biological manufacturing;
- human cell, tissue, organoid, embryo, germline, reproductive, genomic, and neural-biological interventions;
- agricultural, veterinary, environmental, conservation, and bioremediation biotechnology;
- laboratory research involving pathogens or enhanced biological capability;
- automated biological design, AI-assisted sequence generation, and laboratory automation;
- biological data, samples, provenance, and transfer interfaces;
- field trials, releases, monitoring, recall, remediation, and decommissioning;
- biotechnology incidents with possible public-health, ecological, occupational, or transboundary consequences.

The Accord does not replace clinical medicine, public-health law, environmental permitting, occupational safety, research ethics, patent law, trade law, Indigenous law, criminal law, humanitarian law, or specialized scientific standards. It specifies how these domains should interface when biotechnology activities cross their boundaries.

## 1.3 Non-authority clause

Nothing in this Accord independently authorizes any person or institution to:

- enact law or create an offence;
- establish universal biosafety categories with binding legal effect;
- issue, suspend, or revoke a facility, product, clinical, manufacturing, transport, or release licence;
- compel research participation, treatment, vaccination, genetic testing, sample provision, sterilization, implantation, pregnancy continuation or termination, or germline intervention;
- classify an individual, people, territory, species, ecosystem, or genetic resource as globally owned;
- digitize, publish, transfer, train AI on, commercialize, or retain protected biological material or knowledge;
- impose a field-release moratorium or facility shutdown;
- declare a health, biosafety, ecological, or existential emergency;
- override Indigenous jurisdiction, refusal, non-engagement, or protected non-transfer;
- seize samples, equipment, intellectual property, or facilities;
- compel licensing, revoke patents, impose levies, or allocate public funds;
- direct police, military, intelligence, border, or armed units;
- adjudicate liability, impose sanctions, or execute remedies.

A framework reference is not a source of law. Every consequential action requires an **Authority Provenance Record** identifying the constituting law, treaty provision, contract, Indigenous legal order, professional mandate, court order, emergency instrument, or other valid source.

## 1.4 No residual biotechnology jurisdiction

Where no competent authority has been validly assigned, the power remains unassigned. It does not flow upward to the Planetary Health Council, Meta-Governance, the Planetary Immune System, a scientific panel, a data registry, or an emergency coordination body.

# 2. Governing principles

1. **Biological liberty:** No person may be subjected to biological intervention without a valid legal basis and the applicable consent, rights, and review protections.
2. **Precaution with proportionality:** Uncertainty may justify narrow, reversible protective action where potential harm is grave, but not unbounded prohibition or permanent emergency governance.
3. **Function separation:** Standards, assessment, regulation, emergency coordination, funding, adjudication, and enforcement remain institutionally distinct.
4. **Activity-specific governance:** Biotechnology is governed by its use, operator, location, scale, dependencies, affected parties, and possible effects—not by one global technology tier.
5. **Plural observation:** No single model, laboratory, company, government, community, or knowledge system determines biological risk alone.
6. **Indigenous authority:** Affected Indigenous peoples exercise authority through their own institutions and law; the GGF does not create a universal Indigenous proxy.
7. **Protected non-transfer:** Legitimate stewardship may require non-digitization, local custody, non-publication, non-commercial use, non-training, return, deletion, destruction, or repatriation.
8. **Ecological restraint:** Field release requires attention to persistence, spread, non-target effects, evolutionary response, reversibility, and intergenerational consequences.
9. **Access without enclosure:** Essential biotechnology access should be expanded through lawful public-interest conditions, manufacturing capacity, procurement, licensing, and financing rather than unauthorized global ownership claims.
10. **Worker and participant protection:** Research, laboratory, manufacturing, clinical, field, agricultural, sanitation, logistics, and community workers retain full labour and safety rights.
11. **Disability and reproductive justice:** Governance must not encode a hierarchy of lives, bodies, traits, or ways of being.
12. **Open science with bounded disclosure:** Transparency is the default for safety claims and public funding, but personal, Indigenous-restricted, confidential, security-sensitive, and high-risk dual-use information receives layered protection.
13. **Restoration after harm:** Incident response includes containment, care, ecological restoration, data and sample disposition, compensation, institutional correction, and authority return.
14. **Failure may stop scaling:** A pilot may validly conclude that a technology, registry, standard, institution, or intervention should remain local, be redesigned, or be abandoned.

# 3. Biotechnology Activity, Dependency, and Impact Profile

## 3.1 Purpose

The **Biotechnology Activity, Dependency, and Impact Profile (BADIP)** replaces the previous use of a single BSL 1–5 ladder as a complete governance system. Established laboratory containment categories may remain useful technical standards, but they do not by themselves determine legal jurisdiction, field-release approval, research ethics, product authorization, emergency action, or public access.

## 3.2 Required dimensions

Each profile records, as applicable:

- organism, construct, sequence, cell line, vector, material, or process;
- intended purpose and reasonably foreseeable secondary uses;
- laboratory, clinical, manufacturing, agricultural, environmental, or field context;
- pathogenicity, toxicity, immunogenicity, and biological effect;
- transmissibility and route of transmission;
- host range and susceptible populations;
- environmental persistence and reproductive capacity;
- horizontal transfer, mutation, recombination, and evolutionary uncertainty;
- geographic and temporal spread;
- reversibility, recall, remediation, and decommissioning options;
- manufacturing and distribution scale;
- digital, automated, and supply-chain dependencies;
- dual-use and misuse capability;
- operator competence, safety culture, and financial capacity;
- containment and monitoring systems;
- worker, patient, participant, community, Indigenous, ecological, and intergenerational rights;
- data, sample, provenance, confidentiality, and protected-knowledge constraints;
- emergency detection, notification, response, and continuity arrangements;
- alternatives, including non-biotechnological options;
- major uncertainties, dissenting assessments, and conditions for reassessment.

## 3.3 Profile status

A BADIP is an assessment and coordination instrument. It does not constitute a licence, legal classification, public endorsement, safety guarantee, or authorization to disclose or transfer material.

## 3.4 Activity classes for routing

For routing rather than legal ranking, an activity may be described as one or more of:

- contained research;
- diagnostic or analytical use;
- clinical investigation;
- approved therapeutic or preventive use;
- manufacturing or scale-up;
- agricultural or veterinary use;
- environmental application;
- ecosystem-scale or self-propagating release;
- restricted dual-use work;
- emergency response or countermeasure production;
- decommissioning, remediation, or archival custody.

The same activity may move among classes as evidence, scale, or context changes.

# 4. Separation of functions

| Function | Permitted role | Constitutional limit |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific standards | develop test methods, containment guidance, terminology, and technical practices | does not issue licences or create law |
| Risk assessment | evaluate evidence, uncertainty, failure modes, alternatives, and safeguards | does not approve its own client or impose sanctions |
| Accreditation | assess assessor, laboratory, and auditor competence | does not decide the underlying legal authorization |
| Regulation | apply valid facility, product, clinical, occupational, environmental, transport, or release law | does not adjudicate its own contested enforcement action |
| Research ethics | protect participants and review research conduct | does not replace public, Indigenous, environmental, or labour authority |
| Indigenous authority | govern affected peoples’ territories, genetic material, knowledge, and legal relations | is not supplied by a universal GGF body |
| Incident coordination | share information and coordinate requested assistance | does not declare emergencies or command without a valid activation instrument |
| Access and financing | support procurement, manufacturing, public-interest licences, and capacity | does not impose taxes or expropriate intellectual property |
| Adjudication | decide disputes under valid jurisdiction and due process | remains separate from investigation and enforcement |
| Enforcement | execute valid regulatory or judicial orders | cannot create the order it executes |
| Learning | review outcomes, errors, dissent, and institutional performance | cannot retroactively legitimize unlawful action |

No institution should control the complete chain from risk definition through licence, incident command, judgment, and enforcement.

# 5. Scientific standards and technical guidance

## 5.1 Federated standards network

A **Federated Biosafety Standards Network** may convene laboratories, professional societies, public agencies, Indigenous and community experts where relevant, worker representatives, environmental scientists, clinicians, engineers, and standards bodies to develop voluntary or adoptable technical guidance.

The network may:

- compare existing biosafety and biosecurity standards;
- publish reference methods and minimum evidence fields;
- maintain openly documented revision histories;
- identify where standards conflict or do not travel across contexts;
- develop low-resource and offline-compatible alternatives;
- publish uncertainty, minority reports, and unresolved questions;
- support translation and accessibility.

It may not:

- issue binding global rules;
- certify legal compliance;
- approve releases;
- determine Indigenous authority or consent;
- declare a technology safe in every context;
- suppress lawful research disagreement.

## 5.2 Adoption

A standard becomes binding only when adopted by a competent authority through a valid legal, contractual, procurement, professional, or institutional instrument. The adopting instrument should identify the version, scope, affected actors, transition period, equivalent methods, review process, and challenge route.

## 5.3 Technology neutrality and equivalent safety

Standards should state required outcomes where possible rather than constitutionalizing one vendor, database, blockchain, genetic safeguard, automated tool, or laboratory architecture. Equivalent or superior local methods remain permissible where independently demonstrated.

# 6. Independent assessment and accreditation

## 6.1 Assessment bodies

Risk assessment should be performed by bodies whose mandate, competence, funding, conflicts, methods, data access, and liability are documented. High-consequence activities require plural assessment and an **Observation Independence Map** identifying shared evidence, shared models, common funding, common personnel, and correlated failure modes.

## 6.2 Assessment record

A high-consequence assessment records:

- the BADIP and activity boundary;
- questions the assessment can and cannot answer;
- evidence quality and provenance;
- assumptions and model dependence;
- comparator and baseline;
- plausible failure modes;
- uncertainty and disagreement;
- affected-party knowledge and objections;
- proposed safeguards and their failure modes;
- alternatives, including delay or non-deployment;
- monitoring and reversal requirements;
- conditions requiring reassessment.

## 6.3 Accreditation

Accreditation evaluates competence and process integrity. It does not guarantee the truth of an assessment or substitute for the regulator’s independent duty. Accreditation bodies must disclose funding, ownership, conflicts, appeals, assessor rotation, and reciprocal-recognition arrangements.

## 6.4 Developer-funded evidence

Where developers fund tests, independent replication, public-interest review, data access, and adverse-result publication should be required proportionate to risk. Confidentiality may protect legitimate trade secrets but cannot conceal safety evidence from competent reviewers, courts, workers, participants, or affected communities under applicable law.

# 7. Lawful regulation and licensing

## 7.1 Sources of authority

Facility, research, product, clinical, manufacturing, transport, environmental, and field-release decisions derive from valid law or other competent instruments. A licence record should identify:

- legal basis and issuing authority;
- jurisdiction and territorial scope;
- authorized activity and prohibited deviations;
- evidence and assessment relied upon;
- safety, monitoring, reporting, insurance, and continuity conditions;
- Indigenous, community, participant, patient, worker, and environmental interfaces;
- data and sample rules;
- duration, review, amendment, suspension, and revocation procedures;
- appeal and judicial review;
- decommissioning and post-closure duties.

## 7.2 No global licence by framework compliance

Compliance with an Aethelred standard, BADIP template, audit, or trustmark does not create legal permission. Conversely, lack of a GGF certification does not itself make an activity unlawful.

## 7.3 Narrow holds and suspensions

A regulator may use provisional holds only where authorized and only for the identified activity, material, facility, or release. Holds should state reasons, evidence, scope, continuity protections, duration, review, and compensation rules. A general decline in a social or ecological index cannot automatically pause every biotechnology activity in a region.

## 7.4 Licence conditions and access

Safety conditions should not be used to compel public disclosure of protected genetic information, Indigenous knowledge, patient data, or dangerous dual-use methods. A regulator should accept protected attestations, sealed evidence, confidential review, or local verification where they provide adequate assurance.

# 8. Facility governance, containment, and safety culture

## 8.1 Facility responsibility

Every regulated facility should maintain a **Facility Biosafety and Continuity Record** covering:

- accountable operator and beneficial ownership;
- authorized activities and materials;
- physical and biological containment;
- access controls and personnel competence;
- occupational health and exposure response;
- inventory and transfer controls;
- waste and effluent management;
- cybersecurity and laboratory automation;
- emergency notification and mutual aid;
- business continuity and safe shutdown;
- insurance, financial assurance, and remediation capacity;
- whistleblower and incident-reporting protections;
- decommissioning and archival duties.

## 8.2 Safety culture

Safety cannot be reduced to equipment compliance. Reviews should examine staffing, workload, maintenance, incentives, reporting climate, contractor management, near-miss treatment, production pressure, leadership conduct, and retaliation risk.

## 8.3 Worker participation

Workers and their representatives should participate in hazard assessment, incident planning, protective-equipment selection, exposure monitoring, shift design, training, and after-action review. Worker reports have evidentiary value but are not automatically conclusive.

## 8.4 No compelled heroism

No worker may be compelled to continue unsafe biological work merely because the activity is described as nationally, globally, scientifically, or commercially important. Emergency exceptions require valid law, necessity, protective equipment, training, compensation, medical support, time limits, and independent review.

# 9. Field trials and environmental release

## 9.1 Release-specific decision

A field trial or release requires a decision specific to:

- construct and phenotype;
- release purpose;
- geography and ecological context;
- operator and responsible parties;
- scale, timing, and duration;
- affected communities and jurisdictions;
- Indigenous territories, species relations, and knowledge;
- monitoring and detection capability;
- recall, suppression, mitigation, or containment options;
- cross-border and intergenerational effects;
- liability and restoration funding.

## 9.2 Affected-party process

Affected communities should receive timely, accessible information and meaningful opportunity to question, commission advice, propose alternatives, and participate in monitoring. This process does not manufacture jurisdiction where none exists, and a general community assembly does not replace Indigenous authority, individual rights, environmental law, or national and local public law.

## 9.3 Indigenous territories and relations

No GGF-created Earth Council or fixed representative quota may authorize a release affecting a people. Affected Indigenous peoples determine their own representatives, procedures, evidence rules, consent requirements, and conditions for engagement. Refusal, non-engagement, and protected non-transfer remain legitimate outcomes.

## 9.4 Transboundary release

Where plausible spread crosses jurisdictions, the proponent and competent regulators should establish notification, consultation, monitoring, liability, evidence-sharing, incident, and restoration interfaces before release. Absence of agreement does not automatically authorize a global body to decide.

## 9.5 No deployment-by-emergency shortcut

An emergency does not automatically convert an experimental field release into a lawful intervention. Urgent use requires the applicable emergency, health, environmental, research, and Indigenous authority records and the least irreversible feasible option.

# 10. Containment, reversibility, and failure modes

## 10.1 Containment strategy

High-consequence activities require a layered containment strategy addressing physical, biological, operational, digital, supply-chain, and geographic controls. Each layer should identify assumptions, dependencies, detection thresholds, common-mode failures, and degradation modes.

## 10.2 Biological safeguards

Kill switches, auxotrophy, reproductive limits, genetic barriers, dependency systems, and other biological safeguards may be useful, but no safeguard is presumed universally available, stable, effective, or sufficient. Claims require independent verification under relevant conditions and continuing monitoring for mutation, escape, bypass, or ecological adaptation.

## 10.3 Reversibility record

A **Reversibility, Recall, and Remediation Record** should identify:

- what can realistically be stopped or recalled;
- expected delay between detection and action;
- spatial and temporal limits;
- ecological and clinical side effects of reversal;
- fallback options if the primary safeguard fails;
- material, workforce, funding, and authorization dependencies;
- residual harm that cannot be reversed;
- responsible authority and operator;
- compensation and restoration arrangements.

## 10.4 Irreversibility

Where an activity is materially irreversible, decision-makers should not describe it as reversible merely because monitoring or a theoretical suppression method exists. Irreversibility increases the required evidence, participation, alternatives analysis, and legal authorization.

---

# 11. Monitoring, recall, decommissioning, and long-term stewardship

Monitoring obligations should be proportional to persistence and consequence and continue for as long as scientifically and legally justified. They should specify:

- parameters and baselines;
- responsible observers;
- observation independence;
- data and sample authority;
- protected knowledge and privacy;
- trigger interpretation;
- false positive and false negative handling;
- notification pathways;
- funding and institutional continuity;
- public and protected reporting layers;
- termination criteria.

A recall, suppression, remediation, or decommissioning plan must address worker safety, patient continuity, environmental harm, affected livelihoods, archive and evidence preservation, and the possibility that intervention causes more harm than continued monitoring.

Operators should not be permitted to dissolve, relocate, transfer assets, or abandon facilities in a way that defeats long-term monitoring, liability, or remediation. Financial assurance may be required under valid law.

# 12. Human biological liberty and informed consent

## 12.1 Protected decisions

Human biological liberty includes, subject to valid law and the rights of others:

- refusing research participation;
- refusing genetic testing or disclosure;
- choosing among lawful therapeutic options;
- refusing enhancement or non-therapeutic modification;
- receiving understandable information about biological intervention;
- withdrawing consent where withdrawal remains possible;
- seeking correction, deletion, return, or restricted use of samples and data;
- accessing human review and remedy;
- freedom from discrimination based on genotype, phenotype, disability, ancestry, reproductive decision, or refusal.

## 12.2 Consent quality

Consent must be informed, specific, comprehensible, accessible, voluntary, and documented in a form appropriate to context. Employment, healthcare dependency, benefits, detention, education, military hierarchy, migration status, family pressure, or severe scarcity may compromise voluntariness and require additional safeguards or prohibit the activity.

## 12.3 Future use

Consent to one study, diagnosis, treatment, repository, or commercial use does not automatically authorize unrelated research, AI training, reproductive use, law-enforcement access, ancestry inference, or transfer to another jurisdiction.

## 12.4 Children and persons with impaired decision capacity

Governance must distinguish assent, consent, supported decision-making, substituted decision-making, emergency necessity, and long-term interests. Convenience, institutional benefit, or speculative social benefit is not sufficient to override bodily integrity.

# 13. Heritable and germline interventions

## 13.1 No framework-created universal ban or permission

The distinction between therapy, prevention, enhancement, trait selection, and disability elimination is scientifically, ethically, socially, and legally contested. This Accord does not itself create a universal ban or permission.

## 13.2 Precautionary default

Jurisdictions may adopt a precautionary default against clinical heritable intervention until they establish valid law, scientific and clinical evidence, long-term monitoring, reproductive-rights review, disability-rights review, intergenerational representation, equitable access analysis, and amendment procedures.

## 13.3 Required analysis

Any proposed heritable intervention should address:

- medical indication and alternatives;
- embryo, pregnancy, child, and future-person interests;
- off-target, mosaic, pleiotropic, epigenetic, and multigenerational effects;
- disability and eugenics risks;
- reproductive autonomy and coercion;
- unequal access and stratification;
- cultural and Indigenous concerns;
- data and family privacy;
- follow-up burdens and right not to know;
- reversibility limitations;
- governance across generations and jurisdictions.

## 13.4 No Indigenous proxy review

An Indigenous perspective may be essential where a people is affected or chooses to participate. No universal Indigenous body reviews the ethics of every human germline decision on behalf of all peoples.

---

# 14. Reproductive justice, disability rights, and anti-eugenics safeguards

Biotechnology policy must not define some lives as less worthy, burdensome, defective, or socially undesirable. Disability prevention, treatment, accommodation, and reproductive choice are distinct questions and should not be collapsed into one optimization target.

Required safeguards include:

- participation of disabled people and representative organizations;
- protection against insurer, employer, state, family, or market coercion;
- access to supports and accommodations independent of genetic decisions;
- non-directive counselling;
- protection from discriminatory prenatal, reproductive, or population policy;
- prohibition of non-consensual sterilization or reproductive intervention;
- review of algorithmic and genomic bias;
- recognition of uncertainty and variable expression;
- protection of reproductive privacy;
- accessible appeal and remedy.

Success metrics may not be based on reducing the number of people with a disability, increasing uptake of a preferred reproductive technology, or producing a target population genotype.

# 15. Indigenous genetic rights and nation-specific interfaces

## 15.1 Recognition, not creation

The Accord recognizes Indigenous peoples’ pre-existing authority; it does not create, rank, certify, or replace it. Each affected people determines its lawful representatives and protocols.

## 15.2 Protected objects and relations

Authority may attach not only to samples or datasets but to:

- ancestors and descendants;
- kinship relations;
- sacred or restricted knowledge;
- species and ecological relations;
- place and territory;
- language and naming;
- provenance and history of collection;
- collective identity and misuse risk;
- obligations of return, care, silence, or non-commercialization.

## 15.3 Permissible conditions

An Indigenous authority may require:

- no collection or no digitization;
- local or nation-controlled custody;
- non-transfer or case-specific transfer;
- no commercial use;
- no patent claims;
- no AI training;
- no publication of sequence or phenotype;
- aggregate or sealed attestation only;
- benefit sharing;
- co-authorship or attribution;
- return, deletion, destruction, or repatriation;
- community monitoring;
- nation-specific dispute resolution.

## 15.4 Emergency requests

An emergency request for access to Indigenous samples, data, territory, or knowledge must use the Emergency Interface Specification and the relevant nation-specific interface. A claim of catastrophic importance does not itself erase authority. Where no valid pathway exists, the status may remain unresolved while alternative samples, methods, or interventions are pursued.

# 16. Federated provenance, authority, and protected non-transfer

## 16.1 No universal public genetic registry

The former Genetic Sovereignty Registry is replaced by federated records. No universal blockchain, Love Ledger entry, public-read database, or global ownership map is required.

## 16.2 Record types

Depending on context, a system may use:

- local custodial records;
- jurisdictional registries;
- Indigenous nation records;
- repository records;
- clinical and research records;
- chain-of-custody records;
- sealed judicial evidence;
- aggregate attestations;
- cryptographic proofs without content disclosure;
- offline or paper records;
- time-limited transfer permits.

## 16.3 Genetic Use, Authority, and Provenance Record

A **Genetic Use, Authority, and Provenance Record (GUAPR)** should identify, where lawful:

- material or data category without unnecessary content disclosure;
- collector and collection context;
- custodians and authorized decision-makers;
- personal, relational, familial, collective, Indigenous, evidentiary, clinical, research, and public-interest claims;
- permitted and prohibited uses;
- transfer and onward-transfer rules;
- publication and AI-training rules;
- retention, return, deletion, destruction, and repatriation;
- benefit-sharing terms;
- dispute forum and applicable law;
- provenance gaps and contested claims.

## 16.4 Classification is not ownership

A record may carry overlapping claims. Describing data as personal, collective, public-interest, or ecological does not automatically determine ownership, access, disclosure, deletion, or commercialization.

# 17. Community and affected-party deliberation

## 17.1 Optional deliberative bodies

Jurisdictions may establish **Biotechnology Affected-Party Panels** or other deliberative bodies to review proposed activities, surface local knowledge, examine distributional effects, and propose conditions or alternatives.

## 17.2 Limits

Such bodies do not automatically possess legislative, regulatory, judicial, or Indigenous authority. Fixed representation percentages do not create jurisdiction. Their legal effect depends on the constituting instrument.

## 17.3 Design

A deliberative process should address:

- who is affected and who is absent;
- accessibility, language, disability, youth, and care needs;
- independent advice and community-controlled expertise;
- conflicts and funding;
- confidential and protected knowledge;
- reasons, dissent, and unresolved questions;
- retaliation and economic dependency;
- compensation for participation;
- how recommendations enter the lawful decision process.

## 17.4 No satisfaction-as-consent

Trust scores, satisfaction surveys, and approval percentages are evaluation evidence. They do not substitute for individual consent, Indigenous authority, legal adoption, or public jurisdiction.

# 18. Workers, researchers, participants, and whistleblowers

## 18.1 Covered people

Protections apply to laboratory staff, cleaners, animal-care workers, contractors, couriers, field workers, manufacturing personnel, clinicians, community monitors, data workers, maintenance personnel, emergency responders, students, and research participants.

## 18.2 Minimum protections

- safe staffing and workload;
- training and supervision;
- protective equipment and vaccination where appropriate;
- exposure monitoring and confidential healthcare;
- wages, hazard pay, insurance, and compensation;
- reporting without retaliation;
- work stoppage and safe refusal rights under applicable law;
- independent inspection and worker representation;
- family and caregiving support after exposure or deployment;
- immigration and employment-status protection for reporters;
- mental-health and trauma support;
- retraining and income protection during closure or transition.

## 18.3 Whistleblower evidence

Whistleblower reports may trigger assessment, inspection, or protective action but do not automatically establish guilt. Identity and source protection should be compatible with defence rights and judicial review.

## 18.4 Research integrity

Institutions should protect adverse-result publication, correction, replication, dissent, negative findings, and retraction without treating disagreement as misconduct.

# 19. Biological-risk incidents and shared handoff

## 19.1 Common interface with Global Health v2.2.1

Aethelred governs biotechnology standards, facility and release assessment, genetic rights, access policy, and incident preparedness within lawful interfaces. Global Health v2.2.1 governs population-health observation, clinical response, public-health assistance, countermeasure allocation, and health continuity.

## 19.2 Incident classes

An incident may be provisionally described as:

- occupational exposure;
- containment deviation or near miss;
- confirmed containment failure;
- unauthorized access or transfer;
- data or sequence compromise;
- manufacturing contamination;
- field-trial deviation;
- uncontrolled environmental release;
- unexpected phenotype or ecological effect;
- suspected deliberate misuse;
- unknown or mixed event.

Classification remains revisable.

## 19.3 Laboratory or Field-Release Handoff Record

The handoff records:

- observed event and confidence;
- material, construct, organism, or process;
- location, facility, operator, and responsible authorities;
- exposure and transmission possibilities;
- environmental pathway and persistence;
- worker, participant, patient, community, and ecological needs;
- immediate containment already taken and its legal basis;
- sample and evidence provenance;
- data, confidentiality, Indigenous, and dual-use restrictions;
- requested health, laboratory, environmental, cyber, logistics, or security assistance;
- command and decision boundaries;
- public communication responsibility;
- review, termination, and restoration requirements.

## 19.4 No automatic escalation

A laboratory alert, model output, community report, or suspected release may justify investigation and voluntary precautions. It does not automatically activate the Planetary Immune System, close a facility, seize materials, or authorize foreign deployment.

# 20. Emergency action and continuity

All emergency action follows the Emergency, Catastrophic-Risk, and Continuity Interface Specification v0.1.

## 20.1 Layered operation

- **Ordinary layer:** preparedness, inspections, exercises, stockpiles, mutual aid, and lawful prevention.
- **Alert layer:** non-binding notification and plural state estimation.
- **Assistance layer:** requested or pre-authorized technical and logistical support.
- **Provisional layer:** narrow action for no more than 72 hours under a valid emergency basis.
- **Continued layer:** action renewed in no more than 30-day increments, with extraordinary review beyond 90 cumulative days.

## 20.2 Power-specific activation

Each activated power must identify:

- legal basis;
- competent authority;
- factual predicate;
- affected activity, place, material, and population;
- permitted actions and prohibited actions;
- command structure;
- rights and protected minima;
- evidence, data, and secrecy rules;
- review and challenge;
- duration and termination;
- restoration and compensation.

## 20.3 Possible actions

Depending on valid law, actions may include:

- voluntary facility stand-down;
- operator-initiated safe shutdown;
- regulator hold or licence action;
- court injunction;
- exposure assessment and medical support;
- material isolation or controlled transport;
- environmental containment;
- worker evacuation;
- product recall;
- temporary public advisory;
- requested international technical assistance.

These are distinct powers and should not be activated as one package.

## 20.4 Continuity

Emergency action must protect essential healthcare, safe biological production, food systems, legitimate research, workers, dependants, and affected communities. Broad sector shutdowns require exceptional justification and continuity planning.

# 23. Dual-use research, information, and security

## 23.1 Dual-use assessment

Dual-use governance should evaluate capability, accessibility, tacit knowledge, automation, scalability, detectability, misuse pathway, defensive value, and the harm created by restriction itself.

## 23.2 Layered disclosure

Information may be:

- openly published;
- published with safety redactions;
- shared with accredited reviewers;
- shared through controlled research networks;
- retained under protected institutional access;
- held as sealed evidence;
- restricted for a defined period under valid law;
- not digitized or not transferred.

## 23.3 No secrecy by framework label

Calling information “biosecurity sensitive” does not itself authorize secrecy. Restrictions require a valid basis, reasons, scope, duration, review, and a route to challenge. Secrecy may not conceal misconduct, exposure, worker danger, unlawful experimentation, or evidence needed for remedy.

## 23.4 Responsible communication

Public communication should distinguish confirmed fact, provisional assessment, unknowns, and security-limited details. It should not use crisis communication to suppress lawful criticism or manufacture consent for a policy.

## 23.5 Cyber and automated laboratory systems

Digital design tools, cloud laboratories, automated synthesis, access controls, model interfaces, and sequence-order systems follow Technology Governance v3.6 and Aurora v1.3. No universal digital identity or mandatory blockchain is required.

# 24. Biotechnology access and equity

## 24.1 Access objective

Safe and effective essential biotechnologies should be available without discrimination based on wealth, geography, disability, citizenship, ethnicity, or participation in unrelated GGF systems.

## 24.2 Federated access facility

A **Biotechnology Access and Equity Network** may coordinate public purchasers, regional manufacturers, health systems, research institutions, philanthropic funders, patent pools, technology-transfer partners, and community organizations.

It may:

- map unmet need and production bottlenecks;
- support regional manufacturing and quality systems;
- negotiate voluntary licences and patent pools;
- finance trials and implementation research;
- support cold-chain and offline delivery;
- fund neglected and non-commercial needs;
- publish access, price, and public-investment information;
- support Indigenous and community-controlled biotechnology where invited.

It may not impose levies, compel licences, expropriate property, or allocate public funds without a valid instrument.

## 24.3 Essential status

A scientific panel may recommend that a product or capability is essential. Legal consequences—procurement priority, price regulation, compulsory licensing, stockpiling, or public manufacturing—derive from competent health, patent, trade, procurement, or emergency law.

## 24.4 Benefit sharing

Benefit sharing may include compensation, royalties, community infrastructure, local manufacturing, healthcare access, capacity, co-governance, attribution, return of material, non-commercial commitments, or ecological restoration. It should not be reduced to one financial formula.

# 25. Intellectual property, procurement, and manufacturing

## 25.1 Public-interest conditions

Public funding agreements may lawfully require access conditions such as:

- transparent public investment;
- reasonable pricing;
- non-exclusive or regional licences;
- technology transfer;
- manufacturing readiness;
- open standards;
- data-access commitments compatible with rights;
- stockpile and surge obligations;
- continuity and shortage plans.

## 25.2 Compulsory powers

Compulsory licensing, government use, patent limitation, market authorization, export control, price regulation, or acquisition require valid law and due process. The Accord cannot trigger them by itself.

## 25.3 Manufacturing safety

Scale-up receives its own BADIP and regulatory review. A method that is safe in research may create different hazards at manufacturing scale through volume, workforce, waste, supply chain, automation, or distribution.

## 25.4 Access during emergencies

Emergency access instruments should specify evidence, product quality, liability, procurement integrity, equitable allocation, technology transfer, review, termination, and post-emergency disposition. An emergency does not make every patent or proprietary process a global commons.

# 26. Data, samples, AI, and cyber governance

Aurora Accord v1.3 governs personal, relational, familial, collective, Indigenous-restricted, evidentiary, clinical, research, environmental, and security-sensitive data claims.

## 26.1 Minimum necessary data

Collect only what is necessary for the defined function. Separate clinical care, research, regulation, incident response, law enforcement, commercial use, and AI training.

## 26.2 Samples

Samples are not merely data containers. They may carry bodily, familial, reproductive, ancestral, Indigenous, evidentiary, commercial, and ecological claims. Collection, transfer, retention, secondary use, destruction, return, and repatriation require specific authority.

## 26.3 AI-assisted design and assessment

AI may support literature review, sequence analysis, simulation, laboratory control, anomaly detection, and documentation. It may not independently determine consent, legal status, Indigenous authority, facility closure, criminal liability, heritable intervention approval, or field-release authorization.

## 26.4 Training data and models

Biological data used for model training requires provenance, authority, purpose limits, security, withdrawal or future-use rules, and residual-influence analysis. A model’s public availability does not cure unlawful training or protected-knowledge use.

## 26.5 Audit trails

Audit integrity does not require immutable public storage of biological content. Logs should be tamper-evident, correctable, access-controlled, and compatible with deletion, sealing, source protection, and protected non-transfer.

# 27. Environmental and ecological responsibility

Biotechnology assessment should examine:

- non-target species and ecological relations;
- biodiversity and genetic diversity;
- ecosystem functions and feedbacks;
- horizontal gene transfer;
- resistance and evolutionary response;
- cumulative and interacting releases;
- land, water, soil, air, and food pathways;
- containment and monitoring capacity;
- affected livelihoods and subsistence;
- Indigenous and local ecological authority;
- restoration feasibility and wrongful-measure harm.

Precaution should be calibrated by severity, plausibility, reversibility, alternatives, uncertainty, and the harms of action and inaction. Ecological metrics may trigger investigation or pre-authorized support, but they do not automatically establish causation or legal power.

Guardians or representatives for ecosystems must be constituted by valid law and have defined duties, conflicts, accountability, and standing. The Accord does not create one universal guardian.

# 28. Justice, evidence, liability, and remedy

Justice Systems v1.4 controls jurisdiction, standing, applicable law, due process, evidence, interim measures, remedy, appeal, recognition, and enforcement.

## 28.1 Evidence

Biotechnology disputes may involve:

- laboratory records;
- validation and quality systems;
- sequence and sample evidence;
- exposure records;
- environmental monitoring;
- model outputs;
- expert disagreement;
- worker and community testimony;
- Indigenous-protected evidence;
- trade secrets and security restrictions;
- chain of custody;
- missing or destroyed records.

Protected review, confidentiality, or sealed proceedings may be necessary, but respondents retain a fair opportunity to know and challenge the case against them.

## 28.2 Liability

Liability may arise under applicable product, negligence, environmental, occupational, clinical, research, contract, Indigenous, criminal, administrative, or Treaty law. A risk profile or adverse event does not automatically establish fault or causation.

## 28.3 Remedies

Possible remedies include:

- medical care and monitoring;
- worker and participant compensation;
- correction and disclosure;
- injunction or lawful activity restriction;
- product recall;
- sample return, deletion, destruction, or repatriation;
- data-use restriction and model remediation;
- ecological restoration;
- community infrastructure;
- benefit sharing;
- licence or governance correction;
- institutional reform;
- costs and legal aid.

Remedies should protect innocent workers, patients, communities, research participants, and essential services.

## 28.4 No veto quota

The legality of Indigenous refusal, community objection, regulatory intervention, or judicial override cannot be evaluated by an annual target number. A single unlawful override remains unlawful.

# 29. Finance, insurance, and economic transition

## 29.1 Lawful funding

Funding may come from valid public budgets, grants, contracts, insurance, bonds, licence fees, settlements, voluntary contributions, or separately enacted levies. The Accord does not impose a two-percent biotechnology tax, Automation Tax allocation, or Love Ledger reward.

## 29.2 Independence

Assessors, regulators, public-interest researchers, access programmes, legal-aid bodies, and incident investigators require funding firewalls. Fee-funded systems should disclose dependence and prevent pay-to-approve incentives.

## 29.3 Financial assurance

Jurisdictions may require insurance, bonds, reserve funds, parent guarantees, or restoration trusts proportionate to plausible harm. Trigger, causation, forfeiture, appeal, and use of funds require valid law.

## 29.4 Workers and communities

Closure, recall, prohibition, or transition should include wages, income continuity, retraining, relocation support, health monitoring, pension protection, community tax-base transition, and remediation employment where lawful.

# 30. Monitoring, evaluation, learning, and anti-Goodhart safeguards

## 30.1 Learning questions

Evaluation should ask:

- Were hazards detected early enough?
- Were state estimates independent and honest about uncertainty?
- Did assessment and regulation remain separate?
- Were workers and affected communities able to report and refuse safely?
- Were Indigenous authority and protected non-transfer respected?
- Were access and benefits fairly distributed?
- Did monitoring detect unintended effects?
- Did safeguards work under real conditions?
- Did emergency action remain within scope and time?
- Were facilities, data, samples, and authority properly returned?
- Were harms repaired?
- Did the intervention reduce or increase long-term risk?

## 30.2 Metrics as evidence, not switches

Incident counts, trust scores, species measures, cultural-cohesion indices, audit results, access coverage, and model estimates inform review. They do not automatically pause a sector, authorize intervention, establish causation, or prove legitimacy.

## 30.3 Suggested measures

- near-miss and incident reporting quality;
- time to worker protection and medical support;
- unresolved safety findings;
- assessor independence;
- licence-condition compliance;
- monitoring completeness;
- recall and remediation readiness;
- access, affordability, and regional manufacturing;
- participant and community access to remedy;
- protected-data and sample breaches;
- wrongful holds or shutdowns;
- dissent and adverse-result publication;
- time to authority return after emergency action.

Targets should not require a minimum number of violations found, approvals issued, projects scaled, vetoes overridden, or people persuaded.

# 33. Constitutional readiness gates

A binding implementation is not ready unless it passes all applicable gates:

1. **No framework-to-law conversion:** Accord text is not treated as self-executing law.
2. **No combined biosafety sovereign:** Standards, assessment, licensing, emergency, adjudication, and enforcement are separated.
3. **Authority provenance:** Every consequential action identifies a valid source and competent authority.
4. **No residual jurisdiction:** Unassigned powers remain unauthorized.
5. **Activity-specific profile:** Governance attaches to a defined activity and context, not a universal tier alone.
6. **Plural observation:** High-consequence assessments disclose correlated evidence and model dependence.
7. **Independent challenge:** Assessor, regulator, and adjudicator roles are distinct.
8. **No universal Indigenous proxy:** Affected peoples identify their own authority.
9. **Protected non-transfer:** Non-digitization, local custody, refusal, return, and deletion are operationally possible.
10. **No automatic metric pause:** Indices trigger inquiry, not legal power.
11. **No general veto override:** Emergency interfaces do not presume a tribunal may override Indigenous refusal.
12. **Biological liberty:** Consent, refusal, bodily integrity, and non-discrimination are protected.
13. **Disability and reproductive justice:** No eugenic outcome target or coerced reproductive policy.
14. **Worker protection:** Safety, wages, reporting, refusal, healthcare, and compensation are funded.
15. **Containment realism:** Safeguards disclose failure modes and do not rely on a nominal kill switch.
16. **Field-release reversibility:** Monitoring, recall, restoration, and financial assurance are credible.
17. **Dual-use safeguards:** Disclosure and restriction are lawful, reviewable, and layered.
18. **Data and sample authority:** Aurora-compatible provenance and lifecycle rules exist.
19. **Emergency interface:** The 72/30/90 architecture and power-specific activation are implemented.
20. **No PIS takeover:** PIS support remains observational and coordinative unless separately activated.
21. **Justice separation:** Evidence, interim measures, liability, remedy, and appeal follow Justice Systems v1.4.
22. **Lawful access powers:** Patent, procurement, pricing, and manufacturing actions derive from valid law.
23. **Restoration and compensation:** Wrongful action and biological harm have funded remedy pathways.
24. **Failure can stop:** Pilots and institutions may narrow, not scale, or dissolve.

# 34. Operational records

## Record inheritance and conformance

1. **Specialization does not replace the canonical set.**  
   This framework's domain record list specializes but does not replace the records defined by the Emergency, Catastrophic-Risk, and Continuity Interface Specification and the Emergency and Catastrophic-Risk Cluster Record Profile. Every canonical record remains required whenever its applicability trigger occurs.

2. **Canonical identity.**  
   Every domain record shall identify the canonical `ECRC-##` record or records it `implements`, `extends`, `splits`, `contains`, or `imports`. A domain label may differ from the canonical title, but the canonical identifier, schema version, minimum common header, legal effect, access protections, and lifecycle obligations remain traceable.

3. **Direct import.**  
   A canonical record not mapped to a domain specialization is imported directly when triggered. Absence from a domain appendix, implementation guide, software form, dashboard, or local registry does not make the canonical record optional.

4. **Non-applicability.**  
   A record may be marked `not_applicable` only through a reasoned, event- or action-specific determination identifying: the trigger tested; the deciding function; the evidence and legal basis; the reason the trigger cannot arise or has not arisen; the records that would become required if conditions change; and the review or expiry condition. `Not applicable` does not mean unknown, inconvenient, deferred, or unavailable.

5. **Split records.**  
   Where several domain records jointly implement one canonical record, the framework shall declare a `splits` relationship, identify every component, allocate each required canonical field, designate the function that verifies completeness, and preserve one traceable lifecycle. No component inherits the authority or legal effect of another by implication.

6. **Containing packages.**  
   A domain package may contain several canonical records only when each canonical subrecord remains separately identifiable, reviewable, correctable, exportable, and closable, with its own responsible authority, legal effect, timestamp, expiry, and access class. The Investigation and Evidence Handoff Record (`ECRC-24`) and the Adjudication and Enforcement Handoff Record (`ECRC-25`) shall remain distinct even when displayed in one user interface or transmitted in one package.

7. **No record-to-power conversion.**  
   A record name, canonical identifier, completed form, model output, dashboard state, validation result, digital signature, institutional endorsement, or reference to another framework does not create authority. Consequential action requires a valid Emergency Authority Provenance Record (`ECRC-06`) and every other record required by the applicable activation package.

8. **Shared event identity without shared command.**  
   Records participating in a compound or cross-domain event shall carry the same `cluster_event_id` and identify their responsible function, authority, handoff state, evidence or data custodian, review time, and closure state. Shared identity coordinates information; it does not merge jurisdictions, transfer command, or establish a common legal classification.

9. **Handoff acceptance and retained duties.**  
   A handoff is effective only when the receiving function records acceptance, rejection, deficiency, or concurrent responsibility. A handoff transfers only the responsibility expressly named. It does not erase the sender's retained duties, source obligations, correction duties, evidence-preservation duties, or responsibility for prior acts.

10. **Lifecycle completeness.**  
    Any activation package that can restrict rights, deploy personnel or assets, process emergency data, spend extraordinary funds, or continue beyond a provisional period shall also represent renewal, termination, authority and asset restoration, emergency-data disposition, wrongful-action remedy, after-action review, institutional sunset or continuation, and unresolved risk or dissent. A package is not closed merely because a declaration or mission has ended.

11. **Access and protected material.**  
    Canonical identity does not require universal publication. Public summaries, protected annexes, restricted operational records, protected sovereignty or source records, and sealed adjudicative or security material shall follow the access classes in `ECRC/0.1`. Indigenous authority, non-engagement, protected knowledge, source protection, privacy, security, correction, retention, deletion, return, repatriation, and non-transfer obligations survive transport between frameworks.

12. **Correction, supersession, and integrity.**  
    Record identifiers remain immutable. Corrections, withdrawals, supersession, invalidation, and disputes shall preserve the prior state, reason, responsible actor, time, and linked replacement. Hashes and automated validation may support integrity but do not prove authenticity, accuracy, legal validity, or authority.

13. **Precedence and conflict.**  
    Where a domain record conflicts with the canonical profile, the rule that better preserves valid authority provenance, functional separation, rights, protected knowledge, temporal limits, restoration, and review controls for cluster interoperability controls. A domain framework may impose stronger safeguards. It may not weaken the canonical safeguards through renaming, omission, aggregation, software implementation, or local practice.

14. **Version pinning.**  
    This framework conforms only to the exact profile and interface versions identified in its compatibility manifest. A later framework, profile, schema, or interface version is not substituted automatically. Material changes fail closed until a successful compatibility and scenario test is recorded.

15. **Conformance appendix.**  
    This framework shall publish a complete crosswalk for `ECRC-01` through `ECRC-33`, showing for each canonical record: domain record name; inheritance relationship; applicability rule; responsible function; default access class; required linked records; lifecycle closure; and any current gap. Conformance is not established by a general statement that another framework controls.

16. **Automated systems.**  
    Automated systems may check schema, completeness, links, expiry, consistency, signatures, and access rules. They may not determine whether an authority is legally valid, whether evidence is credible, whether a person is liable or guilty, whether an Indigenous authority has consented, or whether an emergency power should be exercised or renewed.

17. **Failure rule.**  
    Missing, invalid, expired, incompatible, or unresolved authority and lifecycle records block the affected consequential action. They do not transfer authority to a coordinating body, observatory, emergency council, court, fund, platform, model, or residual command.

> **Framework conformance statement:** The Aethelred Accord v1.2.1 adopts Emergency Record Inheritance Clause v0.1 and conforms to Emergency and Catastrophic-Risk Cluster Record Profile v0.1 only to the extent shown in its complete ECRC-01–ECRC-33 crosswalk. Unmapped records are imported directly when triggered. Recorded gaps block the affected consequential action until resolved.

# 35. Conclusion

The responsible stewardship of life’s code cannot be achieved by placing all biotechnology under one global council, one public registry, one risk ladder, or one emergency switch. Biotechnology crosses bodies, families, species, ecosystems, territories, professions, markets, and generations. Its governance must therefore be plural, explicit, and interface-based.

The Aethelred Accord v1.2.1 preserves the moral ambition of preventing catastrophic biological harm while making five constitutional corrections:

1. risk classification no longer manufactures jurisdiction;
2. Indigenous authority is recognized rather than represented by a universal proxy;
3. protected biological material and knowledge need not enter a global registry;
4. emergency action is power-specific, time-bounded, and reviewable;
5. access to essential biotechnology is pursued through lawful public-interest instruments rather than self-authorized taxation or expropriation.

A mature biosafety system does not promise perfect control over life. It builds the capacity to know what is being done, by whom, under whose authority, with which uncertainties, protections, alternatives, and responsibilities—and to stop, repair, compensate, or decline deployment when those conditions cannot be met.

## Appendix conformance rules

- A domain record carries the canonical identifier in its header and preserves the common 27-field header defined by `ECRC/0.1`.
- A `direct canonical import` is instantiated from the profile without waiting for a domain-specific form.
- `ECRC-24` and `ECRC-25` are separate records even when transmitted in one package or displayed in one interface.
- A package that reaches termination is not closed until required restoration, data disposition, claims transfer, unresolved-risk custody, and archival steps are complete.
- Any later interface, profile, clause, or domain-framework version requires a recorded compatibility test before substitution.
