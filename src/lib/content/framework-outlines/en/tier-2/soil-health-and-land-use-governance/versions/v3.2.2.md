## 0. Controlled successor notice

Soil v3.2 preserves the source framework’s central vision: land as relation rather than commodity; living soil and microbial communities as foundations of food, water, health, biodiversity, and culture; Indigenous stewardship and rematriation; regenerative agriculture; seed and knowledge commons; community land trusts; temporal justice; urban–rural integration; and practical support for farmers and communities making a difficult transition.

It replaces the v3.1 assumption that a BAZ, council, global accord, score, certificate, registry, target, future-being report, emergency label, or Treaty reference can move directly from soil concern to rights recognition, a land veto, a tax, a sale restriction, a title transfer, tenure revocation, trade sanction, emergency receivership, or coercive intervention.

> **Soil urgency does not create land jurisdiction.**

> **Measurement is not mandate. Soil rights are not title. Planning is not adjudication. Community voice is not an unbounded veto. A tax is not a trade sanction. Certification is not a permit. A registry is not ownership. Emergency need is not receivership.**

This framework may observe, interpret, advise, deliberate, plan, educate, support restoration, administer separately chartered programmes or funds, prepare lawful records, and refer matters to competent authorities. It cannot create a global land sovereign, authenticate sacredness for another people, recognize rights, transfer title, rematriate land, impose universal taxes or bans, revoke tenure, allocate resettlement land, adjudicate claims, declare emergencies, or command coercive execution.

## 2. Vision and constitutional purpose

Healthy soil is a living relationship among minerals, water, air, plants, animals, fungi, microbes, people, memory, work, and place. Soil governance therefore cannot be reduced either to private title or to a single ecological score. It must coordinate knowledge, rights, land law, food systems, housing, livelihoods, public health, ecological repair, and intergenerational responsibility while keeping their authorities visible and challengeable.

The framework connects seven functions without merging them:

1. **ecological intelligence** through EIOI-compatible soil and microbial observation, method, interpretation, uncertainty, and correction;
2. **affected-nation and community authority** through the specific Indigenous nations, customary systems, residents, tenants, workers, farmers, landless people, and food-system users concerned;
3. **rights and guardianship pathways** through CERGTA and separately competent legal authorities;
4. **land, title, trust, and rematriation pathways** through Hearthstone v2.0 and applicable property, Indigenous, public, and reparative law;
5. **land-use, agricultural, tax, permit, and inspection powers** only where individually enacted and institutionally separated;
6. **transition finance and benefits** through separately authorized funds, AUBI, insurance, procurement, and credit systems;
7. **adjudication, emergency action, restoration, and enforcement** through independent legal, CDEE/ECRC, CERGTA, and SCPA routes.

## 3. Core principles
### 3.1 Living soil without metaphysical compulsion

Soil may be treated ethically and, where law provides, legally as a living commons or rights-holder. The framework’s relational and sacred language guides care; it does not impose a metaphysical doctrine or create legal status by declaration.

### 3.2 Affected-nation authority

The specific affected Indigenous nation or nations determine representation, law, FPIC, sacred relations, land and sample authority, protected knowledge, and whether to engage. Global representation cannot consent for another people.

### 3.3 Land as relation with plural lawful interests

Title, customary tenure, occupancy, tenancy, stewardship, public duties, ecological rights, creditor interests, and reparative claims can coexist. No one category silently erases the others.

### 3.4 Observation–consequence separation

Soil samples, satellite imagery, AI outputs, dashboards, certificates, Love Ledger entries, BHI or LMCI modules, and thresholds may trigger review. They do not themselves create legal consequences.

### 3.5 Regeneration with food, housing, and livelihoods

Regenerative transition must protect food security, housing, workers, farmers, customary users, disability access, and essential water and services. Ecological gain cannot hide displacement or deprivation.

### 3.6 Microbial commons with sovereignty and biosafety

Microbial diversity and regenerative knowledge should be protected from enclosure, but “open source” never overrides affected-nation authority, sample provenance, biosafety, benefit sharing, privacy, or the right to withhold.

### 3.7 Precaution with review and expiry

Uncertainty may justify study, monitoring, a temporary pause, or a request for urgent protection. It does not justify indefinite restrictions, title transfer, receivership, guilt, or unreviewable power.

### 3.8 Just transition before withdrawal

Chemical restrictions, permit changes, subsidy reforms, land-use changes, certification consequences, and industry transition require alternatives, material support, sequencing, worker participation, and remedy.

### 3.9 Subsidiarity without automatic BAZ sovereignty

Bioregional governance can align institutions with ecological systems, but a BAZ label does not itself create land, tax, permit, court, emergency, or enforcement jurisdiction.

### 3.10 Every authority ends

Every plan, permit, grant, tax, restriction, inspection, investigation, mission, guardianship, stewardship mandate, and delegated power has review, expiry, termination, succession, data disposition, correction, and remedy.

## 4. Governance architecture

### 4.1 Global Soil Knowledge and Monitoring Commons

The source Global Soil Health Monitoring Service is retained as a decentralized **knowledge and method commons**, not a regulator or title registry. It may maintain open methods, support community and low-tech monitoring, publish versioned soil assessments, coordinate laboratories, and preserve correction histories under EIOI v2.7 and the Aurora Accord.

It cannot recognize soil rights, set binding thresholds, alter land-use permissions, impose certification, distribute money, identify legal guilt, declare emergencies, or activate enforcement. Its public dashboard must display method versions, uncertainty, missing evidence, dissent, community validation, protected-data omissions, and a prominent non-authority warning.

### 4.2 Bioregional Land and Food Councils

Councils are retained as locally grounded democratic institutions for deliberation, planning, knowledge exchange, conflict transformation, programme support, and—where expressly enacted—bounded administration. Their charters must identify each activated power and keep the following functions structurally separate:

- public participation and land-use planning;
- Indigenous-nation liaison and protected-knowledge custody;
- technical soil assessment;
- regulatory standard, permit, cap, tax, or sale-review administration;
- grant, subsidy, insurance, AUBI-verification, and procurement functions;
- guardianship and stewardship representation;
- inspection and investigation;
- registry administration;
- mediation, adjudication, and appeal;
- emergency command and coercive execution.

Indigenous leadership and meaningful youth participation strengthen legitimacy but do not allow the Council to speak for an affected nation, impose a universal developmental model, or acquire unspecified jurisdiction.

### 4.3 Land, rematriation, and justice bodies

The source Land Rematriation and Justice Tribunal is not created as a universal court by this framework. A jurisdiction may constitute a specialized chamber, but it must be independent of Councils, guardians, funds, registries, monitors, and parties. Claims, title, trust constitution, rematriation, compensation, tenants, workers, creditors, registry correction, receivership, succession, and dissolution conform to Hearthstone v2.0 and CERGTA.

Traditional justice and healing-centred processes may be used with the authority and consent of the affected people. They cannot be appropriated as compulsory procedure or used to bypass notice, evidence, counsel, independent review, appeal, or non-derogable rights.

### 4.4 Global Regenerative Agriculture Fund

The Fund is retained as a separately chartered financial institution or programme. Its governance, investment, grant approval, procurement, verification, audit, complaint, and recovery functions must be separated. It may support small-scale farmers, Indigenous stewards, workers, cooperatives, soil repair, insurance, research, and transition costs. It cannot make a soil standard legally binding, decide title, condition essential services on data surrender, or treat an ecological score as automatic eligibility, clawback, or fraud.

The source figures—such as a fifteen-percent market premium for three years—remain illustrative policy options, not universal entitlements or rates.

### 4.5 Knowledge Commons and Open-Source Microbiome Bank

The Knowledge Commons may publish non-sensitive methods and support voluntary sharing. The Microbiome Bank is a network of local custodians, not a global owner of microbial cultures or Indigenous knowledge. Every contribution must record provenance, lawful collection, affected-nation and provider authority, access, biosafety, permitted uses, benefit sharing, derivative research, patent and publication conditions, return or destruction, and withdrawal where feasible.

“Open source” applies only to material whose lawful custodians chose an open licence. It cannot be inferred from deposit, public funding, scientific interest, emergency need, or the framework’s commons philosophy.

### 4.6 Soil Emergency Assistance Missions

The source rapid-response mechanism is reconstituted as optional civilian technical missions under CDEE/ECRC. Missions may assess damage, support decontamination, preserve seed and microbial cultures, stabilize food production, coordinate laboratories and logistics, and assist recovery. They cannot allocate permanent resettlement land, alter title, impose land-use restrictions, conduct legal investigations, establish checkpoints, gather intelligence, search, seize, detain, or use force.

## 5. Affected Indigenous nations, customary law, and land relation

The affected Indigenous nation or nations—not a global council, Earth Council, BAZ, outside elder, scientist, spiritual representative, or framework-appointed guardian—determine their lawful representatives, territorial and relational authority, FPIC requirements, protected knowledge, sacred and cultural meaning, sample governance, internal plurality, dispute route, and whether to engage.

A Council’s Indigenous composition governs the Council’s own decisions only. It cannot provide consent, define another nation’s territory, waive restitution, disclose knowledge, choose a trust, or accept compensation for the affected nation.

Refusal or withdrawal cannot cause loss of environmental protection, food and water support, legal aid, grant eligibility unrelated to the refused activity, standing, or remedy. Missing documentary title cannot erase customary law, oral history, occupation, use, kinship, burial, sacred, or ecological evidence.

## 6. Soil as living commons and ecological rights

The framework supports legal recognition of soil systems, landscapes, microbial communities, and related ecological entities where competent law provides. The route is:

1. candidate and boundary intake;
2. observation, protected knowledge, affected-interest mapping, and uncertainty;
3. rights petition and standing;
4. advisory recommendation and dissent;
5. lawful recognition by a competent authority;
6. rights catalogue, priority, conflicts, and non-derogation;
7. separate guardian nomination and appointment;
8. separate land, title, permit, remediation, and enforcement decisions.

Rights recognition does not itself transfer surface or subsoil title, invalidate leases, terminate farming, establish public access, assign carbon rights, appoint a guardian, or create a universal veto. Ecological rights and human, Indigenous, housing, food, water, disability, labour, and due-process rights must be made explicit rather than compressed into one aggregate “health” objective.

## 7. Land claims, title, rematriation, and the Hearthstone boundary

Soil v3.2 may identify ecological need, document evidence, support claimants, and prepare records. It does not itself decide title, rematriation, conveyance, trust constitution, compensation, debt, tenancy, registry priority, receivership, succession, or dissolution. Those matters route through Hearthstone v2.0 and applicable law.

Before any land transition, the record must identify affected Indigenous nations, current and historical titleholders, customary users, occupants, tenants, farmers, workers, creditors, public services, food and water dependencies, neighbouring interests, ecological rights, and competing reparative claims.

Rematriation means a return into a legal and relational form chosen by the affected people. It is not automatic transfer to a BAZ, Council, Community Land Trust, guardian, or framework-created institution. Soil restoration need may support urgency or interim non-prejudice; it does not eliminate claimants, compensation, housing, livelihoods, evidence, appeal, or independent adjudication.

## 8. Land-use plans and regenerative standards

Councils may co-produce land-use plans and Minimum Regenerative Standards using EIOI-compatible evidence, affected-nation law, local knowledge, food and housing needs, climate risks, watershed effects, biodiversity, animal welfare, workers, and alternatives.

Every plan and standard must identify whether it is proposed, advisory, voluntarily adopted, contractual, locally enacted, nationally enacted, treaty-active, suspended, expired, or terminated. A standard does not become binding because it is scientifically desirable, approved by a Council, included in a dashboard, endorsed by a supermajority, or scheduled for a future phase.

The source Bronze, Silver, and Gold thresholds—such as 25, 40, or 60 percent soil cover—remain illustrative certification parameters until a versioned method and competent adoption decision specify their meaning, regional adaptation, uncertainty, inspection, correction, and legal effect.

## 9. Regenerative agriculture mandates and chemical restrictions

Regenerative practices may include soil cover, reduced disturbance, living roots, crop and habitat diversity, context-sensitive livestock integration, nutrient cycling, reduced synthetic inputs, erosion control, water retention, fungal and microbial restoration, and farmer-led experimentation. No single practice list is universally optimal across arid, tropical, temperate, Arctic, urban, contaminated, or conflict-affected contexts.

A binding mandate or restriction requires a competent authority, defined activity, evidence and uncertainty, affected farmers and workers, customary and Indigenous rights, food and water effects, alternatives, technical and financial support, transition period, exceptions, inspection rules, review, expiry, appeal, and remedy.

A precautionary chemical phase-out may begin with monitoring, labelling, procurement change, subsidy reform, or time-limited restrictions. It cannot create predetermined fines, trade sanctions, criminal liability, or land forfeiture without separate law and adjudication.

## 10. Land-value tax, anti-speculation, and sale review

Land-value taxation and anti-speculation measures are policy options for jurisdictions, not powers created by this framework or by “Treaty trade leverage.” A valid tax identifies its legal source, valuation base, liable person, exemptions, agricultural and housing effects, Indigenous and customary protections, hardship relief, revenue use, notice, appeal, review, and expiry.

A community right of first refusal requires lawful notice, eligible holders, valuation, financing, timelines, anti-circumvention, tenant and worker protections, completion, release, and review. The framework retires an unqualified **Community Veto on Land Sales**. Community and youth objections may require explanation, reconsideration, public reasons, or a lawful temporary pause; a binding restriction requires separately enacted authority, scope, duration, compensation where due, independent review, and remedy.

## 11. Stewardship leases, community land trusts, and food commons

A Generational Stewardship Lease is a lawful tenure instrument, not a score-controlled privilege. It must specify the legal interest, duration, rent or contribution, regenerative duties, housing and livelihood rights, investment and improvements, financing, insurance, succession, transfer, default, cure, termination, compensation, dispute route, and final land disposition.

Community Land Trusts and Food Commons may support housing, agriculture, restoration, rewilding, sacred and cultural uses, public space, and microbial stewardship. Their title and trust constitution conform to Hearthstone v2.0. Designation as an ecopsychological healing site does not reduce tenant rights, create compulsory therapeutic participation, or automatically generate Hearts.

## 12. Stewardship performance, correction, and no-fault harm

Soil Health Scores, BHI modules, LMCI, Love Ledger, certification tiers, satellite data, and microbial indicators may support review. They cannot automatically terminate a lease, revoke a steward, replace a Council, freeze grants, demand repayment, transfer title, or activate receivership.

Performance assessment must distinguish management from wildfire, drought, flood, war, upstream pollution, inherited contamination, climate change, invasive species, market shocks, inadequate finance, contradictory public policy, and measurement error. The normal sequence is evidence, response, independent verification, no-fault analysis, capacity support, correction plan, review, and only then a separately lawful suspension, succession, or termination decision.

## 13. Finance, AUBI, certification, and product passports

Financial and evidentiary routes remain separate:

- a soil activity record verifies what occurred;
- an ecological record assesses contribution and uncertainty;
- a grant, AUBI, subsidy, insurance, procurement, premium, or credit authority independently decides eligibility and amount;
- an audit may correct records or seek lawful recovery;
- suspected fraud requires a separate investigation and adjudication route.

Certification and Digital Product Passports communicate bounded claims. They are not permits, market exclusions, trade sanctions, or proof of liability. Errors require correction propagation, interim relief, reassessment of dependent decisions, and compensation where applicable.

## 14. Soil carbon rights and registry

The source Soil Carbon Rights Registry is divided into method, benefit or ownership, credit issuance, transfer, retirement, reversal, and registry records. A carbon methodology must address baseline, additionality, permanence, leakage, soil depth, sampling, microbial contribution, uncertainty, climate reversals, double counting, and ecological side effects.

A credit or carbon-benefit claim cannot be inferred from land title, tenancy, guardianship, stewardship, data provision, or ecological rights. Affected Indigenous nations and communities retain their lawful rights and benefit-sharing authority. Credits cannot extinguish restoration duties or authorize continued harm elsewhere.

The registry records legally effective facts but does not create title, carbon ownership, ecological rights, guardianship, stewardship, permits, sacred status, or tax obligations. Blockchain is optional and cannot override correction, privacy, protected knowledge, lawful deletion, or court orders.

## 15. Ecological repair, contamination, and liability

The Right to Ecological Repair supports access to restoration knowledge, native seeds, lawful microbial resources, remediation, legal aid, compensation, and corrective action. It does not create an unbounded right to enter land, take samples, compel disclosure, distribute protected cultures, or impose liability without process.

Monitoring, inspection, investigation, evidence custody, scientific attribution, liability, judgment, remediation, and physical execution are separate. A sample or model may support an investigation; it is not proof of guilt. Legacy, cumulative, transboundary, public, and current sources must be distinguished, and exculpatory evidence and dissent preserved.

## 16. Microbial commons, seeds, and anti-patent protection

v3.2 preserves the normative claim that naturally occurring microbial and fungal life should not be enclosed in ways that dispossess communities or impede ecological repair. The framework itself cannot make a patent universally invalid. Competent law may establish exclusions, disclosure duties, prior-art systems, defensive publication, research exceptions, compulsory licences, benefit-sharing, or invalidation routes.

No “anti-patent” mechanism may force disclosure of sacred or protected knowledge. Deposit in a seed or microbiome bank does not waive customary law, confidentiality, attribution, benefit sharing, or the right to restrict derivative use. Biosafety, ecological release, quarantine, transport, and cross-border transfer remain separately governed.

## 17. Future generations and youth participation

Youth delegations and future-generation review retain structural weight through long-horizon analysis, option-preservation review, published dissent, mandatory explanation, reconsideration, and independent review. They do not claim to sovereignly represent all future persons.

A binding youth veto exists only if a competent constituting instrument defines its scope, threshold, procedure, conflicts, accessibility, review, override, expiry, and remedy. The source “supermajority veto” therefore becomes a model option rather than a self-executing power.

## 18. Mental health, culture, and spiritual relationship

Ecopsychological healing, cultural revival, ceremony, language, and spiritual relation can be important parts of land repair. Participation is voluntary and culturally governed. LMCI, spiritual-health measures, ceremony participation, or recognition by cultural guardians cannot affect title, permits, taxes, certification, AUBI, housing, legal credibility, or access to essential services.

The Moral Operating System remains an advisory ethical resource. Its developmental, philosophical, or spiritual categories cannot create land authority or classify people for consequential decisions.

## 19. Emergency soil and food-system protection

Acute contamination, erosion, desertification, salinization, wildfire, flood, crop failure, microbial depletion, or infrastructure collapse may justify an urgent-protection request. The request must identify the competent authority, exact act, affected land and people, alternatives, essential food, water, housing, health, worker, tenant, and Indigenous safeguards, duration, review, expiry, return, compensation, and remedy.

Permanent resettlement, land allocation, title change, eviction, receivership, closure, seizure, or force cannot be inferred from the emergency. Migration, housing, Water v5.2, CDEE/ECRC, Hearthstone, CERGTA, and SCPA interfaces remain distinct.

## 20. Mediation, adjudication, and enforcement

Values-Based Conflict Transformation may support voluntary dialogue over competing land uses. It cannot compel reconciliation, extract evidence, decide title, impose a permit, authenticate sacredness, or become a hidden tribunal. Failure or impasse does not create enforcement authority.

A competent court or tribunal independently determines jurisdiction, applicable law, evidence, interim relief, judgment, restoration, compensation, penalty, appeal, and enforcement. Soil institutions may prepare a bounded referral; they cannot predetermine guilt or execute judgment.

Search, seizure, eviction, restraint, custody, force, cyber action, border measures, asset freezing, or analogous execution requires CERGTA-48 and separately valid SCPA authority. A soil claim may justify a hearing. It does not authorize a raid.

## 21. Data, AI, dashboards, and accessibility

AI, satellites, sensors, precision agriculture, laboratory automation, digital twins, blockchain, and scenario tools may assist observation, accessibility, and administration. They cannot automatically define legal boundaries, recognize rights, assign causation, set a tax, issue a permit, block a sale, revoke tenure, classify fraud, declare an emergency, or select an enforcement target.

Every consequential record must support low-tech and offline participation, accessible formats, language access, correction, human review, protected-data layers, and an explanation that can be understood by affected people. Digital absence is not evidence of non-compliance or lack of rights.

## 22. Adoption and implementation pathway

### 22.1 Phase 0 — Authority and relationship mapping

Before pilots, identify affected Indigenous nations, applicable law, land and resource jurisdictions, existing Councils and agencies, title and tenure systems, food and housing dependencies, soil baselines, data custodians, emergency authorities, courts, funds, and withheld powers.

### 22.2 Phase 1 — Knowledge, participation, and voluntary projects

Establish accessible monitoring, protected-knowledge protocols, farmer and worker support, voluntary restoration, seed and microbial safeguards, conflict transformation, and independent correction. No pilot creates regulatory power.

### 22.3 Phase 2 — Separately enacted standards and programmes

Jurisdictions may adopt standards, grants, permits, tax measures, rights-of-first-refusal, certification, or carbon programmes through exact legal instruments and CERGTA records. Open cases and transition duties are explicitly managed.

### 22.4 Phase 3 — Interoperable bioregional coordination

Councils and authorities coordinate across watersheds, food systems, biodiversity, oceans, urban areas, and national borders while retaining separate jurisdiction, clocks, data custody, and remedies.

## 23. Quick-start guide for a Bioregional Land and Food Council

1. **Map authority before ecology:** publish `SOIL-01`, including powers the Council does not possess.
2. **Identify affected nations and interests:** complete `SOIL-03` and `SOIL-04` before collecting protected knowledge or proposing land consequences.
3. **Establish lawful monitoring:** use `SOIL-05` through `SOIL-08`, with low-tech access, sample sovereignty, uncertainty, and correction.
4. **Choose a non-coercive first project:** compost, erosion repair, farmer support, urban food growing, seed preservation, or voluntary microbial restoration.
5. **Separate ecological verification from payment:** use `SOIL-31` and a competent AUBI or financial decision.
6. **Use conflict transformation without hidden adjudication:** keep mediation voluntary and preserve independent legal routes.
7. **Record all non-activated powers:** use `SOIL-48` for targets, certification tiers, pilot claims, and future ambitions.

### 26.1 Rows with a boundary beyond the default

- **`CERGTA-13`** — Only a separately competent legal authority may recognize soil or landscape rights.
- **`CERGTA-20`** — Sacred and biocultural relations are controlled by the affected nation; a Soil Council cannot authenticate sacredness for another people.
- **`CERGTA-27`** — Title, rematriation, conveyance, trust constitution, and compensation route through Hearthstone v2.0 and competent law.
- **`CERGTA-34`** — A score cannot suspend or revoke stewardship; due process, no-fault context, support, review, and succession are required.
- **`CERGTA-37`** — Standards, permits, caps, taxes, and use conditions remain distinct decisions.
- **`CERGTA-38`** — A community objection is not a self-executing veto or sale prohibition.
- **`CERGTA-43`** — Receivership is external, temporary, reviewable, automatically expiring, and tied to return and compensation.
- **`CERGTA-44`** — Certification and passport data do not themselves create market exclusion or procurement denial.
- **`CERGTA-48`** — Coercive execution requires separate SCPA authority; referral does not transfer power.
