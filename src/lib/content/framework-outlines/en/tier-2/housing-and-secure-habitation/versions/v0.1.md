# Housing & Secure Habitation Framework v0.1

## Secure occupation, tenancy, homelessness prevention, habitability, and continuity of home

**Tier:** 2 — Foundational Application Layer (provisional)  
**Status:** Working architecture outline for adversarial review — deliberately incomplete  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 2026-08-07  
**Responsible:** Global Governance Frameworks initiative  
**Constitutional interface:** current compatible Constitutional Interface Specification (CIS)  
**Lifecycle interface:** current compatible Governance Method, Experiment, Adoption, and Implementation Authority Interface Specification (GMEAIA)  
**Authority effect:** None until lawfully adopted by a competent jurisdiction, Indigenous authority, municipal or regional authority, public-housing body, cooperative, trust, contract, or other valid constituting instrument  
**Primary role:** Governance architecture for the legal and material relationship between people and the places in which they live, independent of who holds title

> **Working-draft note:** This v0.1 intentionally establishes the constitutional object, boundaries, candidate mechanisms, records, interfaces, and open questions before attempting a complete global housing code. Housing law is unusually jurisdiction-specific. The purpose of this draft is to make the missing object legible enough to audit, not to settle every substantive housing-policy choice in advance.

---

# Executive constitutional summary

The GGF already contains substantial architecture for land, property transition, municipal planning, disaster shelter, migration, ecological stewardship, taxation, and shared capital.

What it has lacked is a common architecture for a different object:

> **What rights, duties, records, and continuity protections arise because a person or household actually lives somewhere?**

Title answers who legally owns an asset. Land-use governance answers what may be built or done in a place. Disaster governance answers how emergency shelter and recovery operate. Migration governance answers reception and mobility questions. None of those, by itself, defines the ordinary relationship between a resident and a dwelling.

This Framework calls that relationship a **secure-habitation interest**.

A secure-habitation interest may arise through many legal and social forms: tenancy, ownership, cooperative membership, customary occupation, family arrangement, community tenure, public or social housing, supported housing, employer-linked accommodation, informal occupation recognized by law, or another jurisdiction-specific basis. The framework does not collapse these forms into one universal tenure regime.

Its role is to provide common governance architecture for:

1. security and continuity of habitation;
2. tenancy and other non-title residence relationships;
3. lawful termination, eviction, displacement, and relocation;
4. habitability, accessibility, privacy, and essential-service conditions;
5. homelessness prevention and pathways to stable habitation;
6. public, social, cooperative, community, and other non-market housing allocation where adopted;
7. affordability, rent-setting, deposits, fees, and hardship protections where adopted;
8. resident organization, voice, complaint, challenge, correction, and remedy;
9. housing continuity during tax collection, land transition, industrial closure, disaster recovery, migration, family crisis, disability, institutional reform, and other GGF processes;
10. the interface between housing finance and loss of home without becoming a banking or mortgage-regulation framework;
11. the interface between housing demand and housing supply without becoming the primary owner of land-use planning, construction policy, or title law.

It does **not** create a global landlord-tenant code, universal rent cap, universal eviction ban, global housing authority, global property register, compulsory public housing system, global zoning code, mortgage regime, or power to seize or transfer dwellings.

Four distinctions anchor the framework:

> **Title is not habitation.** Ownership of an asset and the right or interest to live in it are related but distinct legal objects.

> **Shelter is not yet secure habitation.** Emergency physical protection may save a life without creating a stable home.

> **A housing indicator is not an eviction, allocation, rent, or eligibility decision.** Observation may trigger review; consequential acts require constituted authority and process.

> **Continuity of home is a protected interest, not an immunity from all lawful change.** A tenancy, occupancy, building, or settlement may sometimes lawfully end, but the route must identify authority, grounds, notice, challenge, proportionality, vulnerable persons, continuity consequences, and remedy.

# 1. Purpose, scope, and boundaries

## 1.1 Purpose

The purpose of the Housing & Secure Habitation Framework is to make housing security governable as a distinct constitutional and administrative object rather than a vague protected interest invoked by other frameworks.

It provides a common architecture for jurisdictions that wish to:

- protect people from arbitrary or procedurally defective loss of home;
- define minimum conditions for safe and dignified habitation;
- govern tenancy and comparable residence relationships;
- prevent and resolve homelessness;
- govern public or social housing transparently where such systems exist;
- make housing-affordability interventions reviewable and corrigible;
- protect residents during property, economic, ecological, disaster, and institutional transitions;
- distinguish housing policy from land title, financial regulation, and emergency shelter;
- support local experimentation without turning model policy into self-executing law.

## 1.2 Why housing and secure habitation belong in one framework

The public-policy word **housing** refers to dwellings, supply, affordability, finance, construction, allocation, and institutions.

The constitutional object **secure habitation** refers to the relationship between people and the places in which they live.

They belong in one framework because housing policy is dangerous if it can produce units while ignoring residents, and habitation rights are ineffective if they ignore the material availability, adequacy, and affordability of dwellings.

The seam remains explicit. A future revision may separate a housing-systems framework from a secure-habitation protocol if the two develop substantially different authority chains.

## 1.3 Primary object: the secure-habitation interest

A **secure-habitation interest** is a legally or socially cognizable claim, permission, entitlement, or protected relationship connecting a person or household to a place used as a home.

It is deliberately broader than tenancy and narrower than title.

Its exact legal force depends on the constituting jurisdiction and may include:

- ownership occupation;
- leasehold or tenancy;
- cooperative membership;
- social/public housing entitlement or licence;
- customary or community-recognized occupation;
- Indigenous law and residence relations;
- family or household arrangements recognized by law;
- supported or institutional accommodation;
- employer-linked or service-linked housing;
- temporary or transitional habitation;
- informal occupation to which law attaches procedural, humanitarian, or regularization protections.

Recognition of a secure-habitation interest does not automatically create title, perpetual possession, a transferable asset, a rent subsidy, compensation, or immunity from lawful termination.

## 1.4 Matters outside direct scope

This framework does not itself determine:

- land title, rematriation, conveyance, stewardship trust creation, or property conversion;
- zoning, comprehensive land-use planning, density, transport-oriented development, or municipal growth boundaries;
- general building and construction regulation beyond habitation-related minimum outcomes and interfaces;
- taxation, land-value taxation, tax credits, or fiscal collection;
- mortgage underwriting, bank regulation, monetary policy, credit creation, or lender solvency;
- general competition law or real-estate market-concentration thresholds;
- emergency command, evacuation authority, disaster shelters, or humanitarian camp governance;
- migration status, asylum status, citizenship, or border admission;
- family-law custody, guardianship, or intimate-partner adjudication;
- Indigenous title, internal Indigenous law, or community authority;
- criminal law merely because conduct occurs in or around housing;
- compulsory acquisition or expropriation without its own lawful authority;
- universal rent levels, ownership models, unit quotas, or subsidy formulas by publication alone.

## 1.5 No residual housing jurisdiction

A housing need does not become a power of this Framework merely because:

- homelessness is severe;
- rents are high;
- housing supply is inadequate;
- a landlord behaves unfairly;
- a tenant is vulnerable;
- a building is unsafe;
- a community opposes redevelopment;
- another GGF transition threatens housing continuity;
- a housing metric crosses a threshold.

Every consequential act must identify its competent authority, legal or contractual source, jurisdiction, affected persons, review route, duration where applicable, and remedy.

# 2. Core concepts and distinctions

## 2.1 Shelter

**Shelter** is immediate physical protection from exposure, danger, or displacement.

Emergency shelter may be temporary, collective, mobile, improvised, or crisis-governed. Shelter is essential but does not by itself establish an ordinary tenancy, permanent allocation, title, or secure-habitation interest beyond the protections created by applicable law.

## 2.2 Habitation

**Habitation** is occupation of a place as ordinary living space with the material conditions necessary for residence.

A jurisdiction should define minimum habitation conditions appropriate to climate, culture, building type, household structure, disability access, and local infrastructure.

## 2.3 Home

For this framework, **home** is habitation plus a sufficiently durable personal, household, familial, customary, cooperative, community, or legal relationship to a place that loss of that place engages continuity and procedural interests.

This is a governance definition, not a metaphysical claim that the meaning of home can be fully reduced to law.

## 2.4 Resident

A **resident** is a person who in fact lives in a place with more than merely transient presence, subject to jurisdiction-specific rules.

Resident status is not automatically equivalent to citizenship, domicile, tenancy, title, tax residence, household membership, immigration status, or electoral residence.

## 2.5 Occupant and habitation claimant

An **occupant** is a person physically residing in a place.

A **habitation claimant** is a person asserting a secure-habitation interest, procedural protection, allocation right, continuity claim, or remedy concerning a place.

The framework uses these terms because many globally important housing relationships do not fit the conventional landlord-tenant model.

## 2.6 Owner, landlord, provider, operator, and custodian

These roles must not be collapsed.

- an **owner** holds title or another proprietary interest;
- a **landlord** grants or administers a tenancy or comparable occupancy relationship;
- a **housing provider** supplies or allocates accommodation under a public, social, cooperative, charitable, community, private, or mixed model;
- an **operator** manages a building or housing service;
- a **custodian/trustee** may hold assets for beneficiaries without being the ordinary housing decision-maker.

One actor may perform several roles only where conflicts, records, review, and accountability are explicitly managed.

## 2.7 Housing security

**Housing security** means that a person's ability to remain housed is not subject to arbitrary, opaque, unreviewable, or materially impossible-to-challenge loss.

It does not mean every residence relationship is permanent.

# 3. Constitutional principles

## 3.1 Secure habitation as a protected continuity interest

Other GGF processes that can foreseeably affect a person's home shall identify housing consequences before acting and shall not treat displacement as an invisible externality.

Where lawful action nevertheless requires relocation, termination, redevelopment, closure, remediation, or recovery, the responsible process shall identify the affected habitation interests and the applicable continuity, notice, support, compensation, relocation, or remedy route.

## 3.2 No eviction by indicator

Rent arrears data, housing-risk scores, building-condition alerts, benefit status, migration data, employment status, household composition, predictive models, or automated fraud indicators may trigger review where lawfully permitted.

They do not by themselves terminate a habitation interest, authorize entry, order eviction, cancel an allocation, set rent, establish fraud, or remove essential services.

## 3.3 No deprivation through administrative error

A person should not lose essential habitation solely because of an unresolved identity collision, registry error, payment-system failure, incorrect benefit record, inaccessible notice, or other correctable administrative defect.

Applicable law may provide temporary protection, interim relief, suspension, emergency accommodation, or expedited review while the defect is resolved.

## 3.4 Functional separation

Where possible, the institution that owns or operates housing should not be the sole institution that:

- sets binding habitability standards;
- investigates its own alleged violations;
- adjudicates eviction or serious tenancy disputes;
- determines appeals from its own allocation decisions;
- controls the only source of resident legal support.

Where small jurisdictions combine functions, independent review and conflict controls are required.

## 3.5 Rights of residents do not erase rights of others

Secure-habitation protection must coexist with:

- rights of other residents;
- owners' lawful interests;
- worker safety;
- neighbourhood and ecological interests;
- Indigenous authority;
- public-health and building-safety duties;
- creditors and public finance where applicable;
- legitimate redevelopment, remediation, and infrastructure needs.

The framework therefore emphasizes constituted balancing, not absolute possession.

## 3.6 Children, elders, disability, illness, and dependency

Housing decisions shall identify foreseeable disproportionate effects on people whose health, safety, communication, mobility, care relationships, education, or survival depend strongly on continuity of place.

This does not create a universal veto. It requires that those consequences be visible and addressed through the applicable rights, accessibility, welfare, family, health, disability, or social-protection framework.

## 3.7 Indigenous and customary habitation

The framework does not convert Indigenous, customary, collective, sacred, or kinship-based residence relationships into ordinary tenancy.

Affected Indigenous nations and communities determine the applicable law, representatives, consent requirements, territorial relationships, protected knowledge, and non-engagement rights. Global or municipal housing bodies cannot substitute for that authority.

## 3.8 Non-discrimination and practical accessibility

Housing systems shall comply with applicable equality, disability, family, gender, age, migration, and other rights law.

Formal equality is insufficient where the process itself is inaccessible. Notice, applications, hearings, allocation, inspection, complaint, and remedy should support disability access, language access, low-bandwidth and offline routes, representation, and reasonable assistance.

## 3.9 No forced digital dependence

Access to essential housing protection, homelessness assistance, complaint, or review should not depend solely on possession of a smartphone, biometric credential, digital wallet, or high-bandwidth connection.

## 3.10 Material exit and resident voice

A housing system should assess whether residents possess meaningful alternatives or whether dependence on a single employer, landlord, platform, institution, family gatekeeper, or public provider makes nominal consent ineffective.

Resident organization, tenant associations, cooperative governance, ombuds routes, and independent advocacy may be appropriate safeguards where adopted.

The framework should be able to represent at least the following without pretending they are legally identical:

1. owner-occupied homes;
2. private rental tenancies;
3. public or social rental housing;
4. housing cooperatives;
5. Community Land Trust housing;
6. stewardship-trust housing;
7. community or customary tenure;
8. Indigenous residence relationships governed under Indigenous law;
9. informal settlements and informal rental arrangements;
10. family or multigenerational occupation;
11. employer-provided or tied housing;
12. student and institutional accommodation;
13. disability-support and supported living;
14. shelters and transitional housing;
15. migrant, refugee, or reception accommodation;
16. mobile, seasonal, nomadic, or semi-nomadic habitation where applicable;
17. collective residences and intentional communities;
18. mortgage-financed owner occupation.

A later draft should test whether any important habitation form is structurally excluded by this taxonomy.

# 5. Institutional architecture

The Framework creates no mandatory global housing body.

A jurisdiction implementing it should identify which existing or newly constituted institutions perform the following functions.

## 5.1 Housing policy and planning authority

May assess housing need, affordability, supply, public investment, housing mix, and distributional outcomes. It does not acquire zoning, title, tax, credit, or compulsory-acquisition power merely because it plans housing.

## 5.2 Housing provider or landlord

Provides, leases, manages, or allocates dwellings under applicable public, private, cooperative, community, or mixed arrangements.

## 5.3 Habitation standards authority

Sets or enforces legally adopted minimum standards for safety, sanitation, accessibility, essential services, and habitability. Entry and inspection require separate lawful authority.

## 5.4 Allocation authority

Where public, social, community, or scarce subsidized housing is allocated administratively, the allocation authority shall publish eligibility, priority, evidence, waiting-list or queue rules, accessibility accommodations, review, correction, and anti-discrimination safeguards.

## 5.5 Homelessness and housing-continuity service

Coordinates prevention, rapid accommodation, stabilization, benefits/service access, and transition to durable habitation. It should not need to manufacture a punitive legal status before assistance becomes available.

## 5.6 Independent review, tribunal, court, ombuds, or equivalent

Serious eviction, allocation, discrimination, habitability, deposit, rent, or public-provider disputes should have access to an independent route appropriate to the jurisdiction.

The framework does not create a universal housing court.

## 5.7 Resident representation and advocacy

Jurisdictions should specify whether and how tenant unions, resident councils, legal-aid bodies, disability advocates, community organizations, or other representatives participate in housing governance and disputes.

# 6. Minimum secure-habitation floor

This section establishes a design question, not yet a universal numeric standard.

A jurisdiction adopting the Framework should define what conditions are necessary for a place to count as minimally adequate habitation in its context.

The schedule should consider:

- structural safety;
- fire and environmental safety;
- potable water and sanitation;
- protection from weather and climate exposure;
- ventilation and indoor environmental quality;
- essential energy where needed for survival and ordinary habitation;
- reasonable privacy and personal security;
- accessibility and reasonable accommodation;
- space and occupancy standards appropriate to household structure;
- protection from severe contamination, infestation, or hazardous materials;
- connection to essential services where locally necessary;
- culturally appropriate conditions where relevant;
- repair obligations and response times;
- emergency alternatives when a dwelling becomes temporarily uninhabitable.

A failure of one condition does not automatically determine the legal remedy. Depending on severity and law, the response may involve repair, rent adjustment, temporary relocation, enforcement, compensation, closure, public assistance, or another route.

# 7. Tenancy and non-title residence relationships

## 7.1 Constituting relationship

A tenancy or comparable residence relationship should be able to identify:

- parties and authorized representatives;
- dwelling or habitation unit;
- legal source and type of relationship;
- duration and renewal rules;
- rent, contribution, fee, or non-monetary obligations where applicable;
- deposits or security arrangements;
- utilities and service responsibilities;
- repair and maintenance duties;
- privacy, entry, and inspection rules;
- occupancy and household rules;
- subletting or transfer rules where relevant;
- termination grounds and process;
- complaint, correction, and dispute route.

Oral, customary, informal, or low-documentation relationships may require alternative evidence rather than being treated as nonexistent by default.

## 7.2 Rent, fees, and deposits

This framework does not prescribe a universal rent-control regime.

Jurisdictions may choose among policy families including:

- market rent with procedural safeguards;
- rent stabilization;
- rent caps or reference rents;
- cost-linked or income-linked social rents;
- rent-to-income assistance;
- deposit limits or public deposit guarantees;
- anti-gouging rules during scarcity or crisis;
- vacancy or speculation measures through other frameworks;
- negotiated or cooperative rent-setting.

Whichever model is adopted should state its objective, authority, evidence, formula or decision process, affected parties, review, exceptions, distributional effects, and revision path.

## 7.3 Entry, privacy, and inspection

Ownership or landlord status does not create unlimited entry authority.

Housing inspection, emergency entry, landlord access, repairs, safety checks, and law-enforcement access remain distinct powers with distinct legal bases.

## 7.4 Retaliation and resident organization

Where law recognizes resident association, complaint, unionization, collective bargaining, code enforcement, or legal challenge, a landlord or provider should not be able to defeat those rights through retaliatory termination or selective service withdrawal.

The exact protected activities and remedies remain jurisdiction-specific.

# 8. Termination, eviction, displacement, and relocation

## 8.1 Eviction as a consequential act

An eviction or equivalent compulsory loss of habitation is a consequential act requiring a defined legal route.

A lawful route should identify:

- the habitation interest affected;
- the alleged legal ground;
- competent decision-maker;
- evidence and contested facts;
- notice and accessibility;
- time to respond;
- representation or counsel where applicable;
- vulnerability and continuity impacts;
- interim relief;
- adjudication or independent review;
- execution authority;
- treatment of belongings, dependants, animals, medicines, assistive devices, and essential records;
- closure and remedy.

## 8.2 Grounds do not execute themselves

Non-payment, lease expiry, owner need, redevelopment, nuisance, safety risk, illegal activity, overcrowding, abandonment, or another legally recognized ground may justify opening a process.

The ground itself does not authorize self-help eviction, lockout, utility deprivation, seizure of belongings, harassment, or physical removal unless applicable law separately permits the exact act.

## 8.3 Abandonment

Absence from a dwelling should not automatically establish abandonment where displacement, hospitalization, disaster, detention, care obligations, migration, seasonal mobility, conflict, or other circumstances may explain absence.

A jurisdiction should define evidence and correction rules for abandonment determinations.

## 8.4 Redevelopment and public-purpose displacement

Where housing is lost through redevelopment, infrastructure, ecological restoration, land return, hazard mitigation, public works, or other public-purpose action, the constituting process should identify:

- who is displaced;
- title and non-title habitation interests;
- lawful authority;
- consultation and applicable consent;
- relocation options;
- continuity of school, care, work, disability supports, and community ties;
- compensation where legally due;
- right of return or replacement rights where adopted;
- timing and appeal;
- unresolved claims after physical relocation.

## 8.5 No disguised eviction through essential-service withdrawal

Water, sanitation, energy, access systems, repairs, security, or other essential habitation services should not be deliberately withdrawn as a substitute for lawful termination where the jurisdiction would otherwise require an eviction or review process.

# 9. Affordability and rent-burden governance

Housing affordability is a systems outcome, not a single universal ratio.

A jurisdiction may monitor:

- rent-to-income or housing-cost burden;
- residual income after housing costs;
- overcrowding;
- involuntary displacement;
- homelessness entries and exits;
- waiting-list duration;
- vacancy and utilization;
- construction and replacement rates;
- land and dwelling prices;
- mortgage distress;
- household energy and utility burden;
- accessibility-adjusted housing availability;
- geographic mismatch between housing, work, care, education, and mobility.

No metric automatically creates a rent cap, subsidy, eviction moratorium, building obligation, or allocation priority.

Possible policy responses belong to different constitutional owners and may include housing assistance, public/social housing, rent regulation, land policy, taxation, construction policy, transport planning, wage policy, social protection, or credit regulation.

The Framework's role is to make those handoffs explicit.

# 10. Homelessness prevention and durable habitation

## 10.1 Homelessness as a housing-system state

Homelessness should be represented as a condition requiring housing and support response, not merely as individual non-compliance or absence from a registry.

The framework should distinguish at minimum:

- unsheltered homelessness;
- emergency shelter;
- temporary or transitional accommodation;
- insecure couch-surfing or hidden homelessness where relevant;
- imminent housing loss;
- institutional discharge without habitation;
- repeated cycling through unstable accommodation;
- durable habitation.

## 10.2 Prevention

Prevention mechanisms may include:

- rent or arrears mediation;
- emergency financial support;
- legal assistance;
- landlord-provider negotiation;
- domestic-violence relocation support;
- discharge planning from hospitals, prisons, care, military, or institutions;
- employment-transition housing continuity;
- family stabilization;
- mortgage-distress intervention;
- rapid repair or temporary relocation after habitability failure.

The Framework does not mandate one prevention model.

## 10.3 Housing First and other service models

Housing First, supported housing, transitional housing, shelter-led systems, cooperative/community placement, and other models may be tested and adopted according to local evidence and population needs.

The Framework should not constitutionalize one service model prematurely. It should require that programs state their target population, conditions, evidence, resident rights, exit route, failure mode, and long-term housing outcome.

## 10.4 No assistance-by-surveillance default

People should not have to surrender unrelated health, migration, policing, employment, family, biometric, or behavioural data merely to access basic homelessness assistance unless separately lawful and necessary for the stated purpose.

# 11. Public, social, cooperative, and community housing

Where non-market or publicly supported housing exists, its governance should identify:

- legal owner and custodian;
- provider/operator;
- public or community purpose;
- capitalization and ongoing funding;
- eligibility and allocation rules;
- rent or contribution method;
- accessibility obligations;
- resident governance or representation;
- maintenance and reserve obligations;
- procurement and conflicts;
- audit and beneficial ownership of contractors;
- anti-corruption safeguards;
- transfer, redevelopment, sale, or conversion rules;
- continuity of resident rights during provider failure;
- complaint, appeal, and correction;
- dissolution and successor custody.

Community Land Trusts and stewardship trusts remain governed by their relevant land/property frameworks. This Framework governs the habitation relationship of residents within those structures.

---

# 12. Housing supply, construction, and land-use interface

Housing security cannot be produced solely through rights over scarce dwellings. Supply, location, accessibility, infrastructure, land, construction cost, and financing matter.

This Framework therefore requires a **housing-systems interface** without claiming all supply-side powers.

A housing-needs assessment may identify:

- unit shortages or surpluses;
- household formation;
- demographic change;
- accessible-housing need;
- geographic mismatch;
- vacancy and underuse;
- climate and hazard exposure;
- infrastructure capacity;
- replacement needs;
- affordability by tenure type;
- construction bottlenecks.

Those findings may be handed to Bioregional Polis, infrastructure, land, fiscal, finance, labour, materials, ecological, or other competent frameworks.

The housing framework may define adequacy and need. It does not acquire zoning, permitting, compulsory purchase, tax, or construction-command authority merely because supply is inadequate.

---

# 13. Housing finance and homeownership interface

Mortgage-financed ownership creates a dual relationship: a person may hold title while a lender holds a secured financial claim capable of threatening habitation.

This Framework does not regulate banking or mortgage markets generally.

It does govern the **habitation consequence interface** where financial distress may cause loss of home.

A foreclosure, repossession, forced sale, or equivalent process affecting a primary residence should identify:

- valid financial claim and security interest;
- borrower/resident identities;
- applicable consumer and insolvency protections;
- notice and correction;
- restructuring or hardship options where law provides them;
- independent review or adjudication;
- vulnerable residents and dependants;
- execution and possession route;
- treatment of residual debt or surplus;
- housing-continuity assistance where applicable.

A future Monetary & Credit architecture would own underwriting, leverage, lender conduct, securitization, and macroprudential questions.

---

# 14. Cross-framework housing continuity

Any GGF process that can foreseeably cause loss, degradation, or involuntary relocation of habitation should create a **Housing Continuity Handoff** rather than merely stating that housing must be protected.

Candidate triggering domains include:

- Adaptive Tax collection and enforcement;
- Sundown industry closure and worker transition;
- Aegis conversion of security-industry assets and communities;
- Hearthstone property conversion, rematriation, stewardship transition, and asset transfer;
- Bioregional Polis redevelopment and land policy;
- Soil/Living Land remediation and tenure reform;
- Disaster Risk Reduction recovery and managed retreat;
- Migration and refugee reception;
- water, sanitation, energy, or infrastructure failure;
- justice or insolvency processes affecting residence;
- Shared Capital property or fund transitions where housing assets are involved.

The handoff should state:

1. affected residents and habitation interests;
2. causal action likely to affect housing;
3. competent housing authority or service;
4. urgency;
5. continuity requirement;
6. relocation, assistance, compensation, or adjudication route if applicable;
7. unresolved duties that survive completion of the originating process.

# 15. Data, observation, and housing records

## 15.1 Purpose limitation

Housing records can reveal location, family structure, disability, income, migration status, health, intimate relationships, employment, religion, care needs, and political vulnerability.

Collection and reuse therefore require explicit purpose, minimum necessary data, access rules, correction, retention, and remedy.

## 15.2 Recognition-record firewall

Love Ledger, care, civic, cultural, ecological, or relational recognition records are not housing-risk, tenancy-reliability, fraud, allocation-priority, or credit-scoring records merely because they exist.

Where a housing decision needs an income, payment, identity, disability-accommodation, residence, or eligibility fact, the responsible system should use a purpose-appropriate record constituted for that function.

## 15.3 Automated systems

AI or automated systems may support:

- application processing;
- maintenance triage;
- affordability modelling;
- allocation support;
- fraud/anomaly review;
- vacancy analysis;
- inspection scheduling;
- homelessness-service coordination.

They cannot by themselves evict, deny essential shelter, terminate a tenancy, establish fraud, cancel a waiting-list position, set an individualized rent, or classify a person as having abandoned a home unless a competent lawfully constituted process explicitly authorizes bounded automation with correction, review, and human responsibility.

# 16. Phase 0 — map the housing constitution before redesigning it

Phase 0 is the spine of this working draft.

A jurisdiction can begin without adopting a new housing ideology or waiting for a global institution.

## 16.1 Map existing habitation relationships

Inventory:

- owner occupation;
- private rental;
- social/public housing;
- cooperatives;
- CLTs and stewardship structures;
- customary/community housing;
- informal settlements and informal rentals;
- employer-linked housing;
- institutional and supported accommodation;
- homelessness and shelter systems;
- mortgage-financed primary residences.

## 16.2 Map authority

Identify who currently has lawful authority over:

- tenancy creation and termination;
- eviction;
- rent and deposit rules;
- housing standards;
- inspection and entry;
- social-housing allocation;
- homelessness services;
- building closure;
- redevelopment and displacement;
- mortgage foreclosure;
- emergency shelter;
- land title and zoning;
- funding and subsidies;
- adjudication and appeal.

The map should expose gaps and overlaps rather than assume one housing authority owns all functions.

## 16.3 Map continuity failures

Review where people lose housing because systems fail to coordinate, including:

- benefit or payment interruption;
- unemployment or industry closure;
- disability or health crisis;
- family separation or violence;
- landlord/provider failure;
- disaster or climate relocation;
- redevelopment;
- property transition;
- tax or debt enforcement;
- institutional discharge;
- identity or registry error.

## 16.4 Define the local secure-habitation floor

Without yet adopting every possible housing reform, identify:

- minimum habitability;
- procedural protections against arbitrary loss;
- emergency accommodation route;
- correction and interim-relief route;
- accessibility requirements;
- protected continuity during other public processes.

## 16.5 Establish housing-continuity handoffs

Patch the most urgent existing GGF and public processes so that “protect housing” becomes an owned referral with a responsible authority and record.

## 16.6 Pilot substantive reforms separately

Possible pilots may include:

- eviction prevention;
- rent stabilization;
- Housing First;
- social-housing allocation reform;
- tenant legal support;
- cooperative acquisition;
- public development;
- accessible-housing expansion;
- vacancy mobilization;
- mortgage-distress mediation;
- community land/housing combinations.

Each pilot should state what evidence would count as failure.

# 19. Framework interfaces

## 19.1 Constitutional Interface Specification

CIS supplies legitimacy, jurisdiction, sovereignty, observation, data-purpose, challenge, remedy, correction, and cross-framework authority rules. Housing does not locally redefine those states.

## 19.2 GMEAIA

Housing pilots, adoptions, implementation actions, refusals, deferred actions, capacity limitations, constitutional gaps, and closure use the common lifecycle grammar rather than housing-specific substitutes.

## 19.3 Hearthstone Protocol

Hearthstone owns title conversion, stewardship transition, rematriation, trust constitution, conveyance, and property succession.

Housing owns the resident-facing habitation consequences of those processes: notice, tenancy/occupancy continuity, relocation, household claims, accessibility, belongings, service continuity, and unresolved duties.

Neither title transition nor stewardship purpose silently extinguishes a habitation interest.

## 19.4 Bioregional Polis

Bioregional Polis owns municipal land-use strategy, CLTs, LVT, anti-speculation, urban development, and related place-based policy.

Housing owns tenancy, allocation, habitability, homelessness, and resident continuity within the resulting built environment.

The two jointly meet at housing-needs assessment, affordable-housing production, accessibility, redevelopment, displacement, and CLT resident governance.

## 19.5 Soil Health / Living Land

Soil and land frameworks govern ecological restoration, rural land, customary tenure, and productive-land systems within their remit.

Housing supplies the secure-habitation interface where land action affects homes, farmworker housing, customary residence, or rural settlements.

## 19.6 Disaster Risk Reduction

DRR owns emergency shelter, evacuation interfaces, disaster command, and recovery coordination.

Housing owns the transition from emergency shelter or displacement into ordinary secure habitation and the protection of pre-existing habitation interests during recovery.

Emergency absence does not automatically prove abandonment.

## 19.7 Migration and Human Mobility

Migration owns status, reception, mobility, displacement, and migration-specific governance.

Housing supplies ordinary habitability, allocation, resident rights, privacy, and continuity standards for accommodation once those standards are legally applicable. Housing status does not decide migration status.

## 19.8 Adaptive Tax

Adaptive Tax owns fiscal liability, collection, and tax remedies.

Housing defines the receiving continuity route where tax administration could foreseeably cause loss of a primary home. A housing-vulnerability finding does not itself alter tax liability.

## 19.9 Shared Capital and Social Inheritance

Shared Capital owns pooled capital, funds, social inheritance, and beneficiary capital claims.

Housing owns resident relationships where shared-capital institutions own or finance housing. Beneficiary or investment objectives cannot silently override resident rights.

## 19.10 Financial Systems and future Monetary/Credit architecture

Financial Systems supplies applicable settlement and financial infrastructure. A future monetary/credit framework would own bank and mortgage-system architecture.

Housing owns only the home-loss and continuity interface when financial claims touch primary habitation.

## 19.11 Work in Liberation / Sundown / Aegis

Employment and transition frameworks must route foreseeable housing loss—especially tied housing, industry closure, relocation, pension/community transition, or regional economic collapse—through a housing continuity process rather than treating “housing” as an unowned checklist item.

## 19.12 Disability, health, family, justice, and social-equity frameworks

Housing imports applicable rights and support requirements. It should not attempt to adjudicate disability status, health diagnosis, family custody, discrimination law, or criminal responsibility itself.

## 19.13 Water, sanitation, energy, and infrastructure

Housing depends on essential services but does not govern their technical systems. Service frameworks should preserve minimums and recognize housing consequences; Housing governs the resident-facing habitability and continuity implications.

Each instance should be classified as one of:

1. **conformant handoff already owned elsewhere**;
2. **needs Housing interface reference**;

# 21. Falsification and failure gates

This framework should not survive merely because housing language sounds humane.

A later release should fail or substantially revise itself if evidence shows that it:

1. duplicates land/title governance rather than filling the habitation gap;
2. creates procedural rights that are unusable in practice because residents lack access, time, representation, or alternative shelter;
3. prevents legitimate repair, redevelopment, safety action, or housing supply to such an extent that overall housing insecurity worsens;
4. protects formal tenants while excluding informal, customary, family, migrant, disability-support, or other real residence relationships;
5. encourages landlords/providers to avoid formalizing housing relationships in order to escape duties;
6. creates rent or allocation rules that substantially worsen scarcity, exclusion, segregation, or informalization without compensating benefits;
7. treats homelessness as solved by temporary shelter while durable housing outcomes deteriorate;
8. centralizes housing authority beyond what local legal and institutional diversity can support;
9. produces extensive sensitive registries that become surveillance, migration, credit, policing, or political-control infrastructure;
10. turns “right to housing” rhetoric into claims no institution has capacity or legal authority to honour;
11. makes housing continuity so absolute that necessary ecological restoration, Indigenous rematriation, safety closure, or public infrastructure becomes impossible rather than lawfully balanced;
12. leaves the same cross-framework housing promises unowned after adoption.

# 23. Proposed next drafting sequence

Before expanding this v0.1 into a complete framework:

1. **Corpus boundary audit:** verify every claimed interface and identify housing mechanisms already present elsewhere.
2. **Comparative legal audit:** sample several very different housing systems to test whether the secure-habitation primitive travels across common-law tenancy, civil-law tenancy, social-housing systems, informal settlements, customary tenure, cooperative housing, and Indigenous law.
3. **Failure-case audit:** test eviction, landlord insolvency, unsafe building, rent shock, factory closure, disaster absence, rematriation, mortgage foreclosure, domestic violence, and homelessness discharge.
4. **Authority audit:** determine whether each `HAB-*` record has a lawful owner and whether any record accidentally creates power.
5. **Scope decision:** decide whether housing supply and social housing remain inside this framework or become stronger interfaces to Bioregional Polis / infrastructure.
6. **Only then:** write the fuller v0.2 constitutional architecture and schema wiring.

# 24. Working standfirst

> **Shared rules for the relationship between people and the places they live: secure habitation, tenancy, habitability, eviction, homelessness prevention, housing allocation, and continuity of home across economic, ecological, and institutional change. Title is not habitation, shelter is not yet a home, and no housing metric creates a power to evict, allocate, or exclude by itself.**

# 25. Working conclusion

The GGF has repeatedly promised that taxation, transition, disaster response, land reform, remediation, and institutional change will not casually destroy people's housing.

That promise needs an owner.

The Housing & Secure Habitation Framework begins from a narrow proposition:

> **A person can have a constitutionally significant relationship to a home without owning the land or dwelling, and governance must be able to represent that relationship explicitly.**

The framework therefore sits between property and personhood without collapsing into either. It does not decide who should own all housing, what every rent should be, or which global housing model is correct. It makes the resident–dwelling relationship visible, assigns consequential housing acts to lawful authorities, creates continuity and challenge routes, and gives the rest of the GGF somewhere real to hand off the promises it already makes.

That is enough for v0.1. The remaining design should be earned through adversarial review rather than filled in merely because the section headings exist.
