> **Working-draft note:** This v0.1.4 remains an adversarial working outline rather than a complete global housing code. It preserves the secure-habitation object while replacing the remaining single-parent dependency assumption with a multi-basis model: one habitation position may rest on several independently governed legal, contractual, customary, care, employment, family, membership, allocation, or other bases, and may be affected by non-owner gatekeepers. Housing law is unusually jurisdiction-specific. The purpose of this draft is to make those interfaces governable without prematurely settling rent, supply, finance, family-law, care-law, creditor-priority, customary-law, or tenure-policy choices.

# Executive constitutional summary

The GGF already contains substantial architecture for land, property transition, municipal planning, disaster shelter, migration, ecological stewardship, taxation, and shared capital.

What it has lacked is a common architecture for a different object:

> **What rights, duties, records, and continuity protections arise because a person or household actually lives somewhere?**

Title answers who legally owns an asset. Land-use governance answers what may be built or done in a place. Disaster governance answers how emergency shelter and recovery operate. Migration governance answers reception and mobility questions. None of those, by itself, defines the ordinary relationship between a resident and a dwelling.

This Framework calls that relationship a **secure-habitation interest**.

A secure-habitation interest may arise through many legal and social forms: tenancy, ownership, cooperative membership, customary occupation, family arrangement, community tenure, public or social housing, supported housing, employer-linked accommodation, informal occupation recognized by law, or another jurisdiction-specific basis. The framework does not collapse these forms into one universal tenure regime.

Its role is to provide common governance architecture for:

1. security and continuity of habitation;
2. tenancy and other non-title residence relationships, including access and screening;
3. lawful termination, eviction, displacement, relocation, and replacement habitation when place continuity becomes impossible;
4. habitability, accessibility, privacy, and essential-service conditions;
5. homelessness prevention and pathways to stable habitation;
6. public, social, cooperative, community, and other non-market housing allocation where adopted;
7. affordability, rent-setting, deposits, fees, and hardship protections where adopted;
8. resident organization, voice, complaint, challenge, correction, and remedy;
9. housing continuity during tax collection, land transition, industrial closure, disaster recovery, migration, family crisis, disability, institutional reform, and other GGF processes;
10. minimum protection for people who live in a home without holding the primary lease, title, or allocation instrument, without silently converting them into co-tenants or overriding safety/family-law orders;
11. separation of habitation powers from care, treatment, custody, education, disability-support, or institutional powers where one provider controls both accommodation and daily life;
12. conversion between ordinary habitation and short-term/tourism accommodation where that conversion affects existing residents;
13. slow-onset climate and environmental degradation that can make habitation progressively unsafe before a formal disaster declaration;
14. the interface between housing finance, owner/provider insolvency, creditor claims, and loss of home without becoming a banking, insolvency, or mortgage-regulation framework;
15. the interface between housing demand and housing supply without becoming the primary owner of land-use planning, construction policy, or title law;
16. derived habitation interests such as subtenancies, lodgings, licences, tied housing, and supported residence whose continuation may depend on one or more independently governed habitation bases;
17. competing simultaneous or successive claims to the same dwelling without letting Housing itself decide substantive priority;
18. continuity after a community, customary, family, care, employment, or other competent relationship lawfully terminates residence;
19. identity-light access to housing protection where national civil registration, citizenship documents, or formal identity records are absent, disputed, unsafe to disclose, or inaccessible;
20. fallback custody of unresolved housing-continuity duties where no ordinary provider accepts responsibility, without manufacturing a new housing sovereign;
21. habitation positions supported or constrained by multiple independently governed bases rather than one presumed superior relationship;
22. non-owner and non-provider gatekeepers whose independently lawful decisions can open, continue, modify, or close access to a dwelling;
23. lawful status transitions such as succession, regularization, conversion from derived to direct tenure, or movement from temporary to durable habitation;
24. the distinction between a claim to remain in a particular dwelling and a separately constituted entitlement to suitable accommodation somewhere.

It does **not** create a global landlord-tenant code, universal rent cap, universal eviction ban, global housing authority, global property register, compulsory public housing system, global zoning code, mortgage regime, or power to seize or transfer dwellings.

Four distinctions anchor the framework:

> **Title is not habitation.** Ownership of an asset and the right or interest to live in it are related but distinct legal objects.

> **Shelter is not yet secure habitation.** Emergency physical protection may save a life without creating a stable home.

> **A housing indicator is not an eviction, allocation, rent, or eligibility decision.** Observation may trigger review; consequential acts require constituted authority and process.

> **Continuity of home is a protected interest, not an immunity from all lawful change.** A tenancy, occupancy, building, or settlement may sometimes lawfully end, but the route must identify authority, grounds, notice, challenge, proportionality, vulnerable persons, continuity consequences, and remedy.

# 1. Purpose, scope, and boundaries

## 1.1 Purpose

The purpose of the Housing & Secure Habitation Framework is to make housing security governable as a distinct constitutional and administrative object rather than a vague protected interest invoked by other frameworks.

It provides a common architecture for jurisdictions that wish to:

- protect people from arbitrary or procedurally defective loss of home;
- define minimum conditions for safe and dignified habitation;
- govern tenancy and comparable residence relationships;
- prevent and resolve homelessness;
- govern public or social housing transparently where such systems exist;
- make housing-affordability interventions reviewable and corrigible;
- protect residents during property, economic, ecological, disaster, and institutional transitions;
- distinguish housing policy from land title, financial regulation, and emergency shelter;
- support local experimentation without turning model policy into self-executing law.

## 1.2 Why housing and secure habitation belong in one framework

The public-policy word **housing** refers to dwellings, supply, affordability, finance, construction, allocation, and institutions.

The constitutional object **secure habitation** refers to the relationship between people and the places in which they live.

They belong in one framework because housing policy is dangerous if it can produce units while ignoring residents, and habitation rights are ineffective if they ignore the material availability, adequacy, and affordability of dwellings.

The seam remains explicit. In this working outline it falls most visibly through **§9 (affordability and rent-burden governance), §12 (housing supply and construction interface), and §13 (housing finance and homeownership interface)**. Those sections are intentionally more provisional than the secure-habitation core because their policy instruments are highly jurisdiction-specific and often belong partly to other frameworks.

The seam is therefore governed as an interface rather than ignored: **Housing states the resident-facing adequacy, access, continuity, and distributional conditions that supply-side systems must account for; land, construction, infrastructure, fiscal, credit, tourism, and other competent authorities retain their own substantive powers.** A housing need does not let Housing seize those powers, and an external supply-side power does not erase habitation consequences.

A future revision may separate a housing-systems framework from a secure-habitation protocol if the two develop substantially different authority chains. Keeping them together through v0.1.4 is a testable design choice, not a commitment to permanent merger.

## 1.3 Primary object: the secure-habitation interest

A **secure-habitation interest** is a legally or socially cognizable claim, permission, entitlement, or protected relationship connecting a person or household to a place used as a home.

It is deliberately broader than tenancy and narrower than title.

Its exact legal force depends on the constituting jurisdiction and may include:

- ownership occupation;
- leasehold or tenancy;
- cooperative membership;
- social/public housing entitlement or licence;
- customary or community-recognized occupation;
- Indigenous law and residence relations;
- family or household arrangements recognized by law;
- supported or institutional accommodation;
- employer-linked or service-linked housing;
- temporary or transitional habitation;
- informal occupation to which law attaches procedural, humanitarian, or regularization protections.

Recognition of a secure-habitation interest does not automatically create title, perpetual possession, a transferable asset, a rent subsidy, compensation, or immunity from lawful termination.

## 1.4 Matters outside direct scope

This framework does not itself determine:

- land title, rematriation, conveyance, stewardship trust creation, or property conversion;
- zoning, comprehensive land-use planning, density, transport-oriented development, or municipal growth boundaries;
- general building and construction regulation beyond habitation-related minimum outcomes and interfaces;
- taxation, land-value taxation, tax credits, or fiscal collection;
- mortgage underwriting, bank regulation, monetary policy, credit creation, or lender solvency;
- general competition law or real-estate market-concentration thresholds;
- emergency command, evacuation authority, disaster shelters, or humanitarian camp governance;
- migration status, asylum status, citizenship, or border admission;
- family-law custody, guardianship, or intimate-partner adjudication;
- Indigenous title, internal Indigenous law, or community authority;
- criminal law merely because conduct occurs in or around housing;
- compulsory acquisition or expropriation without its own lawful authority;
- universal rent levels, ownership models, unit quotas, or subsidy formulas by publication alone;
- general care, treatment, disability-support, education, custody, detention, or institutional-discipline powers merely because accommodation is attached to those services;
- tourism licensing, platform regulation, hotel standards, or short-term-letting permission except for the resident-facing habitation consequences of conversion.

## 1.5 No residual housing jurisdiction

A housing need does not become a power of this Framework merely because:

- homelessness is severe;
- rents are high;
- housing supply is inadequate;
- a landlord behaves unfairly;
- a tenant is vulnerable;
- a building is unsafe;
- a community opposes redevelopment;
- another GGF transition threatens housing continuity;
- a housing metric crosses a threshold.

Every consequential act must identify its competent authority, legal or contractual source, jurisdiction, affected persons, review route, duration where applicable, and remedy.

## 1.6 Provisional tier and cross-framework standing

This draft remains **provisionally Tier 2**. The reason is functional: it applies constitutional rules to a concrete domain rather than supplying the constitutional operating system itself.

Tier does not determine whether another framework may ignore a housing continuity duty. Cross-framework standing comes from the constituting law, controlling interface, explicit adoption, and the `HAB-16` handoff—not from a numerical tier hierarchy. If later corpus work shows that secure habitation functions as a common protected-minimum interface analogous to a Tier 1 economic floor, the tier may be revisited without changing Housing's substantive domain ownership.

# 2. Core concepts and distinctions

## 2.1 Shelter

**Shelter** is immediate physical protection from exposure, danger, or displacement.

Emergency shelter may be temporary, collective, mobile, improvised, or crisis-governed. Shelter is essential but does not by itself establish an ordinary tenancy, permanent allocation, title, or secure-habitation interest beyond the protections created by applicable law.

## 2.2 Habitation

**Habitation** is occupation of a place as ordinary living space with the material conditions necessary for residence.

A jurisdiction should define minimum habitation conditions appropriate to climate, culture, building type, household structure, disability access, and local infrastructure.

## 2.3 Home

For this framework, **home** is habitation plus a sufficiently durable personal, household, familial, customary, cooperative, community, or legal relationship to a place that loss of that place engages continuity and procedural interests.

This is a governance definition, not a metaphysical claim that the meaning of home can be fully reduced to law.

## 2.4 Resident

A **resident** is a person who in fact lives in a place with more than merely transient presence, subject to jurisdiction-specific rules.

Resident status is not automatically equivalent to citizenship, domicile, tenancy, title, tax residence, household membership, immigration status, or electoral residence.

## 2.5 Occupant and habitation claimant

An **occupant** is a person physically residing in a place.

A **habitation claimant** is a person asserting a secure-habitation interest, procedural protection, allocation right, continuity claim, or remedy concerning a place.

The framework uses these terms because many globally important housing relationships do not fit the conventional landlord-tenant model.

## 2.6 Owner, landlord, provider, operator, and custodian

These roles must not be collapsed.

- an **owner** holds title or another proprietary interest;
- a **landlord** grants or administers a tenancy or comparable occupancy relationship;
- a **housing provider** supplies or allocates accommodation under a public, social, cooperative, charitable, community, private, or mixed model;
- an **operator** manages a building or housing service;
- a **custodian/trustee** may hold assets for beneficiaries without being the ordinary housing decision-maker.

One actor may perform several roles only where conflicts, records, review, and accountability are explicitly managed.

## 2.7 Housing security

**Housing security** means that a person's ability to remain housed is not subject to arbitrary, opaque, unreviewable, or materially impossible-to-challenge loss.

It does not mean every residence relationship is permanent.

## 2.8 Secondary household resident

A **secondary household resident** is a person who in fact uses a dwelling as ordinary living quarters but is not the sole or primary holder of the lease, title, cooperative share, allocation, licence, or other formal habitation instrument. Examples may include partners, adult children, elders, carers, dependants, relatives, friends, and other household members.

Secondary residence can create procedural and continuity interests without automatically creating co-tenancy, title, inheritance, permanent possession, or a right to remain against a competent safety, family, justice, or protective order.

## 2.9 Community-based habitation interest

A **community-based habitation interest** is a residence relationship constituted or recognized through a kinship, village, neighbourhood, cooperative, religious, customary, mutual-aid, or other collective arrangement that is not adequately represented as a bundle of individual tenancies.

This category does not subsume Indigenous authority. Indigenous residence relationships remain governed by the affected Indigenous nation or community under §3.7.

## 2.10 Derived habitation interest

A **derived habitation interest** is a residence relationship whose legal or practical basis is constituted through, or materially depends upon, one or more other relationships rather than arising solely and directly from the owner, title-holder, public allocator, or primary provider.

Examples may include a lawful subtenant, lodger, licensee from a tenant, resident under a chain lease, tied-housing occupant, student-housing resident, supported-living resident, or another person whose right to occupy is conditioned by one or more independently governed relationships.

A derived interest is not automatically a direct tenancy with the property owner. Its constituting bases, dependencies, duration, termination routes, notice, and any protections on change or loss of a basis must be identified separately. Change in one basis shall not make the derived holder administratively invisible or be presumed to extinguish every other independently constituted basis.

Where a person holds an identifiable licence, lodging, subtenancy, membership-linked residence, or other constituting relationship, however weak its substantive protection may be under local law, this derived-interest architecture governs that relationship. Section 3.11 is the minimum factual-residence floor only where no constituting habitation relationship can be established.

## 2.11 Competing habitation claims

**Competing habitation claims** exist when two or more persons or households assert legally or socially cognizable claims to the same dwelling that cannot all be satisfied by simultaneous physical occupation.

The claims may be simultaneous, successive, family-related, customary, post-conflict, inheritance-linked, derived, or otherwise constituted.

Housing shall preserve claim provenance, interim safety, notice, belongings, and continuity consequences. It does not decide substantive priority unless a competent housing tribunal or other adjudicator has separately been given that power.

## 2.12 Habitation basis

A **habitation basis** is an independently constituted legal, contractual, administrative, customary, family, membership, employment, care, educational, migration, allocation, court-ordered, community, or other relationship that **constitutes, supports, conditions, limits, modifies, or terminates** a person's habitation position.

A single habitation position may have no formal basis beyond factual residence, one basis, or several bases at once.

Bases may relate to a habitation position as:
- **constitutive** — creates the habitation interest;
- **conditional** — continued habitation depends upon it;
- **additive** — independently supports habitation alongside another basis;
- **alternative** — either of several bases can support habitation;
- **limiting** — narrows duration, use, eligibility, or scope;
- **terminating** — a separately lawful decision may end or trigger review of habitation;
- **procedural** — supplies notice, appeal, succession, allocation, or other process without itself creating possession.

The labels describe the relationship for governance purposes; they do not determine the substantive legal validity or priority of the basis.

> **Dependency is representational. Priority is adjudicatory.**

No basis is presumed superior merely because it arises from title, property law, a public authority, or because it is listed first in a record.

## 2.13 Habitation gatekeeper

A **habitation gatekeeper** is an actor whose independently constituted decision can materially determine eligibility for, continuation of, modification of, or transition from a habitation relationship **without necessarily owning, leasing, allocating, funding, or physically managing the dwelling**.

Examples may include a cooperative nominating body, care authority, employer, educational institution, customary council, family court, membership body, immigration authority, programme administrator, or another actor whose decision operates as a condition or gate.

Gatekeeper status gives Housing no authority over the gatekeeper's substantive mandate. Housing governs only the visibility, timing, handoff, and resident-facing habitation consequences of that decision where adopted law so provides.

## 2.14 Place-specific habitation and non-place-specific accommodation entitlement

A **place-specific habitation interest** concerns a particular dwelling, room, site, household, or locality.

A **non-place-specific accommodation entitlement** is a separately constituted duty or claim that suitable accommodation be provided **somewhere**, without necessarily guaranteeing continued occupation of the current dwelling.

Examples may arise in care placement, homelessness assistance, refugee reception, disaster replacement, managed retreat, service accommodation, public allocation, or another domain.

The two may coexist. Lawful loss of a place-specific interest does not by itself extinguish a separately constituted accommodation entitlement; nor does an accommodation entitlement automatically create a right to remain in the current dwelling.

# 3. Constitutional principles

## 3.1 Secure habitation as a protected continuity interest

Other GGF processes that can foreseeably affect a person's home shall identify housing consequences before acting and shall not treat displacement as an invisible externality.

Where lawful action nevertheless requires relocation, termination, redevelopment, closure, remediation, or recovery, the responsible process shall identify the affected habitation interests and the applicable continuity, notice, support, compensation, relocation, or remedy route.

## 3.2 No housing exclusion or eviction by indicator

Rent arrears data, housing-risk scores, tenant-screening scores, credit or payment proxies, building-condition alerts, benefit status, migration data, employment status, household composition, predictive models, behavioural inferences, or automated fraud indicators may trigger review where lawfully permitted.

They do not by themselves deny an initial tenancy or housing allocation, terminate a habitation interest, authorize entry, order eviction, cancel a waiting-list position, set rent, establish fraud, or remove essential services. A consequential housing-access or housing-loss decision requires an independently valid rule, purpose-appropriate evidence, reasons, correction, and an applicable challenge route.

A housing system therefore needs an **entry firewall** as well as an eviction firewall: exclusion before a person becomes a resident can be as consequential as removal after a relationship has formed.

## 3.3 No deprivation through administrative error

A person should not lose essential habitation solely because of an unresolved identity collision, registry error, payment-system failure, incorrect benefit record, inaccessible notice, or other correctable administrative defect.

Applicable law may provide temporary protection, interim relief, suspension, emergency accommodation, or expedited review while the defect is resolved.

## 3.4 Functional separation

Where possible, the institution that owns or operates housing should not be the sole institution that:

- sets binding habitability standards;
- investigates its own alleged violations;
- adjudicates eviction or serious tenancy disputes;
- determines appeals from its own allocation decisions;
- controls the only source of resident legal support.

Where small jurisdictions combine functions, independent review and conflict controls are required.

## 3.5 Rights of residents do not erase rights of others

Secure-habitation protection must coexist with:

- rights of other residents;
- owners' lawful interests;
- worker safety;
- neighbourhood and ecological interests;
- Indigenous authority;
- public-health and building-safety duties;
- creditors and public finance where applicable;
- legitimate redevelopment, remediation, and infrastructure needs.

The framework therefore emphasizes constituted balancing, not absolute possession.

## 3.6 Children, elders, disability, illness, and dependency

Housing decisions shall identify foreseeable disproportionate effects on people whose health, safety, communication, mobility, care relationships, education, or survival depend strongly on continuity of place.

This does not create a universal veto. It requires that those consequences be visible and addressed through the applicable rights, accessibility, welfare, family, health, disability, or social-protection framework.

## 3.7 Indigenous and customary habitation

The framework does not convert Indigenous, customary, collective, sacred, or kinship-based residence relationships into ordinary tenancy.

Affected Indigenous nations and communities determine the applicable law, representatives, consent requirements, territorial relationships, protected knowledge, and non-engagement rights. Global or municipal housing bodies cannot substitute for that authority.

## 3.8 Non-discrimination and practical accessibility

Housing systems shall comply with applicable equality, disability, family, gender, age, migration, and other rights law.

Formal equality is insufficient where the process itself is inaccessible. Notice, applications, hearings, allocation, inspection, complaint, and remedy should support disability access, language access, low-bandwidth and offline routes, representation, and reasonable assistance.

## 3.9 No forced digital dependence

Access to essential housing protection, homelessness assistance, complaint, or review should not depend solely on possession of a smartphone, biometric credential, digital wallet, or high-bandwidth connection.

## 3.10 Material exit and resident voice

A housing system should assess whether residents possess meaningful alternatives or whether dependence on a single employer, landlord, platform, institution, family gatekeeper, or public provider makes nominal consent ineffective.

Resident organization, tenant associations, cooperative governance, ombuds routes, and independent advocacy may be appropriate safeguards where adopted.

## 3.11 Minimum process for factual residence without formal title

A jurisdiction should not make the absence of a written lease, formal title, national identity number, birth certificate, citizenship document, or other civil-registration credential a complete procedural void. Where a person can show more than merely transient factual residence, law should define what minimum protections attach before removal or exclusion from belongings and shelter. Depending on context, these may include notice, an opportunity to contest the asserted facts, safe handling of belongings and medicines, protection against violence or self-help removal, and a handoff to emergency or replacement accommodation where required.

A housing-protection or challenge record should be openable through a purpose-appropriate **provisional or local claimant identifier** where the person's ordinary civil identity is unavailable, disputed, unsafe to disclose, or not required for the housing purpose. Residence may be evidenced through testimony, community attestation, witness statements, utility or service records, correspondence, photographs, provider records, or other lawful means. Housing shall not convert that provisional identifier into citizenship, tax residence, migration status, voter registration, or a general civil identity.

This minimum process does **not** automatically create title, permanent tenancy, compensation, priority over a prior lawful claimant, or protection for coercive seizure. The jurisdiction must define the evidentiary threshold, exceptions for immediate danger, competing claims, and the point at which a fuller secure-habitation interest arises.

## 3.12 Secondary household residence and intra-household exclusion

A person should not fall into a procedural void merely because another resident, rather than the owner/provider, holds the formal habitation instrument. Where a secondary household resident can show more than transient ordinary residence, applicable law should identify what minimum protections attach to exclusion, loss of belongings, interruption of medication or assistive devices, and emergency housing continuity.

Those protections shall not be interpreted as an automatic co-tenancy or as a power to force continued co-residence where domestic violence, stalking, coercive control, child protection, abuse, or immediate safety is at issue. Competent family, justice, health, or protective authorities retain their mandates. Housing's role is to keep the habitation consequences, belongings, notice where safe, and alternative-accommodation route visible.

## 3.13 Housing, care, treatment, and custody powers must remain distinct

Where one institution provides both accommodation and care, disability support, treatment, education, rehabilitation, custody, supervision, or another dependency-intensive service, it shall identify which powers arise from the habitation relationship and which arise from a separate care, clinical, educational, justice, disability, or custodial mandate.

A housing-management decision cannot silently become a treatment sanction, movement restriction, visitor ban, restraint, discharge, search, or custody order. Conversely, loss or change of care eligibility does not by itself execute an eviction unless applicable law creates that consequence through a housing route.

Where no competent receiving framework or authority exists for a claimed non-housing power, that absence is an interface gap, not permission for the housing provider to exercise the power by default.

## 3.14 Legal pluralism and disputed authority

Housing systems often operate under overlapping municipal, national, customary, cooperative, religious, community, Indigenous, contractual, and informal authority. This framework shall not resolve those conflicts by silently privileging state form, formal title, numerical majority, or whichever rule appears most protective in the abstract.

Competing claims to authority shall remain visible. Where two GGF/domain frameworks claim the same decision, allocation follows CIS §18.1; affected Indigenous authority and rights remain non-substitutable. CIS §18.1 does **not** decide priority between two residents, two households, or a resident and a community claimant merely because their interests conflict.

Where no competent authority exists, the case is recorded as a constitutional/interface gap rather than converted into residual Housing jurisdiction.

## 3.15 Derived interests and multiple habitation bases must remain visible when one basis changes

Where a subtenancy, lodging, licence, chain lease, tied-housing arrangement, care-linked residence, membership-linked tenancy, or comparable habitation position depends on one or more other relationships, the system shall record the relevant bases and dependencies.

Termination, expiry, insolvency, foreclosure, death, separation, loss of membership, employment termination, care reassessment, migration-status change, transfer, or invalidation of one basis may affect habitation according to applicable law. It does not by itself authorize the system to:
- treat the resident as never having existed;
- presume that every other independently constituted basis has ended;
- convert a non-housing decision into an unreviewable housing action;
- create a direct tenancy or other new right that applicable law does not provide.

The competent process should identify notice, interim protection, belongings, payment custody, any lawful continuation, succession or regularization route, and any continuity or relocation consequence.

Housing preserves the dependency graph; it does not adjudicate the underlying employment, care, membership, family, migration, customary, property, or other domain.

## 3.16 Competing habitation interests require an adjudication owner

Where two or more valid or plausibly valid habitation claims to one physical dwelling cannot all be satisfied, Housing shall not resolve the conflict by ranking claimants itself.

The competent jurisdiction shall identify the adjudicator or authority able to determine substantive priority under the relevant property, family, customary, Indigenous, succession, post-conflict, tenancy, or other law.

Pending that decision, the system should record:
- each claim and its provenance;
- current physical occupation;
- immediate safety constraints;
- non-dissipation or anti-self-help protections where law provides;
- belongings and essential-support needs;
- interim occupancy or shelter orders, if separately authorized;
- the continuity route for whichever claimant cannot retain the physical dwelling.

A losing claim to a particular dwelling does not automatically establish a universal right to publicly funded replacement housing; any replacement or compensation duty must identify its own lawful basis.

Pending adjudication, no claimant's mere assertion of priority authorizes self-help physical exclusion, service withdrawal, destruction of belongings, or other compulsory habitation loss unless separately authorized under the ordinary safety and emergency rules.

## 3.17 Community or customary termination is distinct from downstream continuity

A competent community, customary, religious, cooperative, family, or Indigenous authority may in some systems lawfully terminate a person's right to remain within a particular collective habitation arrangement.

Housing shall not use continuity language to silently override that authority. The termination decision and the downstream habitation consequences are separate objects.

Where applicable law creates notice, appeal, belongings, emergency shelter, relocation, compensation, or other continuity duties after such termination, those duties remain visible and must be routed to their competent owner. Where no such owner exists, the unresolved duty follows §3.18 rather than becoming a Housing veto over the community decision.

## 3.18 Fallback continuity custody without residual housing sovereignty

An adopting jurisdiction should identify an existing lawful body or function capable of **fallback continuity custody** when an ordinary landlord, provider, programme, framework, or other responsible actor disappears, refuses the handoff, or has no remaining mandate.

Fallback continuity custody means:
1. record the unresolved habitation duty;
2. preserve claimant contact and essential evidence using identity-light methods where necessary;
3. attempt referral to competent housing, homelessness, justice, care, disaster, social-protection, or other authorities;
4. use only emergency-accommodation, safeguarding, interim-relief, or assistance powers the custodian already lawfully possesses;
5. keep the duty visible until accepted, discharged, lawfully extinguished, or recorded as a constitutional gap.

The custodian gains no title, allocation, eviction, tax, funding, expropriation, permanent-housing, family-law, care, or enforcement jurisdiction merely by holding the unresolved duty.

If no such custodian has been constituted, `HAB-28` records a genuine constitutional/interface gap. The framework does not cure absence of state capacity by inventing power.

## 3.19 Loss of one basis does not erase another

Where a person has two or more independently constituted habitation bases, termination or invalidation of one basis does not extinguish another merely because both concern the same dwelling.

Whether one basis legally overrides, conditions, or defeats another is a question for the competent authority or adjudicator under the relevant law.

Housing shall therefore preserve each basis separately until it is:
- lawfully terminated;
- merged or reconstituted by an authorized process;
- determined not to exist;
- superseded by a competent adjudication;
- or closed under the common lifecycle grammar.

## 3.20 External gatekeeper decisions require habitation-consequence routing

A gatekeeper may lawfully decide membership, employment, care eligibility, enrolment, migration status, programme eligibility, customary membership, or another non-housing matter within its own mandate.

If that decision can materially alter habitation, the responsible system shall expose:
- the gatekeeper and its lawful mandate;
- the affected habitation basis;
- whether the housing effect is automatic under law or requires a separate housing decision;
- notice and effective date;
- available challenge or correction route in the gatekeeper's own domain;
- the housing-continuity or termination process that follows;
- any separately constituted non-place-specific accommodation entitlement that survives.

A non-housing decision may lawfully change a housing condition. It does not thereby become a housing decision, and its housing consequence must not disappear between domains.

## 3.21 Status transitions must preserve causal continuity

Where applicable law changes a person's habitation status — for example secondary resident to statutory successor, subtenant to direct tenant, temporary resident to durable allocation, or tied resident to independent tenancy — the system shall record the transition as a causal change rather than close the old status and create an unrelated new one.

`HAB-29` records the authorizing event, old status, new status, bases that continue or end, responsible authority, effective time, rights/liabilities transferred, and any unresolved challenge.

`HAB-29` does not decide whether succession, regularization, transfer, or conversion is legally available.

The framework should be able to represent at least the following without pretending they are legally identical:

1. owner-occupied homes;
2. private rental tenancies;
3. subtenancies, lodgings, licences, and other derived habitation interests;
4. public or social rental housing;
5. housing cooperatives;
6. Community Land Trust housing;
7. stewardship-trust housing;
8. community or customary tenure;
9. Indigenous residence relationships governed under Indigenous law;
10. informal settlements and informal rental arrangements;
11. family or multigenerational occupation;
12. employer-provided or tied housing;
13. student and institutional accommodation;
14. disability-support and supported living;
15. shelters and transitional housing;
16. migrant, refugee, or reception accommodation;
17. mobile, seasonal, nomadic, or semi-nomadic habitation where applicable;
18. collective residences and intentional communities;
19. mortgage-financed owner occupation;
20. secondary household residence where the person is not named on the primary instrument;
21. non-Indigenous community-based or kinship-based collective habitation;
22. mixed housing-and-care, supported-living, residential-treatment, student-residence, or custody-adjacent settings where accommodation and other institutional powers coexist;
23. dwellings converted between ordinary habitation and lawful short-term/tourism accommodation where existing resident interests may be affected.

Commercial hotel/tourist stays are not ordinary secure habitation merely because people sleep there. The framework becomes relevant when an existing home or habitation relationship is created, terminated, degraded, or converted through the tourism/short-term-let system.

# 5. Institutional architecture

The Framework creates no mandatory global housing body.

A jurisdiction implementing it should identify which existing or newly constituted institutions perform the following functions.

## 5.1 Housing policy and planning authority

May assess housing need, affordability, supply, public investment, housing mix, and distributional outcomes. It does not acquire zoning, title, tax, credit, or compulsory-acquisition power merely because it plans housing.

## 5.2 Housing provider or landlord

Provides, leases, manages, or allocates dwellings under applicable public, private, cooperative, community, or mixed arrangements.

## 5.3 Habitation standards authority

Sets or enforces legally adopted minimum standards for safety, sanitation, accessibility, essential services, and habitability. Entry and inspection require separate lawful authority.

A safety finding or condemnation decision must distinguish the **need to protect residents from the building** from the **responsibility of the owner/provider toward the residents**. Closure of an unsafe dwelling does not by itself erase repair, relocation, compensation, successor-provider, or other continuity duties that applicable law places on the responsible party.

## 5.4 Allocation authority

Where public, social, community, or scarce subsidized housing is allocated administratively, the allocation authority shall publish eligibility, priority, evidence, waiting-list or queue rules, accessibility accommodations, review, correction, and anti-discrimination safeguards.

## 5.5 Homelessness and housing-continuity service

Coordinates prevention, rapid accommodation, stabilization, benefits/service access, and transition to durable habitation. It should not need to manufacture a punitive legal status before assistance becomes available.

## 5.6 Independent review, tribunal, court, ombuds, or equivalent

Serious eviction, allocation, discrimination, habitability, deposit, rent, or public-provider disputes should have access to an independent route appropriate to the jurisdiction.

The framework does not create a universal housing court.

## 5.7 Resident representation and advocacy

Jurisdictions should specify whether and how tenant unions, resident councils, legal-aid bodies, disability advocates, community organizations, or other representatives participate in housing governance and disputes.

## 5.8 Mixed housing-and-care provider

An institution that supplies both accommodation and another dependency-intensive service shall maintain a **power-separation map** identifying at minimum:

- the habitation relationship and housing decision-maker;
- the care, treatment, disability, education, supervision, or custody mandate, if any;
- who may control entry, exit, visitors, room access, personal property, fees, medication, movement, privacy, searches, discharge, or relocation;
- the legal source and review route for each power;
- which acts require a separate non-housing handoff;
- what happens to habitation if the non-housing service ends, and vice versa.

Institutional convenience is not a constituting instrument.

# 6. Minimum secure-habitation floor

This section establishes a design question, not yet a universal numeric standard.

A jurisdiction adopting the Framework should define what conditions are necessary for a place to count as minimally adequate habitation in its context.

The schedule should consider:

- structural safety;
- fire and environmental safety;
- potable water and sanitation;
- protection from weather and climate exposure;
- ventilation and indoor environmental quality;
- essential energy where needed for survival and ordinary habitation;
- reasonable privacy and personal security;
- accessibility and reasonable accommodation;
- space and occupancy standards appropriate to household structure;
- protection from severe contamination, infestation, or hazardous materials;
- connection to essential services where locally necessary;
- culturally appropriate conditions where relevant;
- repair obligations and response times;
- emergency alternatives when a dwelling becomes temporarily uninhabitable.

A durable replacement-habitation outcome following forced relocation, managed retreat, condemnation, redevelopment, provider failure, or similar involuntary loss should not permanently ratchet residents below the jurisdiction's applicable minimum secure-habitation floor merely because the original home became unavailable. Emergency or transitional accommodation may temporarily operate under separately defined emergency standards; the durable route must identify how and when the ordinary floor is restored.

A failure of one condition does not automatically determine the legal remedy. Depending on severity and law, the response may involve repair, rent adjustment, temporary relocation, enforcement, compensation, closure, public assistance, or another route.

Where an owner or provider has materially contributed to uninhabitability through deliberate capital stripping, repeated refusal of required maintenance, diversion of repair reserves, or other culpable neglect, a later safety closure should not become a clean route for extinguishing the residents' continuity claims. The safety authority protects people from the unsafe building; a separate process determines repair responsibility, relocation, compensation, provider failure, or other remedy.

# 7. Tenancy and non-title residence relationships

## 7.1 Constituting relationship

A tenancy or comparable residence relationship should be able to identify:

- parties and authorized representatives;
- dwelling or habitation unit;
- legal source and type of relationship;
- duration and renewal rules;
- rent, contribution, fee, or non-monetary obligations where applicable;
- deposits or security arrangements;
- utilities and service responsibilities;
- repair and maintenance duties;
- privacy, entry, and inspection rules;
- occupancy and household rules;
- subletting or transfer rules where relevant;
- termination grounds and process;
- complaint, correction, and dispute route.

Oral, customary, informal, or low-documentation relationships may require alternative evidence rather than being treated as nonexistent by default.

## 7.2 Rent, fees, and deposits

This framework does not prescribe a universal rent-control regime.

Jurisdictions may choose among policy families including:

- market rent with procedural safeguards;
- rent stabilization;
- rent caps or reference rents;
- cost-linked or income-linked social rents;
- rent-to-income assistance;
- deposit limits or public deposit guarantees;
- anti-gouging rules during scarcity or crisis;
- vacancy or speculation measures through other frameworks;
- negotiated or cooperative rent-setting.

Whichever model is adopted should state its objective, authority, evidence, formula or decision process, affected parties, review, exceptions, distributional effects, and revision path.

## 7.3 Entry, privacy, and inspection

Ownership or landlord status does not create unlimited entry authority.

Housing inspection, emergency entry, landlord access, repairs, safety checks, and law-enforcement access remain distinct powers with distinct legal bases.

## 7.4 Retaliation and resident organization

Where law recognizes resident association, complaint, unionization, collective bargaining, code enforcement, or legal challenge, a landlord or provider should not be able to defeat those rights through retaliatory termination or selective service withdrawal.

The exact protected activities and remedies remain jurisdiction-specific.

## 7.5 Housing access, applicant screening, and denial of entry

Private, public, cooperative, community, or platform-mediated housing systems may use lawful screening or eligibility processes, but the process shall identify:

- the purpose of screening;
- the legal or contractual authority;
- the facts or criteria actually relevant to that purpose;
- prohibited or protected characteristics and inferences;
- the role of credit, income, references, prior tenancy, criminal-history, eviction-history, platform, behavioural, or other data where applicable;
- data provenance, correction, and expiry;
- whether an automated or third-party score is used;
- reasons for denial;
- accommodation or alternative evidence routes where required;
- correction and challenge.

A third-party tenant-screening score, opaque risk model, data-broker profile, inferred neighbourhood risk, or proxy for a protected characteristic shall not be treated as self-authenticating evidence of tenancy reliability. Where law permits such evidence, the consequential rule and challenge route must remain visible to the applicant.

This section does not create a universal duty for every private owner to accept every applicant. It governs the integrity and reviewability of whatever access rules the competent jurisdiction lawfully permits.

## 7.6 Secondary household members

Where housing law permits household members who are not named on the primary instrument, the provider and competent authority should distinguish:

- the primary habitation holder;
- secondary household residents;
- dependants and carers;
- persons with a separate legal right to occupy;
- guests or transient presence;
- contested household membership.

A landlord/provider household-composition rule shall not by itself decide family, intimate-partner, domestic-violence, inheritance, guardianship, or custody questions. Where exclusion of a secondary household resident would produce homelessness or loss of essential belongings/support, the applicable `HAB-21` route should preserve minimum process and housing continuity without forcing continued co-residence.

## 7.7 Housing/care/custody power separation

In mixed accommodation-and-care settings, termination of accommodation, change of room, visitor restrictions, lockouts, searches, removal of belongings, and physical exclusion are housing consequences even when the institution describes them as care management. Any independent clinical, protective, educational, or custodial power must be separately identified under §3.13 and `HAB-22`.

## 7.8 Derived interests, multiple bases, and dependency graphs

Where a person's habitation is derived, conditional, or otherwise shaped by one or more independently governed relationships, the constituting record should identify each relevant **habitation basis** rather than forcing the arrangement into one superior-interest chain.

For each basis, record where lawful and necessary:
- basis type and constituting instrument or evidence;
- constituting authority or domain;
- gatekeeper, if any;
- whether the basis is constitutive, conditional, additive, alternative, limiting, terminating, or procedural;
- the dwelling, accommodation class, or habitation position it affects;
- dependency links to other bases;
- whether loss of this basis necessarily affects habitation or only triggers review;
- whether other independently constituted bases survive;
- payments, deposits, service or membership obligations where relevant;
- duration and termination conditions;
- notice and belongings protections;
- any lawful continuation, succession, regularization, status-change, or relocation route;
- the authority that resolves substantive disputes.

A simple A→B→C chain may be represented through linked `HAB-25` entries. A multi-parent arrangement may link several bases to the same habitation position. An optional upstream-relationship reference may assist continuity where known, but no “root property interest” is required and title is not presumed to be the root node.

No dependency record creates privity of contract, tenancy, membership, care eligibility, employment, immigration status, customary authority, or another substantive right unless applicable law independently does so.

## 7.9 Gatekeepers, nominations, eligibility conditions, and external vetoes

Where an actor controls access or continuation without being the owner/provider, the system should identify whether that actor is:
- merely recommending or nominating;
- determining eligibility;
- imposing a condition that another housing actor must apply;
- exercising a legally binding veto;
- triggering a separate review rather than automatic termination.

The gatekeeper's decision and the housing actor's consequential decision should not be collapsed into one record unless the same lawful authority genuinely owns both functions.

A gatekeeper decision that ends one basis does not silently extinguish another independent basis. Where the dwelling-specific interest ends but a non-place-specific accommodation entitlement survives, route that entitlement separately.

# 8. Termination, eviction, displacement, and relocation

## 8.1 Eviction as a consequential act

An eviction or equivalent compulsory loss of habitation is a consequential act requiring a defined legal route.

A lawful route should identify:

- the habitation interest affected;
- the alleged legal ground;
- competent decision-maker;
- evidence and contested facts;
- notice and accessibility;
- time to respond;
- representation or counsel where applicable;
- vulnerability and continuity impacts;
- interim relief;
- adjudication or independent review;
- execution authority;
- treatment of belongings, dependants, animals, medicines, assistive devices, and essential records;
- closure and remedy.

## 8.2 Grounds do not execute themselves

Non-payment, lease expiry, owner need, redevelopment, nuisance, safety risk, illegal activity, overcrowding, abandonment, or another legally recognized ground may justify opening a process.

The ground itself does not authorize self-help eviction, lockout, utility deprivation, seizure of belongings, harassment, or physical removal unless applicable law separately permits the exact act.

## 8.3 Abandonment

Absence from a dwelling should not automatically establish abandonment where displacement, hospitalization, disaster, detention, care obligations, migration, seasonal mobility, conflict, or other circumstances may explain absence.

A jurisdiction should define evidence and correction rules for abandonment determinations.

## 8.4 Redevelopment and public-purpose displacement

Where housing is lost through redevelopment, infrastructure, ecological restoration, land return, hazard mitigation, public works, or other public-purpose action, the constituting process should identify:

- who is displaced;
- title and non-title habitation interests;
- lawful authority;
- consultation and applicable consent;
- relocation options;
- continuity of school, care, work, disability supports, and community ties;
- compensation where legally due;
- right of return or replacement rights where adopted;
- timing and appeal;
- unresolved claims after physical relocation.

## 8.5 No disguised or constructive eviction

Water, sanitation, energy, access systems, repairs, security, or other essential habitation services should not be deliberately withdrawn as a substitute for lawful termination where the jurisdiction would otherwise require an eviction or review process.

The same principle applies to slower forms of constructive eviction. An owner or provider should not be able to evade termination duties by deliberately stripping maintenance, allowing preventable deterioration, creating intolerable conditions, or engineering a condemnation that makes departure inevitable.

A safety authority may still order urgent closure where the building is genuinely unsafe. That protective act does not determine whether the owner/provider caused the condition or erase any separately applicable repair, relocation, compensation, or continuity obligation.

## 8.6 When physical place continuity becomes impossible

Secure habitation does not create a right to remain indefinitely on land or in a structure that competent ecological, safety, disaster, public-health, or Indigenous authority has lawfully determined cannot remain inhabited.

Where permanent or long-duration physical return is no longer lawful or safe, the relevant process should determine whether the resident's **place-based continuity interest converts into a replacement-habitation interest**. That interest may include, where law provides, timely replacement accommodation, continuity of affordability and accessibility, preservation of household and community ties where practicable, relocation support, compensation, return priority if conditions later change, and a clear record of any unresolved claim.

The purpose is to prevent a false choice between anchoring people to dangerous geography and treating the disappearance of a place as the disappearance of the resident's housing claim.

Durable replacement should, after any separately justified emergency period, meet the applicable §6 minimum floor. A relocation programme may lawfully change location, tenure form, unit type, or other attributes; it should not use forced transition as a route to normalize permanently sub-minimum habitation.

## 8.7 Slow-onset habitability degradation

Heat stress, recurrent flooding, coastal erosion, subsidence, water scarcity, contamination, wildfire exposure, permafrost thaw, infrastructure decay, or another progressive condition may undermine habitation long before an emergency authority declares a disaster.

A worsening hazard or habitability indicator may trigger observation, reassessment, repair planning, insurance/finance review, infrastructure action, or proactive relocation planning. It does not by itself terminate a habitation interest or order retreat.

Where evidence shows a credible pathway toward long-duration or permanent uninhabitability, the responsible authorities should be able to open a `HAB-24` process before crisis displacement occurs, identifying uncertainty, timelines, repair/adaptation alternatives, funding dependencies, resident participation, replacement-habitation contingencies, and the authority required for any later consequential act.

# 9. Affordability and rent-burden governance

> **Housing-systems seam — provisional.** This chapter belongs in the joint draft because affordability can destroy secure habitation, but many substantive instruments are fiscal, labour, land, social-protection, or market rules owned elsewhere.

Housing affordability is a systems outcome, not a single universal ratio.

A jurisdiction may monitor:

- rent-to-income or housing-cost burden;
- residual income after housing costs;
- overcrowding;
- involuntary displacement;
- homelessness entries and exits;
- waiting-list duration;
- vacancy and utilization;
- construction and replacement rates;
- land and dwelling prices;
- mortgage distress;
- household energy and utility burden;
- accessibility-adjusted housing availability;
- geographic mismatch between housing, work, care, education, and mobility.

No metric automatically creates a rent cap, subsidy, eviction moratorium, building obligation, or allocation priority.

Possible policy responses belong to different constitutional owners and may include housing assistance, public/social housing, rent regulation, land policy, taxation, construction policy, transport planning, wage policy, social protection, or credit regulation.

Primary counterparties at this seam include **Adaptive Tax** for fiscal instruments, **AUBI and Work in Liberation** for income and employment continuity, **Bioregional Polis** for land and municipal supply conditions, **Financial Systems and any future Monetary/Credit architecture** for payment and credit machinery, and the competent social-protection authority for housing assistance. Housing may state affordability and continuity outcomes that these systems must account for where law adopts them; it does not choose those systems' instruments by implication.

The Framework's role is to make those handoffs explicit.

# 10. Homelessness prevention and durable habitation

## 10.1 Homelessness as a housing-system state

Homelessness should be represented as a condition requiring housing and support response, not merely as individual non-compliance or absence from a registry.

The framework should distinguish at minimum:

- unsheltered homelessness;
- emergency shelter;
- temporary or transitional accommodation;
- insecure couch-surfing or hidden homelessness where relevant;
- imminent housing loss;
- institutional discharge without habitation;
- repeated cycling through unstable accommodation;
- durable habitation.

## 10.2 Prevention

Prevention mechanisms may include:

- rent or arrears mediation;
- emergency financial support;
- legal assistance;
- landlord-provider negotiation;
- domestic-violence relocation support;
- discharge planning from hospitals, prisons, care, military, or institutions;
- employment-transition housing continuity;
- family stabilization;
- mortgage-distress intervention;
- rapid repair or temporary relocation after habitability failure.

The Framework does not mandate one prevention model.

## 10.3 Housing First and other service models

Housing First, supported housing, transitional housing, shelter-led systems, cooperative/community placement, and other models may be tested and adopted according to local evidence and population needs.

The Framework should not constitutionalize one service model prematurely. It should require that programs state their target population, conditions, evidence, resident rights, exit route, failure mode, and long-term housing outcome.

## 10.4 No assistance-by-surveillance default

People should not have to surrender unrelated health, migration, policing, employment, family, biometric, or behavioural data merely to access basic homelessness assistance unless separately lawful and necessary for the stated purpose.

# 11. Public, social, cooperative, and community housing

Where non-market or publicly supported housing exists, its governance should identify:

- legal owner and custodian;
- provider/operator;
- public or community purpose;
- capitalization and ongoing funding;
- eligibility and allocation rules;
- rent or contribution method;
- accessibility obligations;
- resident governance or representation;
- maintenance and reserve obligations;
- procurement and conflicts;
- audit and beneficial ownership of contractors;
- anti-corruption safeguards;
- transfer, redevelopment, sale, or conversion rules;
- continuity of resident rights during provider failure;
- complaint, appeal, and correction;
- dissolution and successor custody.

Community Land Trusts and stewardship trusts remain governed by their relevant land/property frameworks. This Framework governs the habitation relationship of residents within those structures.

# 12. Housing supply, construction, and land-use interface

> **Housing-systems seam — provisional.** Housing needs must be visible here, while zoning, construction command, land use, infrastructure, materials, procurement, and many supply instruments remain external powers.

Housing security cannot be produced solely through rights over scarce dwellings. Supply, location, accessibility, infrastructure, land, construction cost, and financing matter.

This Framework therefore requires a **housing-systems interface** without claiming all supply-side powers.

A housing-needs assessment may identify:

- unit shortages or surpluses;
- household formation;
- demographic change;
- accessible-housing need;
- geographic mismatch;
- vacancy and underuse;
- climate and hazard exposure;
- infrastructure capacity;
- replacement needs;
- affordability by tenure type;
- construction bottlenecks.

Those findings may be handed to **Bioregional Polis** for municipal land-use and spatial supply, **Hearthstone** where title/stewardship conversion is implicated, **Regenerative Journeys** where visitor-economy conversion affects ordinary housing, infrastructure and materials authorities for physical delivery constraints, **Adaptive Tax** for fiscal instruments, and other competent land, labour, ecological, or public-investment systems.

The housing framework may define adequacy, access, distributional effects, and need. It does not acquire zoning, permitting, compulsory purchase, tax, tourism-platform, or construction-command authority merely because supply is inadequate.

## 12.1 Habitation-status conversion and short-term/tourism accommodation

A dwelling may move between ordinary habitation, vacancy, seasonal use, and lawful short-term or tourism accommodation. The legal permission for that conversion belongs to the competent land-use, tourism, platform, tax, or other authority. Housing governs the resident-facing consequences where an existing secure-habitation interest may be lost, degraded, or displaced.

A conversion process affecting an occupied home should identify:

- current habitation status and resident interests;
- the legal authority for conversion;
- whether ordinary termination/eviction law is engaged;
- notice, challenge, belongings, relocation, and continuity duties;
- whether the conversion is temporary or durable;
- any housing-supply or displacement assessment required by local law;
- the minimum data exchanged with tourism/platform regulators and its CIS purpose boundary.

A tourism-demand, occupancy, neighbourhood-pressure, or platform indicator does not itself terminate a tenancy or convert a home into commercial accommodation. Conversely, Housing does not create a general power to prohibit short-term letting merely because housing supply is tight.

# 13. Housing finance and homeownership interface

> **Housing-systems seam — provisional.** This chapter governs habitation consequences of finance and insolvency, not the general architecture of credit, secured lending, banking, insolvency, or capital markets.

Mortgage-financed ownership creates a dual relationship: a person may hold title while a lender holds a secured financial claim capable of threatening habitation.

This Framework does not regulate banking or mortgage markets generally.

It does govern the **habitation consequence interface** where financial distress may cause loss of home.

A foreclosure, repossession, forced sale, or equivalent process affecting a primary residence should identify:

- valid financial claim and security interest;
- borrower/resident identities;
- applicable consumer and insolvency protections;
- notice and correction;
- restructuring or hardship options where law provides them;
- independent review or adjudication;
- vulnerable residents and dependants;
- execution and possession route;
- treatment of residual debt or surplus;
- housing-continuity assistance where applicable.

The primary counterparties are **Financial Systems and any future Monetary & Credit architecture** for payment, underwriting, leverage, lender conduct, securitization, and macroprudential questions; **Adaptive Tax** where tax enforcement or incentives touch the home; **Hearthstone** where title or stewardship changes; and **Shared Capital & Social Inheritance** where pooled/public capital owns or finances residential assets.

Housing may require that home-loss consequences, existing resident claims, notice, challenge, and continuity remain visible. It does not thereby dictate the pricing of credit, creditor priority, portfolio strategy, or title conversion.

## 13.1 Owner/provider insolvency, receivership, and creditor sale

A resident's habitation interest and the owner's title are separate enough that owner insolvency must not be treated as proof that the residence relationship never existed. Where a private corporate landlord, cooperative, trust, public provider, or other housing owner enters insolvency, receivership, creditor enforcement, or forced sale, the process should identify:

- valid title and creditor claims;
- existing tenancy, licence, customary, cooperative, or other habitation interests;
- which interests survive transfer under applicable law;
- rent/payment custody during administration;
- maintenance, insurance, utilities, and emergency-repair responsibility;
- whether sale with vacant possession is legally permitted and under what process;
- notice, representation, and challenge for residents;
- successor-owner or successor-provider obligations;
- relocation, compensation, public assistance, or other continuity route where occupation lawfully ends;
- treatment of deposits, resident funds, belongings, and records.

This Framework does not reorder creditor priority or create an insolvency stay by itself. It requires the insolvency or property process to make habitation interests visible and to use the protections that competent law has actually created instead of silently extinguishing residents to maximize asset value.

# 14. Cross-framework housing continuity

Any GGF process that can foreseeably cause loss, degradation, or involuntary relocation of habitation should create a **Housing Continuity Handoff** rather than merely stating that housing must be protected.

Candidate triggering domains include:

- Adaptive Tax collection and enforcement;
- Sundown industry closure and worker transition;
- Aegis conversion of security-industry assets and communities;
- Hearthstone property conversion, rematriation, stewardship transition, and asset transfer;
- Bioregional Polis redevelopment and land policy;
- Soil/Living Land remediation and tenure reform;
- Disaster Risk Reduction recovery and managed retreat;
- Migration and refugee reception;
- water, sanitation, energy, or infrastructure failure;
- justice or insolvency processes affecting residence;
- Shared Capital property or fund transitions where housing assets are involved;
- Kintsugi release, survivor support, property repair, sanctuary exit, or reintegration where stable habitation is required;
- Peace and Conflict Resolution return, reintegration, reconstruction, or reconciliation processes where housing is at risk or used as leverage;
- Hearthlight child/family stabilization where housing insecurity could otherwise be converted into family-separation pressure;
- health or care transitions, including discharge, isolation/recovery, disability-support changes, or public-health action that foreseeably affects ordinary residence;
- Regenerative Journeys or other tourism/visitor-economy actions that alter short-term letting, suspend tourism during housing shortage, finance housing protection, or otherwise affect ordinary residence;
- slow-onset climate, ecological, water, heat, erosion, contamination, or infrastructure conditions that may make a home progressively unsafe without a declared disaster;
- institutional decisions in mixed housing-and-care settings that change accommodation while exercising a separate care, treatment, disability, education, or custody mandate.

The handoff should state:

1. affected residents and habitation interests;
2. causal action likely to affect housing;
3. competent housing authority or service;
4. urgency;
5. continuity requirement;
6. relocation, assistance, compensation, or adjudication route if applicable;
7. unresolved duties that survive completion of the originating process;
8. any derived habitation interests and the habitation bases on which they depend;
9. any external gatekeeper decision that conditions access or continuation;
10. any competing claims that require separate adjudication;
11. any non-place-specific accommodation entitlement that survives loss of the current dwelling;
12. any status change requiring a continuity bridge under `HAB-29`;
13. the fallback continuity custodian if the ordinary receiving actor disappears or refuses the handoff.

A `HAB-16` handoff does not itself decide priority among competing claimants and does not manufacture a receiving authority. Where ordinary ownership fails, the initiating process must preserve the handoff until `HAB-28` either identifies a lawful fallback custodian or records the unresolved constitutional gap.

# 15. Data, observation, and housing records

## 15.1 CIS information classes and purpose limitation

Housing records can reveal location, family structure, disability, income, migration status, health, intimate relationships, employment, religion, care needs, and political vulnerability. Personal and claimant-controlled housing records are ordinarily **CIS Class C**; community-controlled or Indigenous-restricted housing knowledge may be **Class D**, with protected non-recording or non-transfer possible under Class E where applicable.

Housing implementations inherit CIS notice, access, correction, explanation, appeal, federated-memory, retention, and data-change rules rather than creating a parallel constitutional data regime.

`HAB-04` shall state the constituting housing purpose and permitted recipients. Ordinary housing administration does not by itself authorize reuse for immigration enforcement, tax assessment, criminal investigation, intelligence, political policing, generalized credit/reputation scoring, or unrelated eligibility. Any independently lawful secondary access must identify its own authority and comply with the applicable CIS and receiving-domain requirements; Housing supplies no shortcut merely because it holds the data.

## 15.2 Recognition-record firewall inheritance

Love Ledger, care, civic, cultural, ecological, or relational recognition records are governed by the CIS §10.4 Recognition-Record Purpose Firewall. Housing does not restate or weaken that test.

Such records are not housing-risk, tenancy-reliability, fraud, allocation-priority, or credit-scoring records merely because they exist. Where a housing decision needs an income, payment, identity, disability-accommodation, residence, or eligibility fact, the responsible system should use a purpose-appropriate record constituted for that function. Any exceptional consequential secondary use of a recognition record must satisfy CIS §10.4.1 and the applicable Housing decision route.

## 15.3 Automated systems

AI or automated systems may support:

- application processing;
- maintenance triage;
- affordability modelling;
- allocation support;
- fraud/anomaly review;
- vacancy analysis;
- inspection scheduling;
- homelessness-service coordination.

They cannot by themselves deny an applicant an initial tenancy or housing allocation, evict, deny essential shelter, terminate a tenancy, establish fraud, cancel a waiting-list position, set an individualized rent, or classify a person as having abandoned a home unless a competent lawfully constituted process explicitly authorizes bounded automation with purpose-specific evidence, correction, review, and human responsibility.

An automated system that acts before entry is not constitutionally harmless merely because no tenancy yet exists. Screening and allocation systems must therefore be tested for opaque exclusion, proxy discrimination, stale or incorrect data, circular use of prior eviction or homelessness records, and lack of a practical correction route.

## 15.4 Identity-light and event-driven administration

A person shall not need a general civil identity record merely to open a housing challenge, protect belongings, contest removal, or enter an emergency-continuity process where applicable law permits other proof.

`HAB-02`, `HAB-03`, `HAB-10`, `HAB-12`, `HAB-16`, `HAB-17`, `HAB-18`, `HAB-21`, `HAB-25`, `HAB-26`, `HAB-27`, `HAB-28`, and `HAB-29` may therefore use a purpose-limited provisional/local claimant identifier where necessary, with later reconciliation if a competent identity system becomes available.

`HAB-21` is **not** a standing household-membership registry. It should ordinarily be created when a secondary resident needs standing, protection, continuity, correction, or adjudication.

`HAB-23` is **not** a continuous government register of daily owner intent or platform occupancy. It should ordinarily be created when a lawfully authorized conversion, investigation, enforcement, planning, or resident-continuity process requires the status question to be decided.

Purpose limitation under CIS applies to both. The desire to prevent evasion does not create blanket housing surveillance authority.

**Current GGF precedent note:** Housing presently uses purpose-limited provisional/local claimant identity because the wider framework canopy has no dedicated civil-registration/legal-identity owner. This is an intentional local workaround, not a claim that Housing owns civil registration. A future identity architecture should be able to import this distinction: **a domain may need to distinguish a claimant for one lawful purpose without constituting that person's universal legal identity.**

# 16. Phase 0 — map the housing constitution before redesigning it

Phase 0 is the spine of this working draft.

A jurisdiction can begin without adopting a new housing ideology or waiting for a global institution.

### Phase 0 Light — minimum local starter

A municipality, community organization, cooperative, BAZ, or other competent local actor should be able to begin with a lightweight map rather than a full administrative stack. A Phase 0 Light exercise may answer only:

1. what are the main local habitation relationships;
2. who can lawfully terminate, evict, condemn, allocate, or relocate;
3. where people currently lose housing through coordination failure;
4. what minimum emergency and durable-habitation routes already exist;
5. which other public or GGF processes currently say “protect housing” without a receiving authority;
6. how residents can challenge an error or imminent loss.

Phase 0 Light creates learning and an interface-gap register. It creates no eviction, allocation, rent, inspection, title, or funding power.

- owner occupation;

Without yet adopting every possible housing reform, identify:

- minimum habitability;
- procedural protections against arbitrary loss;
- emergency accommodation route;
- correction and interim-relief route;
- accessibility requirements;
- protected continuity during other public processes.

Patch the most urgent existing GGF and public processes so that “protect housing” becomes an owned referral with a responsible authority and record.

Absence of an owner is not permission for the originating framework or Housing to invent one.

# 19. Framework interfaces

## 19.1 Constitutional Interface Specification

CIS supplies legitimacy, jurisdiction, sovereignty, observation, information classes, data-purpose, challenge, remedy, correction, and cross-framework authority rules. Housing does not locally redefine those states.

Housing occupancy and claimant records inherit CIS Class C/D/E boundaries as applicable. Recognition records inherit CIS §10.4 rather than a Housing-specific substitute. When responsibility for a housing consequence or purpose-appropriate record spans or is disputed between frameworks, the relevant functions are allocated through CIS §18.1.

CIS §18.1 allocates framework/domain responsibility; it does not adjudicate competing resident claims to the same dwelling or decide whether a customary/community termination is substantively valid. Those questions require the competent legal authority recorded through `HAB-26` or `HAB-27`.

If no ordinary receiving actor accepts a continuity duty, `HAB-28` routes it to an already constituted fallback continuity-custody function where one exists. If no competent authority or custodian exists, that is an interface/constitutional gap, not a licence for Housing or the initiating framework to self-authorize.

## 19.2 GMEAIA

Housing pilots, adoptions, implementation actions, refusals, deferred actions, capacity limitations, constitutional gaps, and closure use the common lifecycle grammar rather than housing-specific substitutes.

## 19.3 Hearthstone Protocol

Hearthstone owns title conversion, stewardship transition, rematriation, trust constitution, conveyance, and property succession.

Housing owns the resident-facing habitation consequences of those processes: notice, tenancy/occupancy continuity, relocation, household claims, accessibility, belongings, service continuity, and unresolved duties.

Neither title transition nor stewardship purpose silently extinguishes a habitation interest.

## 19.4 Bioregional Polis

Bioregional Polis owns municipal land-use strategy, CLTs, LVT, anti-speculation, urban development, and related place-based policy.

Housing owns tenancy, allocation, habitability, homelessness, and resident continuity within the resulting built environment.

The two jointly meet at housing-needs assessment, affordable-housing production, accessibility, redevelopment, displacement, and CLT resident governance.

## 19.5 Soil Health / Living Land

Soil and land frameworks govern ecological restoration, rural land, customary tenure, and productive-land systems within their remit.

Housing supplies the secure-habitation interface where land action affects homes, farmworker housing, customary residence, or rural settlements.

## 19.6 Disaster Risk Reduction

DRR owns emergency shelter, evacuation interfaces, disaster command, and recovery coordination.

Housing owns the transition from emergency shelter or displacement into ordinary secure habitation and the protection of pre-existing habitation interests during recovery. Where managed retreat or permanent hazard makes physical return impossible, Housing governs the resident-facing replacement-habitation and continuity route while DRR retains emergency/hazard authority.

Emergency absence does not automatically prove abandonment.

## 19.7 Migration and Human Mobility

Migration owns status, reception, mobility, displacement, and migration-specific governance.

Housing supplies ordinary habitability, allocation, resident rights, privacy, and continuity standards for accommodation once those standards are legally applicable. Housing status does not decide migration status.

## 19.8 Adaptive Tax

Adaptive Tax owns fiscal liability, collection, and tax remedies.

Housing defines the receiving continuity route where tax administration could foreseeably cause loss of a primary home. A housing-vulnerability finding does not itself alter tax liability.

## 19.9 Shared Capital and Social Inheritance

Shared Capital owns pooled capital, funds, social inheritance, and beneficiary capital claims.

Housing owns resident relationships where shared-capital institutions own or finance housing. Beneficiary or investment objectives cannot silently override resident rights.

## 19.10 Financial Systems and future Monetary/Credit architecture

Financial Systems supplies applicable settlement and financial infrastructure. A future monetary/credit framework would own bank and mortgage-system architecture.

Housing owns only the home-loss and continuity interface when financial claims touch primary habitation.

## 19.11 Work in Liberation / Sundown / Aegis

Employment and transition frameworks must route foreseeable housing loss—especially tied housing, industry closure, relocation, pension/community transition, or regional economic collapse—through a housing continuity process rather than treating “housing” as an unowned checklist item.

## 19.12 Disability, health, family, justice, and social-equity frameworks

Housing imports applicable rights and support requirements. It should not attempt to adjudicate disability status, health diagnosis, family custody, discrimination law, domestic-violence responsibility, criminal responsibility, treatment, restraint, or custody itself.

Where a care, disability, health, education, family, justice, or custodial institution also controls accommodation, `HAB-22` separates the legal sources and review routes for each power. A care/custody objective cannot silently become a housing sanction, and a housing rule cannot manufacture non-housing authority.

## 19.13 Water, sanitation, energy, and infrastructure

Housing depends on essential services but does not govern their technical systems. Service frameworks should preserve minimums and recognize housing consequences; Housing governs the resident-facing habitability and continuity implications.

## 19.14 Kintsugi, Peace, Hearthlight, and health/care systems

**Kintsugi** may provide survivor support, material assistance, shelter, property/housing repair, sanctuary, release/reintegration support, and root-cause funding. Housing receives the ordinary secure-habitation handoff. Refusal of mediation, reconciliation, testimony, police involvement, or another restorative process cannot become a housing exclusion rule merely because Kintsugi is involved.

**Peace and Conflict Resolution** may coordinate return, reintegration, reconstruction, reparative processes, and peace dividends. Housing remains an unconditional civil/material interest unless an independently valid housing rule says otherwise; reconciliation is not a substitute for a housing allocation or continuity decision.

**Hearthlight** may address child/family stabilization and poverty-linked risks. Where housing insecurity is contributing to threatened family separation or child harm, Housing owns the habitation and homelessness-prevention route while Hearthlight retains child-rights and family-support functions. The exact corpus clause should be pinned before a controlled release.

**Global Health and other health/care systems** may create discharge, isolation, recovery, disability-support, home-care, or emergency-continuity situations that affect residence. They retain clinical/public-health authority; Housing governs ordinary resident rights and continuity once housing action is required.

Where an affected person is a secondary household resident rather than the formal tenancy/title holder, `HAB-21` should make that habitation interest and any safety constraints visible without deciding family-law or domestic-violence questions inside Housing.

## 19.15 Regenerative Journeys and short-term/tourism accommodation

Regenerative Journeys may govern tourism demand, tourism levies, tourism suspension, host/community tourism policy, and tourism-linked housing-protection funding within its lawful mandate. Housing owns the resident-facing continuity consequences when ordinary dwellings move into or out of short-term/tourism use or when tourism policy materially affects housing access.

`HAB-23` is the interface object. A tourism or platform indicator does not itself evict a resident, cancel a tenancy, or authorize conversion. Housing does not acquire tourism-licensing or platform-regulation jurisdiction merely because short-term letting affects supply.

Data exchange between housing and tourism/platform systems is purpose-limited under CIS and `HAB-04`.

## 19.16 Housing-systems seam counterparties

The provisional chapters at §§9, 12, and 13 are not ownerless policy space. They are interfaces whose counterparties should be explicit:

| Housing concern | Primary external owner(s) | Housing contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Affordability, disposable income, arrears pressure | Adaptive Tax; AUBI/social protection; Work in Liberation; competent rent authority | Defines resident-facing burden, continuity, and distributional consequences; does not choose fiscal or labour instruments |
| Land, density, location, development pattern, CLTs | Bioregional Polis; Hearthstone where title/stewardship changes | Defines housing need, accessibility, displacement, and resident continuity; does not acquire zoning/title power |
| Short-term/tourism conversion | Regenerative Journeys; competent local land-use/platform/tourism authority | Governs existing-resident consequences and `HAB-23`; does not license platforms or tourism |
| Physical delivery, utilities, materials, construction capacity | Infrastructure/materials/building authorities | Defines habitability, accessibility, and need; does not command construction by implication |
| Mortgage, credit, foreclosure machinery | Financial Systems and any future Monetary/Credit architecture; insolvency/property law | Governs home-loss visibility, resident claims, notice, challenge, and continuity |
| Tax enforcement or fiscal incentives affecting dwellings | Adaptive Tax | Receives/defines housing continuity consequences; does not alter liability by itself |
| Housing assets held by funds/trusts | Shared Capital; Hearthstone as applicable | Protects resident interests while external frameworks govern capital/title |

A supply-side or finance-side instrument may legitimately change prices, incentives, availability, or risk. Those effects are not automatically jurisdictional overrides. The conformance question is whether the competent owner sees and lawfully handles the resulting habitation consequences.

Where an external counterparty also acts as a **habitation gatekeeper**, its substantive decision remains in its own domain. `HAB-25` records the basis/dependency and gatekeeper role; `HAB-16` carries the cross-framework consequence; `HAB-29` records any lawful change of habitation status. These records do not grant Housing appellate authority over the external decision.

# 20. Existing-corpus migration questions

This section is intentionally provisional until a corpus audit pins exact clauses and versions.

The first migration pass should identify every place where another GGF document currently says, in substance:

- protect housing;
- preserve housing continuity;
- prevent housing collapse;
- provide relocation housing;
- protect tenants;
- provide affordable housing;
- provide shelter after transition;
- do not extinguish occupants;
- account for housing in compensation or continuity.

Each instance should be classified as one of:

1. **conformant handoff already owned elsewhere**;
2. **needs Housing interface reference**;
3. **contains substantive housing policy to migrate or jointly own**;
4. **contains an automatic or self-authorizing housing consequence that must be removed**;
5. **is merely illustrative language and needs no migration**.

Priority audit candidates from the housing-gap review include Bioregional Polis, Hearthstone, Disaster Risk Reduction, Migration and Human Mobility, Soil/Living Land, Sundown, Aegis, Adaptive Tax, Water & Sanitation, **Kintsugi, Peace and Conflict Resolution, Hearthlight, Global Health, Regenerative Journeys**, and frameworks carrying essential-service continuity lists.

The first pass should distinguish two especially important handoff shapes:

- **support conditionality:** housing or shelter is mentioned inside a care, peace, family, migration, or restorative process and must not become contingent on reconciliation, testimony, compliance, or unrelated participation;
- **continuity after a causal intervention:** another framework lawfully causes closure, return, relocation, transition, remediation, discharge, or asset change and must route the housing consequence to `HAB-16` rather than treating housing as an externality.

# 21. Falsification and failure gates

This framework should not survive merely because housing language sounds humane.

A later release should fail or substantially revise itself if evidence shows that it:

1. duplicates land/title governance rather than filling the habitation gap;
2. creates procedural rights that are unusable in practice because residents lack access, time, representation, or alternative shelter;
3. prevents legitimate repair, redevelopment, safety action, or housing supply to such an extent that overall housing insecurity worsens;
4. protects formal tenants while excluding informal, customary, family, migrant, disability-support, or other real residence relationships;
5. encourages landlords/providers to avoid formalizing housing relationships in order to escape duties;
6. creates rent or allocation rules that substantially worsen scarcity, exclusion, segregation, or informalization without compensating benefits;
7. treats homelessness as solved by temporary shelter while durable housing outcomes deteriorate;
8. centralizes housing authority beyond what local legal and institutional diversity can support;
9. produces extensive sensitive registries that become surveillance, migration, credit, policing, or political-control infrastructure;
10. turns “right to housing” rhetoric into claims no institution has capacity or legal authority to honour;
11. makes housing continuity so absolute that necessary ecological restoration, Indigenous rematriation, safety closure, or public infrastructure becomes impossible rather than lawfully balanced;
12. leaves the same cross-framework housing promises unowned after adoption;
13. prevents arbitrary eviction while allowing opaque or discriminatory automated screening to exclude the same people before entry;
14. lets owner insolvency, receivership, or creditor sale silently extinguish habitation interests that applicable law would otherwise protect;
15. allows deliberate deferred maintenance or capital stripping to convert a landlord/provider's own breach into a safety-based route for resident removal without continuity responsibility;
16. either anchors residents to permanently unsafe geography or treats managed retreat as permission to erase their replacement-habitation claims;
17. protects only the named leaseholder/title holder while leaving ordinary secondary household residents in a procedural void, or conversely turns factual household residence into automatic co-tenancy that undermines safety or competing rights;
18. lets care, treatment, disability-support, education, or custody providers use accommodation control as an unreviewed disciplinary or dependency lever;
19. lets short-term/tourism conversion bypass ordinary housing termination, continuity, or data-purpose rules;
20. waits for a declared disaster before acting on credible slow-onset habitability degradation, or lets a hazard indicator become an automatic relocation order;
21. permits forced relocation to normalize durable replacement housing below the jurisdiction's own minimum floor;
22. lets housing registries become default immigration, tax, criminal, intelligence, political, credit, or generalized social-governance infrastructure without independently lawful purpose and CIS safeguards;
23. requires such administratively heavy records that low-capacity jurisdictions lose the ability to provide timely housing protection;
24. records an unresolved housing duty but lets it disappear because no single framework initially owns it, rather than routing disputed responsibility or preserving the constitutional gap.
25. forces a multi-basis habitation position into one presumed superior relationship, causing an independent legal, customary, family, care, employment, membership, allocation, or other basis to disappear;
26. treats a gatekeeper's non-housing decision as if it automatically executed eviction or allocation where applicable law requires a separate housing act;
27. lets loss of one habitation basis silently extinguish another independent basis held by the same claimant without competent adjudication;
28. treats loss of a place-specific dwelling as automatically extinguishing a separately constituted duty to accommodate the person elsewhere, or treats a general accommodation entitlement as an automatic right to remain in that dwelling;
29. breaks continuity during lawful succession or regularization because the new habitation status is opened as an unrelated claimant/case.

# 23. Proposed next drafting sequence

Before consolidating this v0.1.4 into a fuller framework:

1. **Corpus boundary audit:** verify every claimed interface and identify housing mechanisms already present elsewhere.
2. **Comparative legal audit:** sample several very different housing systems to test whether the secure-habitation primitive travels across common-law tenancy, civil-law tenancy, social-housing systems, informal settlements, customary tenure, cooperative housing, and Indigenous law.
3. **Failure-case audit:** test applicant screening/algorithmic exclusion, eviction, private-landlord insolvency, creditor sale, constructive eviction through deferred maintenance, unsafe building, rent shock, factory closure, disaster absence, managed retreat, rematriation, mortgage foreclosure, domestic violence, secondary-household exclusion, residential-care expulsion, short-term/tourism conversion, slow-onset climate degradation, institutional discharge, and homelessness discharge.
4. **Authority audit:** determine whether each `HAB-*` record has a lawful owner and whether any record accidentally creates power.
5. **Scope decision:** decide whether housing supply and social housing remain inside this framework or become stronger interfaces to Bioregional Polis / infrastructure.
6. **Multi-basis adversarial audit:** test cases where one person's habitation depends simultaneously on two or more independently governed bases or gatekeepers, especially care+licence, employment+housing, membership+tenancy, customary+statutory, migration+service housing, and family/court-order combinations.
7. **Status-transition audit:** test death/succession, separation, assignment, regularization, derived-to-direct tenancy, temporary-to-durable allocation, and care-placement changes for causal continuity through `HAB-29`.
8. **Fallback-duty audit:** test whether `HAB-28` can preserve continuity without manufacturing municipal, state, customary, or Housing jurisdiction.
9. **Then consolidate:** if these object-model tests do not reveal another missing primitive, treat v0.2 as a consolidation/pruning/schema-readiness release rather than another expansion round.

> **Shared rules for the relationship between people and the places they live: secure habitation, tenancy, household and derived residence, multiple habitation bases, external gatekeepers, habitability, eviction, homelessness prevention, housing allocation, competing claims, lawful status transitions, and continuity of home across economic, ecological, care, employment, membership, tourism, customary, family, and institutional change. # 25. Working conclusion

The GGF has repeatedly promised that taxation, transition, disaster response, land reform, remediation, and institutional change will not casually destroy people's housing.

That promise needs an owner.

The Housing & Secure Habitation Framework begins from a narrow proposition:

> **A person can have a constitutionally significant relationship to a home without owning the land or dwelling, and governance must be able to represent that relationship explicitly.**

The framework therefore sits between property and personhood without collapsing into either. It does not decide who should own all housing, what every rent should be, or which global housing model is correct. It makes factual, household, derived, community, multi-basis, place-specific, non-place-specific, and competing habitation positions visible; identifies gatekeepers without absorbing their domains; assigns consequential housing acts to lawful authorities; creates continuity and challenge routes; and gives the rest of the GGF somewhere real to hand off the promises it already makes.

Where no actor can presently discharge a habitation duty, v0.1.4 keeps the duty and the gap visible without pretending that documentation is equivalent to capacity or that Housing has acquired residual sovereignty. Where several independently governed bases converge on one home, it preserves the graph without pretending that representation decides legal priority.

That remains enough for the v0.1 series. The remaining policy design should be earned through comparative legal testing and adversarial review rather than filled in merely because the section headings exist.
