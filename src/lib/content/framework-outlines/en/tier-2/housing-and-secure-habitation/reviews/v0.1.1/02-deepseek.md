This is a rigorous and unusually honest working draft. It correctly identifies the constitutional gap—the resident–dwelling relationship distinct from title, land-use, and emergency shelter—and builds architecture around that object rather than around a preferred policy model. The adversarial hardening from v0.1 to v0.1.1 visibly improved the entry, insolvency, constructive-eviction, and replacement-habitation seams.

Below I’ve organised my input as you requested: overlooked aspects, answers to selected open questions, and broader insights.

---

## What may have been overlooked

### 1. Short-term, transient, and tourist accommodation as a housing-system disruptor
The draft asks about short-term rentals in open question 24, but the main text barely touches the governance machinery needed when a dwelling flips between housing and hospitality. This is not merely a Bioregional Polis zoning matter; it creates a *habitation-status churn* that the framework should be able to represent. A unit that is a home in February, a hotel room in July, and vacant in November confuses every `HAB-` record. A future revision should explicitly model:
- When a dwelling ceases to be habitation and becomes a commercial short-term letting;
- Minimum notice and relocation duties if a tenant is displaced for that conversion;
- Data interfaces between housing registries and platform/tourism regulators without turning the housing registry into a surveillance tool.

### 2. The “non-leaseholder household member” problem
Many people live in homes without being named on a lease, title, or allocation record: partners, adult children, elders, caregivers, friends. The draft acknowledges informal occupation broadly, but the specific vulnerability of *intra-household* secondary occupants—who can be evicted by the primary leaseholder or excluded by a landlord’s household-composition rules—needs a clearer seat at the table. Without it, domestic-violence protections and family separation risks remain awkwardly stitched from outside. A specific sub-interest or minimum procedural shield for de facto household members who do not hold the formal habitation interest would close a common rights gap.

### 3. Housing-as-care infrastructure and the blurred boundary with institutional settings
The draft lists supported living and institutional accommodation in scope but does not yet grapple with the governance seam where housing, care, and confinement merge. Many people live in settings where a care-provider, not a landlord, controls entry, exit, visitors, and daily life. The risk is that a “housing” framework accidentally normalises a care regime as mere accommodation. The framework should require that any institution claiming to provide both housing and care identify which powers are housing-powers (subject to `HAB-` protections) and which are care- or custody-powers (subject to health, disability, or justice frameworks). This prevents a care home from calling an expulsion a “housing management decision” without review.

### 4. The communal and collective dimension beyond legal recognition
Section 4 lists collective residences and intentional communities, and section 7.4 nods to tenant organisation. Still, the draft treats the resident largely as an individual or nuclear household claimant. Many cultures and functioning housing systems embed the right to dwell in family, kinship, neighbourhood, or village-level relationships that are not formal cooperatives. The framework may need a placeholder for *community-based habitation interests* that are not reducible to individual tenancies, with the community itself holding the interface role—much as Indigenous nations do, but for other collective tenure forms.

### 5. Housing and climate adaptation beyond disaster
Disaster Risk Reduction gets a cross-framework handoff, but gradual climate impacts—heat stress, recurrent flooding, water scarcity—will reshape habitability without a declared emergency. The framework should contemplate a *slow-onset habitability degradation* interface. This might include triggers for proactive relocation planning, habitability reassessment protocols, and replacement-habitation funds that accrue before homes become uninhabitable, rather than waiting for a DRR declaration.

### 6. The risk of a two-tier habitability standard through “emergency” loopholes
Section 8.6 acknowledges that physical return may become impossible, but the draft does not yet clearly state that the replacement-habitation floor should not degrade permanently into a lower standard merely because the transition was forced. Without that, managed retreat, condemnation, or redevelopment can permanently shift people into materially worse housing under the guise of “continuity.” Some normative anchor—such as “replacement habitation shall not, over the long term, be less adequate than the minimum floor defined under §6”—would prevent a slow downwards ratchet.

### 7. The enabling role of housing data for wider social governance
The framework warns correctly that housing registries could become surveillance infrastructure. It may be necessary to add a positive prohibition: data collected for habitation continuity, allocation, or habitability purposes shall not be repurposed for immigration enforcement, tax assessment, criminal investigation, or political policing absent an independently authorised legal gateway. Many jurisdictions will feel pressure to merge housing registries with other civil databases. A strong firewall is not a technical detail; it’s a constitutional safeguard.

## Answers to selected open questions

I won’t tackle all 44, but I’ll group the most consequential and offer defensible positions the drafters could adopt or test.

**Q1. Naming: Housing & Secure Habitation Framework vs. Secure Habitation Protocol.**  
Keep the joint name for discoverability in policy and advocacy contexts, but elevate “secure habitation” to the primary legal object in all constitutional interfaces. Internally, you could refer to it simply as the Secure Habitation Framework, with “Housing” retained as a public-facing subtitle.

**Q2. Tier placement.**  
Tier 2 is correct for the substantive housing policy architecture. However, the *protected continuity interface*—the requirement that other frameworks hand off housing consequences—should be expressed as a Tier 1 duty in the Constitutional Interface Specification. That is, the CIS should say: “Any framework action foreseeably affecting primary habitation must create a HAB-16 handoff.” Then Housing remains Tier 2 but the obligation to use it is constitutional.

**Q4. “Secure-habitation interest”—too vague?**  
It is appropriately broad as a canopy concept. The legal test is whether it can structure specific records, processes, and remedies. The `HAB-03` record, with its basis, duration, limits, and challenge route, does that work. The term is no vaguer than “property interest” in common law, which likewise takes its colour from context. The draft’s taxonomy of relationship types (§4) sufficiently bounds it.

**Q6. Minimum process for factual residence without title (section 3.11).**  
The most portable threshold: a person can show *more than transient presence* and *use of the place as ordinary living quarters* through any combination of testimony, utility receipts, witness statements, photographs, or administrative records. Minimum protections should include (a) a reasonable notice period before removal, (b) an opportunity to be heard before an independent official unless immediate danger is credibly shown, (c) safe custody of personal documents, medicines, and assistive devices, and (d) a referral to emergency shelter or replacement accommodation. This does not create a tenancy; it creates a procedural floor that even trespass law can accommodate.

**Q9. Universal anti-self-help eviction rule?**  
Yes, it is defensible as a baseline: no person shall be physically expelled from their ordinary dwelling, or deprived of essential services to force departure, except through a lawfully authorised process. Many legal systems already recognise this principle; making it explicit prevents a race to the bottom. Exceptions can be tightly drawn for imminent threats to safety or property.

**Q12. Energy access as habitability.**  
Energy should be a contextual requirement: where heating or cooling is necessary to prevent death, illness, or the dwelling becoming uninhabitable, energy access is part of the minimum floor. In temperate climates without such risk, it may be a quality-of-life standard beyond the floor. The framework should require jurisdictions to make a reasoned determination rather than imposing one global rule.

**Q15. Tied/employer housing.**  
Loss of employment should never trigger immediate loss of housing without a continuity process. The employer-as-landlord situation requires a mandatory notice period, a clear separation between employment dismissal and eviction procedures, and a duty on the employer (or a public backstop) to provide transitional housing or relocation assistance. The framework should treat employer housing as a high-risk category with additional safeguards.

**Q28. Primary residence vs. investment property under tax/insolvency.**  
Yes, the framework should advocate a distinction: primary-residence habitation interests deserve stronger continuity protections than investment properties. In tax enforcement, sale of a primary residence should be a last resort with a mandatory affordability/replacement assessment. In insolvency, a homestead exemption or equivalent should be the expected baseline, with forced sale only where no other reasonable satisfaction of creditors exists and replacement housing is assured.

**Q29. Which habitation interests survive owner insolvency (section 13.1)?**  
As a minimum, all legally recognised tenancies, registered cooperative interests, and formalised social-housing allocations should survive transfer unless a competent tribunal orders otherwise. Informal but long-standing occupants should at least receive the §3.11 procedural floor and a relocation duty on the administrator before vacant possession is granted. The framework cannot rewrite secured-creditor priority, but it can require that administrators treat residents as more than trespassers by default.

**Q31. Elements of a universal replacement-habitation claim (section 8.6).**  
A universal core: (a) timely replacement accommodation that meets the §6 minimum floor, (b) preservation of household composition (not forced family splitting), (c) continuity of essential support services (disability, health, education) where geographically feasible, (d) transportation or relocation-cost coverage, and (e) a record of any outstanding claim for additional compensation or return priority. Everything else is jurisdiction-specific.

**Q40. When to split into Secure Habitation Protocol + Housing Systems Framework.**  
Split if and when the affordability, supply, and social-housing sections (§9, §12, §13) begin to carry substantive rule-making authority that diverges from the resident-continuity core. The natural seam: a narrow *Secure Habitation Protocol* owns the resident–dwelling relationship, habitability, eviction, homelessness prevention, and cross-framework continuity handoffs. A separate *Housing Systems Framework* owns supply, affordability instruments, public housing allocation design, and finance interfaces. This separation would mirror the CIS/GMEAIA split between constitutional process and substantive systems.

**Q44. First explicit HAB-16 migrations.**  
Begin with the frameworks whose promises are currently most consequential and least owned:
- Hearthstone: any clause implying “property transition will not displace residents”;
- Disaster Risk Reduction: “emergency shelter to durable housing” promises;
- Sundown/Aegis: worker and community housing continuity after industry closure or base conversion;
- Kintsugi: any housing or shelter provision attached to survivor support that could be misread as conditional on participation;
- Peace and Conflict Resolution: return, restitution, and reconstruction housing commitments;
- Adaptive Tax: enforcement against primary residences.
A small migration team should audit those five to seven instruments and propose exact clause amendments within one drafting cycle.

## Additional insights

### The “housing-systems seam” is the most fragile design choice—lean into it deliberately
Sections 9, 12, and 13 are labelled provisional for good reason. Many housing frameworks collapse because they try to simultaneously govern resident rights and fix supply, affordability, and finance. Yours avoids that mistake, but the seam risks being a permanent grey zone. I recommend that v0.2 explicitly define the seam as a governed interface: Housing writes the *demand-side* habitation quality and continuity standards; the *supply-side* instruments (land, construction, credit, tax) must meet those standards without being dictated by Housing. This creates accountability without conflation.

### The Phase 0 mapping is the framework’s true implementation engine
The most powerful part of this draft is that a jurisdiction can begin without new legislation, simply by mapping existing relationships, authorities, and continuity failures. Consider adding a “Phase 0 light” checklist for municipalities or community groups, because the global-to-local translation will depend on it. Without that, the framework risks being a shelf document for national ministries only.

### The framework needs a “failure of last resort” principle
If every responsible authority fails—landlord disappears, provider insolvent, public body bankrupt, disaster recovery incomplete—who has the residual duty? The draft currently implies that an unresolved duty record (`HAB-18`) endures, but it doesn’t say where the buck stops. A future version should articulate a subsidiary obligation, perhaps defaulting to the most local competent government level, to provide emergency accommodation and a route back into durable housing. Otherwise the “continuity” promise has no floor when all other actors vanish.

### Watch for the “perfect record” trap
The `HAB-` records are well-conceived, but in high-volume, low-capacity environments, requiring a full record for every housing interaction could become a barrier rather than a protection. Consider a tiered approach: a core set of records mandatory for any system (e.g., HAB-10 eviction decision, HAB-16 handoff), and an extended set recommended where administrative capacity permits. This aligns with the framework’s anti-utopian posture.

### Legal pluralism is treated as an exception; it should be a design principle
The draft acknowledges Indigenous and customary law, but the overall architecture still assumes a state or state-like constituted authority. Many housing systems operate under overlapping or contested authorities. The framework might need an explicit pluralism module: when multiple authorities claim jurisdiction over habitation, the framework provides conflict-of-laws rules that default to the authority closest to the affected community and most protective of continuity, without extinguishing competing claims.

### The falsification gates are excellent—make them operational
Section 21’s failure criteria are unusually self-critical. To make them real, assign each gate a metric or case-type that would trigger a review. For example, gate 13 (automated screening replacing eviction) could be operationalised as: “If the rate of housing-entry denial via automated systems exceeds the eviction rate in a given jurisdiction, trigger a review of whether exclusion has simply been moved upstream.” Embedding such triggers makes the framework genuinely falsifiable rather than aspirationally self-critical.

---

This draft is already ahead of most global governance instruments in its clarity about what it does *not* do. The next draft will benefit most from real-world Phase 0 mapping exercises in two or three radically different housing systems, because that will reveal whether the `HAB-` records and continuity handoffs survive contact with lived complexity. You are right to earn the remaining design through adversarial review.
