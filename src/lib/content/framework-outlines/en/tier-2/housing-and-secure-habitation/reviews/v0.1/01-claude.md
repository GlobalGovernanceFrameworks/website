Gemini's four findings are all sound and all confirmable in the draft — §15.3 does cover automated eviction and not automated denial of entry; §11 does cover public and cooperative provider failure but not private landlord liquidation; §8.5 does block service-withdrawal eviction but not deferred-maintenance condemnation; and the managed-retreat tension in §19.6 is real. I'd add the entry-gap point is the most consequential of the four, because tenant screening is where housing discrimination has actually migrated.

Here's what I can add from the corpus that neither of us could see without it.

## §14's trigger list is missing two frameworks that make housing promises

The Housing Continuity Handoff in §14 is the single most valuable mechanism in this draft. It converts the 133 unbacked "must protect housing" deferrals I found into an actual handoff with a named recipient. That's the whole point of the audit, discharged.

But the candidate trigger list was built from reasoning rather than from the corpus, and it misses the second-densest housing document in the entire GGF.

**Kintsugi Protocol** carries 12 housing mentions — more than Bioregional Polis, more than DRR, second only to Soil Health. It's the collective-care, survivor-sovereignty and restorative-accountability framework, and its housing content is about release and reintegration: *"condition of safety, services, legal protection, housing, benefits, release, employment, education, migration"* and *"property and housing repair"* after harm. That's a housing-continuity trigger of exactly the kind §14 exists for — someone leaving custody, a survivor whose home was damaged, a person whose housing depended on a relationship that harm ended — and it isn't on the list.

**Peace and Conflict Resolution** has 6, including a line that reads like it was written for this framework: *"people are pressured to reconcile before receiving housing | Housing remains unconditional; reconciliation is not a precondition."* A framework already policing housing-as-leverage should be a named handoff partner.

Two more worth considering: **Hearthlight**, whose CPS Shield mobilises rental assistance to defeat poverty-based child removal — that's homelessness prevention under §10.2 operating in a different framework — and **Global Health**, at 4 mentions.

## The zero-hit functions are the ones the draft fills

Worth recording, because it's the falsification check on my audit. These returned literally zero hits across all 77 documents: security of tenure, social/public/council housing, housing allocation and waiting lists, housing supply and construction and stock, habitability, emergency accommodation.

The draft covers every one — §6 habitability floor, §5.4 allocation, §11 public and social housing, §12 supply. So the object it constructs maps onto the hole rather than onto some adjacent thing. That's a good sign, and it's the strongest argument that this is a real framework rather than a restatement of Bioregional Polis.

## On tier — I'd push back on Gemini, gently

Tier 2 is defensible but the comparators cut both ways. Bioregional Polis is Tier 3, Foundation Protocol Tier 3, Migration Tier 3, DRR Tier 2, Hearthstone Tier 2, Mental Health Tier 2. On substance, Tier 2 fits.

But the argument for this framework's existence is that twenty-eight documents defer to it as a *protected continuity interest* — the same structural role AUBI plays at Tier 1. Frameworks other frameworks make promises about tend to sit higher than frameworks that apply constitutional rights to a domain. The question isn't "how foundational is housing conceptually" but "does a Tier 2 document have the standing to be the thing Adaptive Tax, Aegis, Hearthstone and Soil Health are all deferring to." I don't have a firm answer, but Gemini's reasoning — that it's an application rather than base OS — doesn't engage that, and it's the reason the framework was written.

## Two things the draft is missing that its siblings all have

**No source freeze and no version history.** Adaptive Tax, Shared Capital, CIS 0.2.3 and Polycentric 0.6.1 all carry digest tables and version-history entries. This one carries neither, and it's the document making the most claims about what other frameworks currently say. If you want them, the relevant digests are in my earlier messages.

**The §1.2 seam runs through the substantive chapters.** §1.2 says housing systems and secure habitation belong together and flags a future split. But §1.4 disclaims land use, building regulation, mortgage regulation, tax and competition — and then §9 (affordability and rent-setting), §12 (supply and construction) and §13 (housing finance) all sit on the housing-systems side of that line. Those are also the three most jurisdiction-specific chapters, and therefore the ones most likely to end up either vacuous or presumptuous.

I'd not split them now — §1.2's argument that units-without-residents and rights-without-dwellings both fail is correct. But I'd name where the seam actually falls: it's at §9, §12 and §13, not at some abstract future boundary. Saying so makes the eventual split cheap and tells a reader which chapters to treat as most provisional.
