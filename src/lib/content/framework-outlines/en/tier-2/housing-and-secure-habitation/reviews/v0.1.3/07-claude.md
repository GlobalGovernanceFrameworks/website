**On the DeepSeek audit**

The comparative range is real — German *Untermiete*, English lodger-versus-subtenant, civil-law *usufruit*, customary West African and Melanesian banishment, Bosnia and Colombia restitution. That's genuine breadth and it's mostly accurate.

But every verdict is positive. "Works." "Holds." "Survives without needing structural change." "High portability" on four of five components. For a document that was three revisions old at the time, that's a suspiciously clean result — and I think it's prompt-shaped rather than document-shaped. You asked "can you do a comparative-law object-model audit," which is neutral; Gemini was asked to break the object and find falsifying cases, and found real ones. A clean pass under a neutral prompt isn't evidence of robustness. It's evidence that nothing pushed.

Two things it got wrong or under-weighted, and one it didn't test.

**The lodger routing is a real ambiguity it surfaced without noticing.** On English lodgers it says §3.11's factual-residence minimum process would still require notice and belongings protection. But §2.10 explicitly names "lodger, licensee from a tenant" as *derived* interests, while §3.11 is for people with no formal interest at all. A lodger with an express licence has a formal interest — a weak one, derived from the tenant. DeepSeek routed it to §3.11 because English law gives excluded licensees near-zero protection, which conflates *weak protection under local law* with *no formal interest*. That distinction is the framework's central claim. As written, a lodger can enter through either door and the two doors carry different process. §2.10 or §3.11 needs a sentence saying which governs when both could apply — probably that a derived interest, however weak, is governed by §2.10 and §7.8, and §3.11 is the floor only where no constituting relationship exists at all.

**The succession bridge is the one real hole it found, and it downgraded it to guidance.** There's no mechanism converting a `HAB-21` secondary resident into a `HAB-03` holder when law grants succession. DeepSeek notes this, then suggests "a status-change event record or simply note in guidance." But death of a parent or partner is the single most common route by which a secondary household resident becomes — or fails to become — a primary interest holder. Without a status-change mechanism the framework can't represent that as a transition at all; it can only represent an ending and an unrelated beginning, which means the continuity it exists to protect is precisely what gets lost in the records. That's a record, not guidance.

**What it didn't test: two-parent derivation.** §2.10 defines a derived interest as depending on *another habitation interest*, singular. Tied housing has two superior relationships of different kinds — the employer's interest in the dwelling, and the employment contract, which is the actual termination trigger. §7.8's field list has "the superior habitation interest" in the singular but then says "another habitation holder, provider, employer, institution, or contractual chain," which quietly admits the non-habitation parent without modelling it.

The same shape appears in supported living: a resident's interest derives from the care provider's interest in the building *and* from the care eligibility, and the care eligibility is what ends. That chain crosses the §7.7 housing/care power separation, so the power-separation map has to hold across a derivation — which nobody has tested. DeepSeek tested chains only in ordinary rental settings, where there's one parent.

**One corpus note**

Its identity-light section is the strongest part of the audit, and it lands on the same dependency as before: `HAB-03` and §3.11 both work around the absence of civil registration, and civil registration has no owner anywhere in the GGF. The framework is now handling that absence well enough that it's arguably better to leave it — a provisional claimant identifier that explicitly refuses to become citizenship or tax residence is a good design, and it would be worse if it deferred to a register that didn't exist. But it's worth recording in the migration questions that Housing is currently the only framework in the corpus that has confronted the identity gap, which makes it the de facto precedent if that object is ever built.
