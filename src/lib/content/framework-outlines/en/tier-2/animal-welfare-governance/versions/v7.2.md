### Version 7.2 change summary

- Preserves **“guardianship, not ownership”** while distinguishing enduring moral commitments from revisable policies and technology pathways.
- Replaces automatic CAFO-to-pasture and fixed slaughter phase-out requirements with **evidence-gated transitions**. The former 2030/2035/2040 numerical targets are retained in the revision history as **unvalidated scenarios**, not binding commitments.
- Defines **animal welfare outcomes**, including severe suffering, positive welfare, lifetime experience, species substitution, and displacement of harm, as primary evaluative concerns.
- Establishes a **Just Transition and Food Security Protocol**, a distributional harm ledger, and decision rules for pausing, modifying, and replacing interventions.
- Reworks species protection to distinguish **evidence of sentience**, **severity of welfare risk**, and **legal protection** without inferring moral worth from brain complexity.
- Corrects technical specifications concerning pig stunning, dairy-calf care, fish welfare, insect handling, and sentience thresholds; removes unsupported universal methods.
- Requires publicly reviewable evidence, adverse-result reporting, conflict-of-interest safeguards, and technology-neutral verification.
- Separates **individual animal welfare** from **ecosystem health**, including difficult trade-offs involving wild animals.
- Corrects section numbering, identifies illustrative scenarios, and creates an evidence and assumptions register.

## 1. Introduction

The Global Guardian Framework seeks to transform humanity's relationship with other animals through a model of **guardianship, not ownership**. Guardianship means recognizing that animal lives and experiences are not merely inputs to human production, while also acknowledging that humans already participate in relationships of care, dependence, ecological management, and cultural significance with other animals.

The framework establishes a moral presumption against severe, avoidable suffering and deprivation. It also supports opportunities for **positive animal welfare**: health, comfort, social relationships, species-appropriate activity, choice, and other experiences that may make an animal's life go well. Whether breeding, ownership, and killing can be justified even in low-suffering systems remains an explicit ethical question, not one purportedly settled by welfare metrics alone.

The framework combines ambitious protection with **fallibilist governance**: scientific knowledge may change; promising interventions may backfire; human livelihoods and food security matter; and different ethical commitments may generate real conflicts. The response to uncertainty is neither paralysis nor rigid implementation. It is proportionate precaution, transparent comparison of alternatives, and structured learning.

### 1.1 Framework Purpose

The framework aims to:

1. Prevent severe, avoidable harms in animal agriculture, fisheries, research, trade, entertainment, wildlife management, and other human-animal interactions.
2. Expand positive animal welfare where human activity shapes animals' lives.
3. Develop low-harm food and livelihood systems while protecting human nutrition, dignity, and the rights of affected communities.
4. Respect Indigenous sovereignty, Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC 2.0), traditional ecological knowledge, and culturally significant human-animal relationships.
5. Make welfare interventions open to independent verification, correction, and public moral deliberation.
6. Coordinate protections for individual animals with biodiversity, ecosystem health, and One Health objectives without treating them as interchangeable.

### 1.2 Scope and Coverage

**Beings and evidence:** The framework presumptively addresses vertebrates; cephalopod mollusks; decapod crustaceans; insects and other invertebrates where credible evidence warrants concern; and emerging biological or synthetic entities through a separate precautionary review. Protection is proportional to the **credible risk of morally significant harm**, not simply an anatomical hierarchy. Ecologically important organisms without demonstrated sentience remain protected through the Planetary Health framework.

**Activities:** Farmed and companion animals; transport and slaughter; dairy and breeding; capture fisheries and aquaculture; laboratories; wildlife trade; hunting; habitat and wildlife management; animal-related technology; and emergency response.

**Geography:** Global coordination with national, regional, BAZ, local, and Indigenous adaptation. Previously proposed pilot regions (Amazon Basin, European agricultural regions, Southeast Asia) remain candidates, not announced programs.

**Planning horizon:** A staged 2027–2040 design horizon with scheduled reassessments. Historical dates from v7.1 (2025–2026) require a verified status audit before any claim of implementation.

### 1.3 Relationship to Planetary Health Governance

The framework operates as a proposed specialist application layer within the PHC ecosystem. Animal welfare and the Biosphere Health Index (BHI) inform one another but remain **separate evaluative dimensions**. An ecologically beneficial intervention can still impose serious individual suffering; protecting an individual animal can also have ecological trade-offs. Neither dimension automatically cancels the other.

### 1.4 Sentience Research Integration

A proposed Scientific Advisory Panel would conduct annual public reviews and maintain a versioned, publicly accessible sentience evidence registry. A provisional **5% of the Animal Welfare Council research budget** is retained as a **budget proposal**, subject to resource allocation review. The Emerging Sentience Protocol applies when evidence suggests a realistic possibility of experience or welfare-relevant affect. Urgent protective action need not wait for final taxonomic classification.

### 1.5 Edge Cases and Uncertainty

Novel genetic variants, artificial or hybrid biological systems, and entities with uncertain sentience require **case-specific assessments** of welfare risk and ethical implications. No universal neuron-density threshold, fixed six-month scientific verdict, or assumption that detecting pain capability is straightforward is adopted. Precautionary measures can be time-limited and periodically reviewed without implying that the underlying ethical uncertainty disappears on a deadline.

## 2. Core Principles

### 2.1 Evidence-Grounded Compassion and Adaptive Protection

> The Global Guardian Framework recognizes the moral significance of sentient experience and establishes a continuing obligation to prevent severe, avoidable suffering while cultivating opportunities for positive animal welfare. It distinguishes enduring ethical commitments from revisable institutional and technological strategies. Major interventions shall be compared with realistic alternatives, accounting for animal welfare, human dignity and basic needs, cultural and Indigenous rights, ecological consequences, and the distribution of benefits and burdens. Strong evidence of serious, preventable harm warrants timely protection; uncertainty about implementation warrants proportionate precaution and learning, not indefinite inaction. Evidence of failure must trigger correction, without automatically extinguishing the ethical concern that motivated action.

### 2.2 Principles of Application

1. **Sentience-centered consideration.** The capacity for experience, including possible negative and positive affect, is morally relevant. Species membership and neural complexity are not standalone measures of worth. Evidence, uncertainty, and welfare risk are reported separately.
2. **Prevention of severe harm.** Where credible evidence identifies intense, prolonged, or widespread suffering, its continuation requires strong justification and timely risk reduction.
3. **Positive welfare and agency.** Protection extends beyond survival and absence of injury to meaningful opportunities for movement, rest, exploration, social interaction, and animal-relevant choice, as supported by evidence.
4. **Guardianship rather than commodity primacy.** Animals are not treated merely as production units. Duties of care apply across breeding, housing, transport, handling, disease management, and end-of-life decisions.
5. **Protection of essential human interests.** No transition should knowingly impose severe, preventable nutritional deprivation or destitution when reasonable alternatives exist. Human vulnerability and animal suffering both warrant moral consideration.
6. **Distributional justice.** Assess **who** bears costs, not only aggregate gains. Support and remedy must prioritize households and communities facing the gravest burdens.
7. **Indigenous sovereignty and cultural humility.** FPIC 2.0 and legitimate community decision-making are substantive constraints, not ceremonial consultation. Cultural importance merits consideration but is not assumed to justify every form of harm.
8. **Ecological responsibility.** Land, emissions, biodiversity, water, and wild-animal impacts must be assessed alongside farmed-animal welfare; pasture, plant-based production, and technology are not automatically benign.
9. **Adaptive, independent accountability.** Policies should have baseline evidence, preregistered hypotheses, decision gates, adverse-outcome reporting, and legitimate avenues for revision and appeal.
10. **Technological proportionality.** Use the simplest trustworthy tools capable of the task. Blockchain, AI, satellite sensing, and digital identity are optional, independently assessed instruments rather than prerequisites.
11. **Plural ethical inquiry.** Reducing suffering, avoiding killing, respecting animal rights, and maintaining ecosystems are related but distinct objectives. Their unresolved tensions must be documented rather than hidden behind a composite score.
12. **Subsidiarity with shared obligations.** Decisions should occur at the lowest competent level, with higher-level support where welfare harms, supply chains, financial capacity, or ecological effects cross boundaries.

### 2.3 The Long-Term Post-Slaughter Aspiration

The v7.1 aspiration of a **90% transition to post-slaughter food systems by 2040** is retained as a **scenario for ethical and feasibility assessment**, not a binding global mandate. The same applies to the prior 2030 CAFO and industrial dairy phase-outs.

The Animal Welfare Council should maintain three separately evaluated pathways:

- **Immediate protection:** eliminate or substantially reduce well-established severe harms in current production and use.
- **Structural substitution:** reduce dependence on harmful production through affordable, nutritious alternatives and changes in demand and supply.
- **Long-term ethical transformation:** deliberate on whether breeding for use, ownership, and nonessential killing are compatible with guardianship, including disagreement about animals whose lives may contain positive welfare.

Success in one pathway does not automatically establish success in the others. The framework shall not use slaughter volume or industry contraction as the sole proxy for moral progress.

## 3. Structural Components

### 3.1 Sentience Evidence, Welfare Risk, and Protection Regimes

Version 7.1's ordinal species tiers are replaced by **three linked, separately recorded assessments**:

| Assessment | What it asks | Operational consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Sentience evidence profile** | What supports or challenges the possibility of conscious, welfare-relevant experience? | Confidence rating and precautionary research agenda |
| **Welfare-risk profile** | What harms, intensities, durations, and opportunities for positive experience arise in this setting? | Urgency and type of intervention |
| **Protection regime** | Which standards, rights-sensitive limits, monitoring, and remedies apply? | Enforceable species- and context-appropriate duties |

**Minimum protection domains and priorities:**

- **Primates and cetaceans:** stringent controls on captivity, social deprivation, handling, and invasive procedures; sanctuary and habitat alternatives where appropriate, with welfare of any relocated animal assessed.
- **Other mammals, including cattle and pigs:** species-appropriate social and physical environments, health and breeding safeguards, transport protections, and validated stunning/killing protocols where killing remains permitted. No blanket approval of gas anesthesia.
- **Birds:** protection against harmful stocking densities, restricted movement, chronic hunger, injuries, mutilations, and behavioral deprivation; targeted monitoring of animal-based indicators.
- **Fishes:** species- and production-specific controls on water quality, crowding, handling, capture, air exposure, transport, and stunning/killing; research where species-specific methods remain uncertain.
- **Cephalopods and decapod crustaceans:** explicit protection, including handling and humane killing research; do not relegate octopuses to a low-priority category solely because they are invertebrates.
- **Insects and other uncertain taxa:** precautionary welfare-risk assessment where credible evidence supports concern, with proportionate handling standards; **no default mandatory anesthesia of honeybees** absent validated indication and safety evidence.
- **Emerging or uncertain entities:** interim non-harm measures suited to credible risk, subject to independent review and revision.

Protection is not ranked solely by species. A widespread, severe welfare hazard affecting fish, poultry, or invertebrates may warrant high urgency even where evidence of sentience is less complete than for mammals.

### 3.2 Animal Welfare Council

A proposed specialist council under the PHC would:

- Develop species- and context-specific protection standards informed by veterinary science, welfare science, ethics, and community knowledge.
- Commission baseline studies, pilots, independent impact evaluations, and transparent reviews of adverse outcomes.
- Administer certification criteria, transition assistance, and the Protein Innovation Fund (subject to GGF budget approval).
- Coordinate with Oceans and Marine Governance, the GTC, food-security institutions, and relevant national authorities.
- Recommend proportionate responses to violations, including corrective orders, suspensions, and—as a last resort and under appropriate legal authority—sanctions reviewed through the proposed Digital Justice Tribunal.
- Maintain a public **Decision and Evidence Register**, the **Distributional Harm Ledger**, and an **Unresolved Ethical Questions Register**.

**Proposed composition:** Retain v7.1's initial **50% technical specialists / 50% community representatives** design and its Global South and youth-representation ambitions as **governance design options to test**, not settled legitimacy guarantees. Include expertise in animal welfare, Indigenous governance, disability and food security, worker transitions, ecology, ethics, and evaluation. Affected communities need meaningful agenda-setting and appeal rights; an appointed council must not displace legally protected Indigenous authority.

**Decision practice:** A qualified majority (v7.1 proposed 60%), conflict-of-interest disclosure, documented dissent, accessible publications, periodic independent institutional audit, and procedural review for high-impact decisions. Constitutional, rights, and FPIC obligations are not overrideable through an ordinary council vote.

### 3.3 Community Advisory Councils and Rights-Holders

Regional and BAZ/community bodies would participate in standard-setting, identify implementation failures, document local animal and human outcomes, and request reassessment. Indigenous peoples and rights-holders have separate FPIC 2.0 pathways where their rights, territories, or cultural practices are affected. A **30-day acknowledgment and response target** for concerns is proposed; urgent welfare emergencies use expedited triage.

Community evidence can challenge expert assumptions, but consultations must not replace veterinary assessment or obscure power differences within communities.

### 3.4 Enforcement, Certification, and Monitoring

- **Public standards:** Publish standards, exemptions, enforcement findings, and the evidence supporting each rule, with appropriate privacy protections.
- **Animal-based measurements:** Use health, injury, behavior, fear/distress, mortality, and positive-welfare indicators whenever validated, complemented by resource- and management-based measures.
- **Risk-weighted inspections:** Independent spot checks, confidential reporting, proportionate audit frequency, and remedies for noncompliance. Data access and procedural safeguards apply.
- **Technology-neutral verification:** Digital records, QR labels, satellite data, or machine-learning tools may improve traceability, but none should be required where trustworthy simpler alternatives exist. A ledger proves record integrity, not the underlying truth of a welfare observation.
- **Human review:** AI distress detection and automated scoring are advisory until independently validated for the species, setting, error rates, and welfare implications concerned.
- **Trade and supply chains:** Evaluate displacement before instituting trade measures. Design import requirements compatible with legal obligations, proportionate transition support, and a review of consequences for small producers.
- **Enforcement legitimacy:** Sanctions require a legal basis, due process, independent appeal, anti-corruption controls, and safeguards against discriminatory surveillance.

### 3.5 Independent Welfare and Transition Evaluation Function **[New]**

An evaluation function institutionally separated from program delivery shall:

1. Register intervention hypotheses, baselines, measurement methods, and stopping rules before implementation wherever feasible.
2. Evaluate animal welfare, human needs, ecological effects, trade leakage, cost, and realistic alternatives.
3. Publish negative findings, measurement limitations, distributional effects, and unresolved disputes.
4. Recommend **continue / modify / pause / replace / discontinue**, with reasons and evidence strength.
5. Permit communities, scientific reviewers, and legally recognized rights-holders to request a review.

The Council must publicly respond to adverse findings; disagreement must be reasoned and recorded.

## 4. Implementation Approaches

### 4.0 Bootstrap Phase — Actions Not Dependent on Full GGF Institutions

The framework can begin through existing national laws, animal-protection organizations, producer cooperatives, universities, veterinary networks, Indigenous institutions, and municipal/BAZ coalitions.

| Function | Transitional route |
|---|---|
| Welfare coordination | Existing animal-welfare groups, research institutions, cooperatives, and public authorities |
| Alternative-protein research | Existing universities, public grants, community enterprises, and private innovators with conflict-of-interest controls |
| Legal remedies | Applicable national and local laws, competent regulators, and existing judicial processes |
| Monitoring | Low-tech inspection, veterinary records, participatory monitoring, and validated animal-based measures |
| Economic assistance | Existing training, rural-development, nutrition, and worker-transition programs |
| Learning | Small pilots, transparent baseline datasets, and published adverse findings |

**Bootstrap rule:** Begin with interventions supported by strong evidence of preventable harm; do not delay urgent protections while waiting for global architecture, novel technology, or ideal indicators.

### 4.1 Initial Action Program (2027–2030; Proposed)

1. **Severe-harm safeguards:** Prioritize clearly documented high-severity practices and establish enforceable improvements, subject to regional competence and legal processes.
2. **Production-system mapping:** Map animals and populations at risk, welfare conditions, farm and fishery dependence, food-security exposure, and possible market displacement.
3. **Welfare-based facility pilots:** Test standards tailored to species and systems, with independent evaluations and meaningful worker/community participation.
4. **Nutritional access:** Demonstrate affordable substitution or access safeguards before reform steps likely to increase essential food costs.
5. **Alternative protein pathways:** Support plant-based, fermented, and cultivated approaches as research or deployment pathways, not presumed near-term replacements in every region.
6. **Wildlife and habitat collaboration:** Coordinate with biodiversity authorities, Indigenous governance, and Oceans and Marine Governance.
7. **Sentience research:** Prioritize understudied taxa and methods for assessing both negative and positive welfare.
8. **Evidence inventory:** Audit v7.1 historical claims and proposals; distinguish what has occurred from what was merely envisaged.

### 4.2 Evidence-Gated Transition Architecture **[Revised]**

Every high-impact transition—such as a phase-out, compulsory production standard, import measure, or large subsidy—must pass the following gates, with proportionality for emergency action:

| Gate | Minimum requirement | Possible decision |
|---|---|---|
| **G0 — Problem definition** | Species, practices, welfare harms, ethical rationale, affected groups | Specify, narrow, or reject proposal |
| **G1 — Baseline and alternatives** | Credible baseline, plausible counterfactuals, at least one less disruptive alternative | Pilot, gather evidence, or urgent targeted safeguard |
| **G2 — Welfare case** | Credible expected reduction in severe harm or increase in positive welfare; state uncertainty | Proceed, redesign, or defer scale-up |
| **G3 — Human justice and readiness** | Basic nutrition, livelihoods, rights-holder consent where applicable, burden and remedy plan | Adjust timing, compensation, jurisdiction, or scope |
| **G4 — Ecological and displacement** | Land, biodiversity, emissions, wild-animal harms, trade leakage, species substitution | Redesign or mitigate |
| **G5 — Governance and reversibility** | Independent review, metrics, legal authority, redress, explicit triggers, irreversible-step assessment | Authorize staged scale-up or reject |
| **G6 — Post-implementation review** | Observed outcomes against baseline and counterfactual; adverse events; opportunity costs | Continue, modify, pause, replace, discontinue |

**No fictional precision:** Thresholds and timing are to be set and publicly justified for particular interventions before evaluation. A lack of cross-species welfare conversion factors must be disclosed, not concealed behind a falsely precise composite index.

**Rights floor:** Significant preventable human malnutrition or destitution, violations of Indigenous rights, or grave unmitigated animal harms cannot be casually justified by aggregate benefits. Where unavoidable conflicts remain, the Council must publish an explicit moral proportionality analysis rather than rely on a single benefit-cost ratio.

**Prior milestones:** The v7.1 figures—25% reduction in industrial animal use by 2030; 50% by 2040; 90% post-slaughter transition by 2040; industrial dairy/CAFO replacement by 2030; 100% ethical slaughter compliance by 2035—are **archived as unvalidated scenarios** until definitions, baselines, welfare and food-security consequences, feasibility, and legal authority are established. No implication of completed action or automatic adoption is intended.

### 4.3 Wildlife and Habitat Protection

Coordinate protection of habitat connectivity, threatened species, and vulnerable populations with Planetary Health and Indigenous institutions. The global “30x30” biodiversity objective is an external policy reference, not an animal-welfare outcome measure or unilateral land-management mandate.

Wildlife management must make explicit the distinction between population/ecosystem outcomes and welfare outcomes for individual animals. Predator restoration, disease control, invasive-species interventions, relocation, and culling require comparative assessments of welfare harms, ecological consequences, proportionality, and nonlethal alternatives.

Research on wild-animal welfare should be cautious about unintended ecosystem effects. Do not imply that all predation or naturally occurring suffering is simply a governance failure to be eliminated.

### 4.4 Just Transition and Food Security Protocol **[Expanded]**

Before a high-impact reform is scaled, the responsible authority shall prepare and publish:

- **Nutrition protection plan:** identify populations at risk of reduced access to energy, protein, vitamin B12, iron, zinc, or other relevant nutrients; specify affordable substitutes, supplementation/fortification when appropriate, monitoring, and remedy mechanisms.
- **Livelihood assessment:** distinguish contract farmers, paid workers, smallholders, pastoralists, fishing communities, processors, and large asset owners. Existing debt and switching costs must be considered.
- **Distributional Harm Ledger:** record who gains and loses, baseline vulnerability, severe risks, compensation/support offered, actual uptake, and unresolved claims.
- **Just transition financing:** wage bridges, retraining, debt/workplace transition aid, cooperative enterprise development, and proportionate compensation for stranded investment when justified. *Full compensation for all losses is not automatic.*
- **Cross-border effects review:** prevent shifting harmful production elsewhere, while avoiding unjustified exclusion of small exporters and poorer producers.
- **Community and rights-holder pathway:** accessible decision documents, meaningful representation, cultural mediation, and FPIC 2.0 where required.
- **Social safeguards and triggers:** predefined criteria for correcting severe food insecurity, livelihood deterioration, or discriminatory enforcement.

The Protein Innovation Fund remains proposed, but the **$10 billion by 2030** amount from v7.1 is a **historical illustrative budget assumption pending costing**. Funding should support multiple plausible pathways and report opportunity costs, including comparison with other animal-welfare and human-welfare uses of public resources.

### 4.5 Innovation and Technology Integration

Priority research includes nonharmful protein production, validated welfare measures, better housing and handling, lower-risk slaughter/stunning where legally permitted, humane wildlife-management tools, and traceability proportionate to risk. Research funding requires welfare-impact evaluation and conflict-of-interest controls.

- **Plant-based, fermented, and cultivated foods:** assess nutrition, affordability, energy and land requirements, accessibility, scalability, and welfare effects; no assumed universal cost target.
- **Welfare monitoring:** compare AI or sensor systems with trained human assessment; validate before consequential use.
- **Genetic interventions:** assess immediate and intergenerational welfare, potential suppression of behavioral capacities, ecological hazards, consent/governance limits, and alternative non-genetic solutions.
- **Blockchain, tokens, VR, and related proposals:** optional experiments only if demonstrably useful. No welfare compliance claim may rest on a token, blockchain entry, or VR simulation alone.
- **Synthetic or hybrid lifeforms:** apply GTC and welfare-ethics review, including uncertainty about sentience and whether creating welfare-capable entities is morally appropriate.

### 4.6 Ethical Dairy Transition Pathway **[Revised]**

The v7.1 **“Willing Cow”** vision is retained as a **candidate high-welfare system**, not a fully validated certification standard or proof of animal consent. The phrase **must not imply literal informed consent by a cow**.

**Candidate design requirements:**

- Protect calf health through timely colostrum, adequate milk intake, nutrition, veterinary care, disease prevention, and validated growth and behavior measures.
- Support prolonged, welfare-positive cow-calf contact or foster-cow alternatives where suitable, with careful management of separation and weaning.
- Provide environments compatible with locomotion, social relationships, lying and resting comfort, appropriate outdoor access where feasible, and freedom from avoidable chronic distress.
- Do not treat natural mating, artificial insemination, or pasture access as intrinsically sufficient evidence of welfare; assess actual practices and impacts.
- Assess the cow's health, reproductive burden, lactation, calf needs, and total lifetime welfare—not only calf weight.
- Define milk as “surplus” only if independent, species-appropriate assessment demonstrates that removal does not compromise calf or cow welfare. **Remove v7.1's unvalidated six-hour nursing rule and automatic 25th/50th percentile stop/restart thresholds.**
- Use records, veterinary review, and independent observations appropriate to the producer's scale; digital tags are optional.

A scientific and community panel should develop a pilot protocol, compare it with other dairy and non-dairy systems, and decide whether certification is justified. Claims such as “ethical dairy” and “consent” should be carefully qualified to avoid welfare-washing.

### 4.7 Ethical Hunting, Fishing, and Wildlife Management

**Hunting:** Maintain v7.1's distinction among commercial exploitation, recreational hunting, subsistence practices, and ecologically justified control. Evaluate animal suffering, ecological effects, human needs, and rights-holder governance. Avoid blanket claims that any named method is universally humane; set performance and competency standards and forbid demonstrably severe practices where justified.

**Indigenous subsistence:** Respect FPIC 2.0, food security, customary governance, and community-led conservation, while supporting collaborative approaches to avoiding unnecessary suffering. The Earth Council's proposed oversight role must not displace legally protected Indigenous decision-making.

**Emergency culling:** Require documented necessity, scientific and veterinary advice, least-harm feasible methods, disease-control proportionality, humane handling, and after-action review. Avoid framing culling as a routine management shortcut.

**Capture fisheries and aquaculture:**

- Evaluate capture time, entanglement, crowding, pressure and temperature change, air exposure, bycatch, handling, and time to effective unconsciousness or death.
- Use context- and species-specific assessments of fishing gear; avoid categorical assertions that pole-and-line, particular nets, or individual devices are intrinsically humane in all conditions.
- Develop validated stunning and killing standards that account for species, equipment, operator competence, failure rates, and confirmation of unconsciousness/death.
- Apply WOAH farmed-fish guidance where relevant and explicitly note that wild-capture systems have additional hazards and evidence gaps.
- Coordinate quotas and ecosystem effects with Oceans and Marine Governance; ecological sustainability is not proof of low individual suffering.

### 4.8 Emergency and Precautionary Response **[New]**

Where credible evidence indicates imminent, severe, and preventable suffering, competent authorities may implement proportionate, time-limited protective measures ahead of a full assessment. An emergency record shall state the hazard, evidence, legal authority, expected welfare benefit, human and ecological risks, duration, review date, and appeals route. Emergency powers cannot silently become permanent defaults.

### 4.9 Policy Adjustment, Suspension, and Abandonment **[New]**

The following are distinct decisions:

- **Modify:** a promising intervention needs altered parameters or additional safeguards.
- **Pause:** evidence indicates serious new harms or material uncertainty before an irreversible next step.
- **Replace:** another feasible measure offers materially better outcomes at proportionate cost.
- **Discontinue:** credible evaluation indicates the intervention fails its welfare purpose, violates hard protections, causes unjustifiable net harm, or cannot be made workable.
- **Revisit the moral premise:** scientific evidence materially changes judgments about sentience or welfare, or philosophical deliberation changes the framework's understanding of rights, harm, or guardianship.

A failed policy does **not** establish that animal suffering is morally irrelevant. Conversely, ethical commitment must **not** be used to preserve failed interventions indefinitely. Serious repeated failures should reduce confidence in the feasibility of that transformation pathway.

## 5. What Success Looks Like

*Metrics tell us whether interventions achieve their stated goals. A living account of success must also describe lives, relationships, and freedoms that indicators imperfectly represent. The following are **illustrative desired futures**, not documented events or projections.*

**A former intensive farm** has transitioned through a locally agreed pathway. Workers have secure livelihoods and a voice in the new enterprise. Fewer animals are bred into conditions that previously caused serious deprivation. Independent evaluation, not the appearance of a new building or a publicity campaign, establishes whether animal suffering actually declined.

**An Indigenous community** continues culturally significant subsistence relationships with animals and the land under its own legitimate governance. Its knowledge contributes to ecological and welfare understanding, and FPIC 2.0 is respected in practice. The community is a partner and rights-holder, not merely an exemption category.

**A child at a school lunch** receives affordable, nutritious, appealing food. Lower-harm options are easy to choose, and nobody is deprived of adequate nutrition. Claims about the food's animal-welfare or environmental performance are independently checkable.

**A dairy herd and its calves** live in a system that supports health, social bonds, movement, and choice. Producers can show, with validated measures rather than labels alone, that lactation and milk removal do not compromise the animals' welfare. The ethical question of breeding animals for use remains open for public deliberation.

**A fishing crew** uses methods evaluated for its species and conditions, minimizes handling stress, and has access to validated stunning or killing procedures where capture continues. Independent monitoring includes bycatch and cumulative effects, not just certified gear.

**A whale in the Southern Ocean** can communicate through a less disturbed acoustic environment. Shipping measures are chosen based on observed wildlife and ecological outcomes, not solely incentives or target compliance.

**The central aspiration:** The question is not only how to minimize suffering, or how to replace one production system with another. It is how humans can share the Earth with other beings while respecting their lives, the integrity of ecosystems, and the legitimate needs of human communities.

## 6. Metrics and Evaluation

### 6.1 Animal Welfare Outcomes and Adaptive Evaluation Protocol **[New]**

For major interventions, the minimum evaluation dossier must include:

1. **Baseline:** affected species, approximate numbers, current practices, welfare harms and benefits, existing uncertainty, and distribution of harms across individuals.
2. **Counterfactual:** credible projections of what happens without intervention and under at least one alternative; assumptions disclosed.
3. **Welfare outcomes:** incidence, severity, and duration of negative experiences; mortality and morbidity; injuries and disease; relevant behavioral measures; and opportunities for positive experiences and animal choice.
4. **Production and displacement:** total animals bred, confined, handled, transported, and killed; shifts between species; imports/exports; black-market and offshoring risks.
5. **Human effects:** nutritional adequacy, affordability, livelihood and employment changes, community effects, distribution by vulnerability, and procedural rights.
6. **Ecology and wild animals:** land, emissions, water, biodiversity, and harms or benefits to free-living animals.
7. **Economic and opportunity costs:** program expenditure, alternative uses of resources, distribution of costs, and financing sustainability.
8. **Uncertainty:** source quality, missing information, welfare-comparison assumptions, sensitivity analysis, and unresolved ethical disagreements.
9. **Review plan:** preregistered measures, responsible evaluator, planned review dates, and modify/pause/discontinue triggers.

**Measurement rule:** Where cross-species comparability is unknown, report multiple welfare dimensions and sensitivity analyses. Do not express speculative estimates as precise universal “suffering units.”

### 6.2 Primary Outcome Families

| Outcome family | Illustrative indicators | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Severe negative welfare | Proportion affected, frequency, estimated duration/intensity, documented adverse events | Welfare states often must be inferred; proxies need validation |
| Positive welfare | Social contact, exploration, locomotion, resting, choice/control, play where relevant | Different species and ages express welfare differently |
| Harmful industry exposure | Animals by production system, time in confinement, transport and slaughter exposures | Industrial-use reduction alone is not welfare improvement |
| Human basic needs | Food insecurity, dietary adequacy, price and access, serious livelihood harm | Average prices conceal unequal burdens |
| Ecological consequences | Land use, pollution, biodiversity, habitat change, wild-animal impacts | Ecosystem gains and individual welfare may conflict |
| Fairness and legitimacy | Compensation received, FPIC compliance, complaints, appeals, representation | Formal consultation may not equal actual influence |
| Learning and correction | Completed reviews, published adverse findings, changes made after findings | Procedure counts do not prove beneficial outcomes |

### 6.3 Targets, Baselines, and Scenario Register

**Binding global numeric targets are not established by this draft.** Each eventual target must specify: baseline year, affected population, geographic coverage, data source, confidence level, welfare rationale, rights/food-security guardrails, resources required, responsible authority, and revision conditions.

**Version 7.1 historical proposals requiring validation:**

| Legacy v7.1 figure | v7.2 treatment |
|---|---|
| 25% reduction in industrial animal use by 2030; 50% by 2040 | Preserve as candidate scenarios; define “industrial use,” denominator, and expected welfare gains |
| 90% slaughter phase-out or post-slaughter transition by 2040 | Preserve as long-term scenario for ethical, nutrition, infrastructure, and feasibility review |
| 100% “ethical slaughter” compliance by 2035 | Replace vague label with independently validated, species-specific process/outcome standards |
| $5/kg cultivated meat by 2035 | Retain only as a technology-cost sensitivity scenario after evidence audit |
| Zero IUCN-listed extinctions by 2035 | Keep as an ecological aspiration in the appropriate biodiversity framework, not an attributable animal-welfare performance indicator |
| Livestock antibiotic reduction and research milestones | Set context-specific baselines; coordinate One Health and research agendas |

### 6.4 Leading Indicators and Early Warning

Indicators may include facility inspections, qualified staff, species-relevant handling practices, validated welfare outcome sampling, alternative-food access, nutrition surveillance, farm and fishery transition readiness, trade leakage, and conflicts involving Indigenous rights. Each indicator should have a stated reason for predicting the outcome of interest.

**Early-warning triggers** include emerging severe food insecurity, marked deterioration in animal welfare in replacement systems, production displacement to worse welfare settings, grave violations of rights, significant ecological harm, corruption, and failed or misleading welfare certification. Triggers must initiate review; they need not all force identical automatic responses.

### 6.5 Transparency and Accountability

A proposed Live Transparency Dashboard should publish accessible, downloadable, disaggregated data and methodological notes. Annual independent evaluations report favorable and unfavorable results. The proposed Global Citizens' Assembly and relevant representative bodies may review broad direction; rights-holder protections are not delegated away. Dashboards must display uncertainty and data gaps, not only rankings.

### 6.6 Cultural Adoption and Rights Monitoring

Measure consent quality, co-design participation, rights-holder satisfaction, language access, responsiveness of appeals, and practical effects on community sovereignty. “Adoption” should not be interpreted as moral success if it is obtained through coercion, misinformation, or lack of alternatives.

### 6.7 Distributional Harm Ledger **[New]**

For each high-impact reform, publish a privacy-preserving ledger covering: affected group; type of harm; seriousness; duration; preventability; who decided; available recourse; mitigation promised; mitigation delivered; and unresolved claims. The ledger is part of G3 and G6 evaluation (see §4.2).

## 7. Cultural Sensitivity and Justice

### 7.1 Decolonization and Indigenous Sovereignty

The framework recognizes Indigenous peoples as rights-holders, not merely stakeholders. Territorial governance, traditional ecological knowledge, resource access, and relevant legal obligations must be respected through FPIC 2.0 and appropriate institutions. Proposed Earth Council roles require legal and legitimacy review to avoid superseding community sovereignty.

Animal welfare considerations must be discussed with affected communities without treating either scientific expertise or cultural tradition as automatically sufficient to settle contested harms.

### 7.2 Cultural Innovation and Non-Coercive Transition

Support community-designed alternatives, sanctuary initiatives, humane husbandry and fishing improvements, and culturally appropriate dietary options. Avoid identity-based blame. Practical choice depends on affordability, food availability, infrastructure, social relations, and health needs—not information campaigns alone.

### 7.3 Language and Communication Justice

Provide materials in relevant local and Indigenous languages, plain-language explanations, accessible participatory forums, and routes to contest scientific or policy claims. Counter-misinformation efforts should publish evidence and corrections transparently; **celebrity advocacy and behavioral nudges must not replace informed deliberation or disguise uncertainty**.

### 7.4 Economic Justice

Prioritize severe hardship and low bargaining power. Separate worker/household protection from compensation to owners of capital. Make compensation rules public, proportionate, and appealable. AUBI and Hearts may supplement support if instituted under the wider GGF, but cannot be assumed available in bootstrap phases.

### 7.5 Human Rights, Animal Protection, and Irreducible Conflict **[New]**

Some decisions will have no costless solution. Disputes involving animals' welfare, Indigenous rights, human nutrition, sacred practices, and ecosystem integrity require an explicit proportionality record addressing: the competing goods, affected populations, feasible alternatives, who bears the loss, legal constraints, epistemic uncertainty, and why the chosen intervention is justified. Ethical dissent must be recorded even when a lawful decision is reached.

## 8. Integration with Planetary Health Governance

### 8.1 Roles and Shared Data

The PHC provides coordination with biodiversity, food systems, health, and ecological indicators. The Animal Welfare Council retains responsibility for the individual welfare lens; other councils retain their mandates. Common data systems may improve coordination but **must not collapse welfare, carbon, species survival, and economic productivity into a single unexamined metric**.

### 8.2 One Health and Resilience

Joint agendas include antimicrobial resistance, zoonotic risks, food security, public health, biosecurity, and climate adaptation. These are potential co-benefits or trade-offs, **not automatic success criteria for every animal-welfare intervention**. Failure to reduce emissions, for example, does not by itself invalidate a reform that robustly reduces severe animal suffering without unjustifiable countervailing harms.

### 8.3 Oceans and Marine Governance

The Oceans and Marine Governance framework addresses quotas, biodiversity, marine protected areas, and ecosystem resilience. The Guardian Framework addresses welfare consequences of capture, transport, farming, handling, and killing, including impacts on target and nontarget animals. Joint reviews must reconcile or explicitly document conflicts rather than assume sustainability implies humane methods.

### 8.4 Finance and Digital Justice

Potential instruments include public funding, progressive levies, targeted producer transition assistance, cooperative finance, philanthropic grants, and carefully evaluated welfare-linked funds. Animal welfare carbon credits, welfare impact bonds, and Hearts/Leaves tokens remain **experimental proposals**; no financing instrument should double-count benefits or equate financial transactions with verified suffering reduction. The proposed Digital Justice Tribunal serves a legally constituted appeal/enforcement role only where authorized.

### 8.5 Institutional Learning and Feedback to the GGF

Animal-welfare transition pilots can provide evidence for the GGF's broader theories of subsidiarity, adaptive governance, and institutional learning. Reports should document not only successful outcomes but failed hypotheses, unintended incentives, uneven burdens, and the conditions under which lessons transfer between places.

## 9. Appendices

### 9.1 Case Studies and Scenarios

**Status labeling is mandatory:** `Verified empirical case`, `Literature-based synthesis`, `Scenario`, or `Fictional illustration`.

- **New Zealand livestock sea-export ban (2023):** *Policy precedent requiring case-specific evaluation*, not proof that pasture replacement, cultured meat, or emissions reductions succeeded.
- **Amazon Basin community-led subsistence and habitat governance:** *Scenario; partner and rights-holder arrangements to be confirmed.*
- **Dutch alternative-protein transition:** *Scenario; any claimed national mandate must be separately verified.*
- **Vermont cow-calf dairy:** *Fictional illustration pending welfare validation.*
- **Scandinavian moose management and Pacific tuna fishery:** *Illustrative scenarios; no claim of verified welfare outcomes.*
- **Factory farm conversion to protein enterprise:** *Scenario; economic and welfare gains must be measured, not assumed.*

Remove unattributed quotations presented as testimony from named or unnamed Indigenous elders unless consent and provenance can be verified.

### 9.2 Implementation Tools

Deliverables to develop and test: regional playbooks; model species-appropriate standards; veterinary and livelihood assessment checklists; FPIC 2.0 protocol; community deliberation guide; preregistration template; impact evaluation checklist; distributional harm ledger template; appeals process; and plain-language summaries. Claiming a toolkit exists requires a linked, published artifact.

### 9.3 Technical and Data Specifications

The minimum data specification includes species; location and setting; animal population and sampling frame; outcome definition and validation; assessor; collection date; uncertainty; confounders; audit trace; access restrictions; and correction history. Prefer open standards, privacy-preserving publication, and offline-capable tools. Records may be paper-based or electronic. Any AI model must publish validation conditions and known failures.

### 9.4 Economic Models and Transition Scenarios

Maintain separate models for food affordability, physical capacity, producer debt, rural employment, consumer substitution, public finance, externalities, and trade leakage. Include sensitivity to uptake, implementation delay, technological cost, and policy compensation. The v7.1 “20-year self-sustaining financing” outline is an **unvalidated scenario**, not a financial forecast.

### 9.5 Emerging Challenges Protocol

Cover climate displacement of animals, disease outbreaks, new aquaculture systems, genetic engineering, and artificial/synthetic biological entities. Apply proportionate interim safeguards, expert and community review, time-limited authorization, and clear escalation. Where sentience remains uncertain, unresolved status must remain visible; classification deadlines are administrative review dates, not claims that science must converge.

### 9.6 Industrial and Supply-Chain Transition

Assess contract dependencies, producer debt, processing and transport workers, food substitution, farm conversion, international trade, fraud, animal population reduction without mass culling, and ecosystem restoration. Pilot transition plans should explicitly consider whether an apparent welfare gain merely moves suffering to a different species or geography.

### 9.7 Community Governance and Dispute Resolution

Define consent rights, representational rules, local complaint procedures, independent scientific challenge, human-rights and Indigenous-rights review, and appeal routes. International GGF arbitration is proposed only where parties have legitimate jurisdictional and legal authority; this document does not itself create such authority.

### 9.8 Glossary

- **Animal-based measure:** An observation of an animal's health, behavior, condition, or other welfare-relevant outcome, rather than merely a facility input or record.
- **Animal welfare:** The animal's state and experiences across relevant physical, behavioral, social, and affective domains, including possible positive as well as negative welfare.
- **Guardianship, not ownership:** Normative commitment to duties toward animals beyond commercial utility; legal forms of custody/property remain subject to separate deliberation and reform.
- **Severe avoidable suffering:** Suffering sufficiently intense, prolonged, or widespread that prevention merits strong priority, where feasible means exist to avoid or substantially reduce it; operational definitions are context-specific.
- **Sentience evidence profile:** Versioned assessment of evidence and uncertainty concerning an organism's capacity for welfare-relevant experience.
- **Protection regime:** Context-appropriate obligations, standards, inspection, and remedies applicable to particular animals or interactions.
- **Evidence gate:** A publicly specified requirement to evaluate a policy before irreversible commitment or scale-up.
- **Distributional Harm Ledger:** Record of costs, benefits, severity, remedies, and unresolved burdens by affected group.
- **Policy leakage:** A reform's harmful activity moves to another location, jurisdiction, species, or less visible production system.
- **Positive welfare:** Opportunities or experiences that may benefit an animal beyond merely avoiding negative states.
- **FPIC 2.0:** GGF's proposed enhanced Free, Prior and Informed Consent standard; requires consistency with applicable Indigenous rights and authorities.
- **BAZ, AUBI, Hearts, Leaves, PHC, GTC, BHI:** GGF terms retained from companion frameworks; their authority, financing, and implementation are not assumed to exist already.
- **“Willing Cow”:** Working title for a cow-calf-centered welfare hypothesis; **not** a verified statement of animal consent or an approved certification.

### 9.9 Sentience Research and Ethical Edge Cases

**Research priorities:** cross-taxon evidence of experience; behavior and preference tests; welfare-relevant neural and physiological measures; pain, fear, sociality, exploration, and positive affect; validity across environments and life stages; cephalopod and decapod welfare; insect welfare uncertainty; and the ethics of creating novel potentially sentient entities.

**Assessment method:** evidence synthesis, transparent competing hypotheses, confidence and disagreement reporting, precaution proportional to credible harm, and periodic review. No single neuron-density number or algorithm shall decide moral status.

**Ethical distinctions:** scientific evidence can inform whether and how an animal experiences harm; it cannot alone settle whether killing is wrong, how human and animal interests compare, or whether breeding an animal with a positive life is beneficial. These questions belong in transparent plural ethical deliberation.

### 9.10 Outstanding Ethical Questions **[New]**

1. **Suffering and killing:** When, if ever, is painless killing morally permissible independently of welfare during life and death?
2. **Population ethics:** Is bringing an animal with a potentially good life into existence beneficial, neutral, or problematic? How should nonexistence be compared with possible lives?
3. **Rights and guardianship:** Which rights, if any, follow from sentience or social complexity, and how should they relate to legal custody and commercial practices?
4. **Cross-species comparisons:** What can be said under deep uncertainty about severity, duration, and moral weighting?
5. **Wild animals:** How should individual welfare relate to natural predation, ecological stability, restoration, and noninterference?
6. **Irreducible sacrifices:** How should unavoidable costs to vulnerable humans and animals be weighed when no intervention protects every interest?
7. **Future entities:** What duties arise before creating novel systems with a credible potential for welfare-relevant experience?

These questions **do not** suspend well-supported immediate protections.

| ID and owner | Versioned decision identifier, responsible institution, legal basis |
| Objective | Welfare harm addressed and expected benefit |

## Closing Commitment

The Global Guardian Framework seeks a world in which animals are not valued only by what they provide to humans, and in which human communities are not treated as expendable obstacles to compassionate reform.

It commits to **moral seriousness without policy infallibility**: protect beings from credible severe harm, recognize positive welfare and relational life, account for real human and ecological consequences, publish uncertainty, and change course when evidence demonstrates a better path.

The measure of success is not whether every early milestone was achieved, but whether our relationships and institutions become demonstrably more capable of reducing unjustifiable harm and supporting flourishing—while remaining accountable to the beings and communities whose lives they affect.
