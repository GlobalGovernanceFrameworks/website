## 0. Controlled successor notice

Biodiversity v1.1 preserves the source framework’s purpose: Indigenous-led stewardship, ecosystem rights, restoration, economic transition, community monitoring, just transition, and real accountability for severe ecological harm. It replaces the v1.0 assumption that a biodiversity body, score, threshold, future date, pilot, or coalition can move directly from ecological concern to personhood, veto, guardian appointment, payment, market exclusion, emergency deployment, prosecution, or punishment.

> **Biodiversity urgency does not create biodiversity jurisdiction.**

> **Observation is not attribution. Attribution is not legal recognition. Recognition is not title. Guardianship is not regulation. Stewardship is not payment. Certification is not market exclusion. Allegation is not guilt. Emergency support is not command.**

This framework may coordinate, observe, advise, build capacity, support affected Indigenous nations and communities, administer programmes or funds where separately chartered, facilitate restoration, prepare lawful records, and refer matters to competent authorities. It cannot create a global ecological sovereign, grant legal personhood, appoint guardians, transfer title, issue universal permits or tariffs, prosecute, blacklist, freeze funds, seize assets, declare emergencies, or command force.

## 2. Vision and constitutional purpose

Biodiversity loss is a governance failure rooted in extractive incentives, fragmented jurisdiction, colonial dispossession, unaccountable supply chains, weak protection of ecological relationships, and the systematic undervaluation of stewardship. The answer is not to replace private extraction with unbounded ecological administration. The answer is to build lawful, plural, reviewable institutions that can protect ecosystems while preserving affected peoples’ authority, human rights, livelihoods, animal welfare, scientific humility, and the possibility of correction.

The framework’s purpose is to connect five functions without merging them:

1. **ecological knowledge** from EIOI, affected nations, communities, science, and lawful monitoring;
2. **rights and guardianship pathways** through CERGTA and competent legal authorities;
3. **stewardship and restoration programmes** through locally grounded, affected-nation-led institutions;
4. **economic transition** through separately authorized AUBI, finance, subsidy, procurement, and trade systems;
5. **accountability and remedy** through independent investigation, adjudication, restoration, appeal, and—where required—SCPA-governed execution.

## 3. Core principles

### 3.1 Affected-nation authority

The specific affected Indigenous nation or nations determine their representatives, law, territorial and relational authority, consent requirements, protected knowledge, governance forms, and whether to engage. Global Indigenous representation inside the BCT strengthens the BCT’s legitimacy but cannot provide FPIC for another people.

### 3.2 Rights of nature through lawful recognition

Ecosystems may receive legal rights and standing where competent law provides. Biodiversity institutions may support petitions and evidence but cannot create personhood by resolution, target, dashboard, or threshold.

### 3.3 Observation–consequence separation

Ecological observations, citizen science, digital twins, satellite data, AI classifications, Bio-Credits, BHI modules, and degradation thresholds may trigger review. They do not themselves create legal rights, guardians, permits, vetoes, funding outcomes, market restrictions, guilt, or emergency power.

### 3.4 Precaution with due process

Precaution may justify enhanced study, monitoring, interim non-prejudice, or a request for temporary protection. It does not erase jurisdiction, affected-nation authority, participation, proportionality, review, expiry, or remedy.

### 3.5 Stewardship with livelihoods and animal welfare

Restoration and conservation must address workers, farmers, fishers, residents, customary users, food systems, accessibility, and individual-animal welfare. Ecological improvement cannot be measured solely by aggregate habitat outcomes while localized burdens disappear from view.

### 3.6 Just transition before withdrawal

No ban, subsidy phase-out, procurement exclusion, protected-area transition, or industry conversion is constitutionally complete without lawful authority, affected-worker and community analysis, alternatives, material support, sequencing, review, and remedy.

### 3.7 No ecosystem collateralization

An ecosystem’s legal rights, personhood, sacred status, or restoration need cannot be pledged as collateral, monetized as a waiver, or extinguished through a bond, debt swap, credit, certification, or investment contract.

### 3.8 Open tools, protected knowledge

Methods, public standards, and non-sensitive tools should be open and accessible. Traditional, sacred, genetic, personal, worker, commercial, and security-sensitive data remain purpose-limited and protected.

### 3.9 Subsidiarity and transboundary coordination

Action should occur at the lowest competent level while recognizing that migratory species, watersheds, oceans, atmospheric effects, and supply chains cross borders. Coordination does not create sovereignty; every participant retains only lawfully delegated power.

### 3.10 Every authority ends

Every mandate, grant, restriction, investigation, mission, certification, guardianship, and programme has an expiry, review, termination, succession, data-disposition, correction, and remedy path.

## 4. Governance architecture

### 4.1 Global Biodiversity Commons Trust (BCT)

The BCT is retained as a **non-sovereign coordination and programme institution**. It is not the trustee or owner of the biosphere, the universal representative of ecosystems, or a court.

Its possible functions, only when separately chartered, include:

- coordinate voluntary and lawfully adopted biodiversity programmes;
- publish model standards and interoperability guidance;
- support ecological-rights petitions without deciding them;
- maintain public programme and method registries;
- facilitate technical assistance and peer learning;
- administer the Global Biodiversity Fund through an independently constituted financial function;
- commission transparent programme evaluation;
- support affected Indigenous nations, communities, guardians, and restoration bodies at their request;
- liaise with existing international and regional bodies.

It may not:

- ratify ecosystem personhood;
- appoint guardians;
- determine Indigenous consent or representation;
- own ecosystems or transferred land by default;
- issue binding permits, quotas, tariffs, sanctions, or trade restrictions;
- investigate or adjudicate alleged violations;
- declare emergencies or command response teams;
- order search, seizure, arrest, detention, force, cyber action, or asset freezing.

#### 4.1.1 Functional separation

BCT governance, scientific advice, Indigenous-nation liaison, programme administration, fund custody, procurement, audit, complaints, rights-review support, and enforcement referrals must be structurally separated. A member who participates in grant approval, certification, investigation support, or a contested recommendation must disclose conflicts and recuse where necessary.

#### 4.1.2 Representation

Indigenous, scientific, youth, community, worker, disability, animal-welfare, and affected-region representation is required for BCT legitimacy. Numerical seat allocations govern the BCT’s own internal decisions only. They do not create authority over affected nations, local governments, titleholders, guardians, courts, or communities.

### 4.2 Global Biodiversity Fund

The Fund is a separately constituted public-purpose financial mechanism. Its charter must identify contributors, custodian, eligible uses, allocation criteria, conflicts, beneficial ownership, procurement, reserves, audit, suspension, appeal, restitution, insolvency, and dissolution.

A BCT programme recommendation is not a grant decision. A biodiversity score is not payment eligibility. A funding condition cannot require waiver of Indigenous claims, protected knowledge, legal rights, essential services, or access to remedy.

### 4.3 Regional Biodiversity Hubs

Regional Hubs are implementation and capacity institutions grounded in host jurisdictions and affected-nation relationships. Their possible functions include monitoring support, restoration coordination, community grants, training, low-tech participation, programme reporting, and voluntary stewardship facilitation.

A Hub may exercise regulatory, licensing, inspection, fund, or protected-area powers only when the exact power is lawfully delegated and recorded in `BIO-11`. A Hub cannot make a standard binding merely by adopting language that is “stricter than national regulation.”

### 4.4 Ecological and Animal-Welfare Ethics Board

The Board is an advisory plural-reasoning body. It examines tensions such as rewilding versus individual-animal welfare, invasive-species intervention, assisted migration, disease control, predator reintroduction, captivity, and habitat management.

It must publish:

- the ecological objective and evidence;
- the welfare interests and likely harms;
- affected-nation and community views;
- alternatives and less harmful methods;
- uncertainty and dissent;
- monitoring, review, and stopping conditions.

The Board cannot adjudicate rights, appoint guardians, authorize killing or capture, issue a permit, or impose a project outcome.

### 4.5 Guardians and ecosystem representation

Guardians are appointed only by a competent authority through CERGTA. They hold bounded fiduciary duties to the represented ecosystem and may receive instructions from applicable Indigenous law. They do not own the ecosystem or acquire general regulatory, financial, inspection, or enforcement power.

### 4.6 Independent justice, finance, trade, emergency, and coercion bodies

Adjudication remains with independent competent justice institutions. Financial decisions remain with competent finance and AUBI bodies. Trade and procurement restrictions remain with lawfully constituted trade or procurement authorities. Emergency command remains under CDEE/ECRC. Coercive execution remains under SCPA.

## 5. Constitutional operating chain

The default sequence is:

1. `BIO-02` ecological or programme intake;
2. `BIO-03` observation and provenance;
3. `BIO-04` data and knowledge controls;
4. `BIO-05` method and threshold specification;
5. `BIO-06` interpretation, alternatives, uncertainty, and dissent;
6. `BIO-07` affected-interest mapping;
7. `BIO-08` affected-nation authority;
8. `BIO-09` participation and support;
9. the appropriate rights, stewardship, economic, inspection, urgent-protection, or accountability route;
10. separate competent decision;
11. implementation under its own clock and mandate;
12. review, correction, remedy, termination, and data disposition.

A later step cannot be inferred from completion of an earlier one.

## 6. Indigenous-led biodiversity governance

### 6.1 Territorial and relational authority

In affected Indigenous territories and relationships, the framework follows the nation’s own governance, law, protocols, and decision makers. “Indigenous-led” cannot be satisfied merely through BCT seat percentages, global advisory councils, outside elders, or generic BAZ structures.

### 6.2 FPIC and opt-out

FPIC applies where required by the affected nation and applicable law. Refusal, withdrawal, or non-engagement carries no penalty and cannot justify loss of funding, services, monitoring support, legal standing, or environmental protection.

### 6.3 Traditional Knowledge and benefit sharing

Traditional Knowledge may be public, community-governed, restricted, sacred, or non-disclosable. The affected nation decides permitted use, attribution, benefit sharing, derivatives, publication, retention, return, and deletion. A biodiversity model cannot silently encode protected knowledge into a public score or product passport.

### 6.4 Internal plurality and competing claims

The framework must not assume that a nation, community, clan, family, youth body, women’s body, customary institution, or elected government speaks with one voice. `BIO-08` and `BIO-22` record internal plurality and prevent framework convenience from extinguishing disagreement.

## 7. Ecosystem-rights implementation

### 7.1 Petition and review

Communities, affected nations, guardians, public bodies, or other persons with lawful standing may submit `BIO-13`. The petition identifies the entity, proposed rights, legal basis, ecological and cultural evidence, affected interests, requested relief, and what the petition does not decide.

### 7.2 Recommendation

`BIO-14` provides an advisory record. It includes EIOI evidence, boundary uncertainty, affected-nation law, human and Indigenous rights, animal-welfare implications, conflicts, alternatives, dissent, and review conditions.

### 7.3 Recognition

Only a competent legal authority may create `CERGTA-13`. BCT, PHC, a Regional Hub, a dashboard, or a pilot cannot “ratify” personhood. The recognition decision must specify exact rights, legal jurisdiction, conflicts, remedies, duration or amendment process, and non-derogation.

### 7.4 Boundary and title

The ecological entity and its legal boundary require `BIO-17`. Rights recognition does not transfer adjacent land, infrastructure, concessions, permits, resource title, or public authority. Title, tenure, rematriation, and compensation remain under CERGTA and Hearthstone.

### 7.5 No automatic rights escalation

The v1.0 Rights Escalation Ladder is retired as a self-executing mechanism. A degradation threshold may:

- trigger intensified monitoring;
- require a published review;
- support a rights-review recommendation;
- support a request for temporary urgent protection.

It cannot automatically create a veto, suspend a permit, close a facility, appoint a guardian, or order enforcement.

## 8. Stewardship, protected areas, and restoration

### 8.1 Claim and interest mapping

A protected-area, restoration, biocultural, or stewardship proposal starts with `BIO-21` and `BIO-22`. The process must identify customary uses, housing, workers, farmers, fishers, tenure, concessions, debt, tourism, food systems, sacred relations, animal-welfare interests, and transboundary effects.

### 8.2 Interim protection

Where irreversible loss is plausible, `BIO-23` may seek narrow non-prejudice protection. The request must be least restrictive, time limited, reviewed, and without prejudice to final rights, title, guilt, or compensation.

### 8.3 Stewardship charter

`BIO-24` defines the steward’s legal source, powers, duties, prohibited acts, affected-nation relationship, budget, participation, conflicts, transparency, review, succession, and dissolution.

### 8.4 Restoration plan

`BIO-25` includes ecological methods, Indigenous and local knowledge, workers and livelihoods, species and individual-animal welfare, food and water systems, accessibility, budget, monitoring, stopping conditions, and long-term maintenance.

### 8.5 Performance and correction

`BIO-26` separates stewardship performance from external shocks and inherited degradation. No score, model, or audit automatically suspends a steward or freezes funds. `BIO-27` requires notice, support, correction, independent review, interim relief, succession, and remedy.

### 8.6 Protected-area non-displacement

Conservation cannot be implemented through silent displacement, criminalization of customary subsistence, deprivation of essential services, or transfer of authority to an external NGO. Any restriction or relocation requires separate lawful authority, necessity, alternatives, compensation, livelihood continuity, appeal, and remedy.

## 9. Species, habitat, and precautionary interventions

### 9.1 Intervention review

`BIO-29` governs rewilding, predator reintroduction, invasive-species control, assisted migration, disease response, captive breeding, culling, contraception, habitat engineering, and analogous interventions. It requires necessity, alternatives, welfare analysis, affected-nation and community authority, uncertainty, monitoring, duration, review, and stopping rules.

### 9.2 Emerging technologies

`BIO-30` addresses gene drives, synthetic biology, de-extinction, large-scale geoengineering, autonomous ecological control systems, and other high-uncertainty interventions. Biodiversity institutions may recommend precautionary controls or a moratorium review. They cannot themselves authorize or prohibit the activity unless separately empowered by applicable law.

### 9.3 Precaution does not reverse the burden into guilt

A proponent may bear evidentiary duties before receiving a permit. That does not permit a biodiversity body to presume criminal liability, seize equipment, or impose punishment without a separate legal route.

## 10. UBES and stewardship rewards

### 10.1 Activity verification

`BIO-31` records the stewardship activity and ecological effect. It distinguishes:

- work performed;
- observed ecological change;
- causal attribution;
- participant identity;
- community or affected-nation verification;
- uncertainty and duplication risk;
- accessibility and low-tech evidence;
- correction status.

### 10.2 Financial decision

`BIO-32` routes the verified activity to the competent AUBI or financial authority. The receiving authority independently decides eligibility, amount, timing, withholding, overpayment, fraud review, appeal, and data disposition.

### 10.3 Non-retaliation and essential security

A person cannot lose essential income, water, food, housing, healthcare, legal protection, or community standing merely because ecological evidence is disputed, a record is corrected, or they decline further data collection.

## 11. Economic transformation

### 11.1 Harmful-subsidy reform

The source target of redirecting `$250 billion` is retained as an aspirational programme target, not a standing appropriation. `BIO-33` requires identification of legal budget authority, actual subsidy flows, beneficial owners, ecological effects, workers, communities, food and energy security, alternatives, transition finance, phase-out timing, review, and remedy.

### 11.2 Biodiversity grants and impact bonds

`BIO-34` separates programme recommendation from financial approval. Every instrument must disclose investor return, public guarantee, ecological method, distribution of benefits and risks, community and affected-nation authority, procurement, conflicts, failure conditions, audit, and exit.

An ecosystem, rights-holder status, sacred relationship, or future restoration obligation cannot be collateral.

### 11.3 Debt-for-nature and debt-for-stewardship agreements

`BIO-35` separates debt relief from ecological title and governance. A debt agreement cannot:

- waive affected-nation claims or consent;
- designate a protected area by itself;
- transfer land to the creditor or BCT;
- make an ecosystem collateral;
- create an automatic market sanction;
- eliminate review or remedy.

### 11.4 Bio-Credits and product passports

`BIO-36` separates:

1. method and baseline;
2. observed ecological claim;
3. verifier and audit;
4. certification decision;
5. product-passport field;
6. correction and expiry;
7. any later procurement or market decision.

A negative score is not a market-exclusion order.

### 11.5 Shadow pricing

Shadow pricing may illuminate ecological dependencies and distributional choices. It does not determine inherent worth, legal damages, compensation, title, permit conditions, taxes, or penalties without a separate lawful decision.

## 12. Procurement, trade, and supply-chain accountability

### 12.1 Transparency

Digital Product Passports and supply-chain records must disclose methodology, source, uncertainty, ownership, correction status, and scope. Protected Traditional Knowledge, personal data, whistleblower identity, and security-sensitive information remain restricted.

### 12.2 Restriction request

`BIO-37` may request a CERGTA-44 procurement, market, trade, listing, or delisting decision. The competent authority must independently establish jurisdiction, legal basis, individualized evidence, proportionality, alternatives, worker and community effects, essential-goods safeguards, notice, review, expiry, correction, and delisting.

### 12.3 No collective punishment

A restriction cannot punish an entire nation, Indigenous people, region, community, small producer class, or workforce because of an aggregate national or corporate score when the affected actors and supply paths can be distinguished.

## 13. Monitoring, inspection, investigation, and evidence

### 13.1 Monitoring

Routine ecological observation follows `BIO-03` and `BIO-04`. It creates no entry, search, production, or interrogation power.

### 13.2 Inspection

`BIO-38` requires lawful authority, exact premises or activity, purpose, notice or justified exception, scope, sampling, biosecurity, privacy, protected knowledge, report, challenge, and closure.

### 13.3 Investigation

`BIO-39` opens a distinct investigation into alleged illegal extraction, subsidy fraud, data manipulation, FPIC violation, biodiversity harm, or analogous conduct. Advocacy, monitoring, certification, and grant administration cannot silently become investigation.

### 13.4 Evidence and scientific attribution

`BIO-40` preserves custody, analytical methods, uncertainty, alternative causes, exculpatory material, access, challenge, correction, and admissibility. An AI classification, model output, or Bio-Credit score is not by itself proof of legal responsibility.

## 14. Ecocide and justice interface

### 14.1 No framework-created offence or universal penalty

Biodiversity v1.1 does not define a universally active ecocide crime, predetermine ICC jurisdiction, prescribe automatic `$1 million–$10 million` fines, or establish officer liability. Those questions belong to the competent legal system and the controlling law.

### 14.2 Adjudication referral

`BIO-43` identifies the allegation, legal theory, parties, affected ecosystems and communities, evidence, uncertainty, exculpatory material, requested remedy, jurisdictional questions, access restrictions, and challenge route. The recipient independently decides whether to accept the matter.

### 14.3 Enforcement

A judgment does not execute itself. Search, seizure, arrest, restraint, custody, force, cyber action, border action, and asset freezing require `CERGTA-48` and separate SCPA authority.

## 15. Whistleblowers and community monitors

`BIO-44` provides confidential reporting, anti-retaliation, legal assistance, livelihood support, safe contact, correction, and remedy. Financial rewards, where separately authorized, are not conditioned on a conviction and do not make an allegation more credible. Support does not require continued cooperation, confrontation, or public disclosure.

## 16. Urgent ecological protection and emergency assistance

### 16.1 Urgent protection

Where imminent irreversible harm is plausibly established, `BIO-41` may request `CERGTA-42`. The authorization must identify the exact legal authority, ecological entity, act, alternatives, affected people and workers, service continuity, duration, review, expiry, restoration, and remedy.

The source rule that `>10% habitat loss` automatically triggers a response is retired. A percentage may indicate urgency but cannot create jurisdiction.

### 16.2 Biodiversity Emergency Assistance Teams

The source term **Biodiversity SWAT Teams** is retired. `BIO-42` establishes optional civilian technical-assistance teams for ecological assessment, wildlife rescue, habitat stabilization, seed or genetic-material protection, community support, restoration planning, and logistics.

Teams:

- operate only after acceptance by a competent CDEE/ECRC command;
- remain within a written mission;
- follow affected-nation and host-jurisdiction law;
- cannot search, seize, arrest, detain, use force, gather intelligence, operate checkpoints, or command other agencies;
- have stop-work, worker-safety, termination, data-disposition, and remedy rules.

### 16.3 Emergency finance

No automatic five-percent fund release occurs. Emergency finance requires an identified competent authority, amount, purpose, custodian, procurement route, audit, expiry, correction, and restitution.

## 17. Cultural mobilization and public communication

Campaigns, restoration festivals, art residencies, education, games, storybanks, and public dashboards remain non-governance tools. They must not expose protected knowledge, prejudge legal cases, target dissenters, manipulate affected communities, or represent programme targets as legal facts.

## 18. Implementation pathway

### 18.1 Adoption states

Every mechanism is labelled as one of:

- `proposed`;
- `advisory`;
- `voluntarily_adopted`;
- `contractually_binding`;
- `locally_enacted`;
- `nationally_enacted`;
- `treaty_active`;
- `suspended`;
- `expired`;
- `terminated`.

### 18.2 Bootstrap phase

Regional and affected-nation-led initiatives may begin with voluntary monitoring, restoration, community grants, low-tech tools, legal support, and model-law development. They do not acquire personhood, guardian, market, investigation, or emergency powers merely because global institutions are absent.

### 18.3 Pilots

Pilots in Costa Rica, Bhutan, Palau, or elsewhere may test methods, participation, records, restoration, finance, and legal pathways under local authority. A pilot target such as five ecosystems with personhood is an evaluation target, not authority to create personhood.

### 18.4 Scaling

Scale follows demonstrated legitimacy, ecological benefit, rights protection, affected-nation authority, institutional capacity, correction, and remedy—not only percentages of land protected, score improvement, capital raised, or coalition size.

### 18.5 Coalition and treaty activation

A coalition of twenty or more nations may establish cooperative instruments among its members. Coalition size does not itself make BCT standards, rights recommendations, trade measures, or programme decisions binding. Each power must be enacted under the applicable legal route.

## 19. What success looks like under v1.1

### 19.1 A river seeks legal standing

Community and affected-nation petitioners submit `BIO-13`; EIOI evidence and dissent are recorded in `BIO-14`; the competent jurisdiction creates a rights decision; the legal boundary and guardian are separately constituted. Adjacent land and infrastructure do not silently transfer.

### 19.2 Traditional farming receives stewardship support

The farmer’s activity and ecological effect are verified in `BIO-31`. AUBI independently determines payment through `BIO-32`. Traditional Knowledge remains under the community’s rules, and payment is not conditioned on surrendering it.

### 19.3 A reef reaches a critical bleaching threshold

The threshold triggers enhanced monitoring, a published review, and—where imminent irreversible harm exists—an urgent-protection request. Permits are not automatically suspended, but the competent authority must consider the evidence promptly and explain its decision.

### 19.4 A palm-oil supply chain receives a negative ecological assessment

The product-passport field shows its method, source, uncertainty, and correction status. A procurement or market authority may begin a separate review. Workers, smallholders, food security, affected communities, and regenerative alternatives are considered before any restriction.

### 19.5 A community species count reveals a previously unseen decline

The teenager’s observation has epistemic value and enters `BIO-03`. It contributes to ecological interpretation and possibly a rights petition. It does not alone establish legal personhood, a criminal offence, or a payment.

### 19.6 A debt swap funds long-term restoration

The agreement reduces debt and finances restoration, but does not make the ecosystem collateral, waive affected-nation claims, or bypass a lawful protected-area or stewardship process.

## 25. Versioning, adoption, and compatibility

A jurisdiction adopts v1.1 only through a controlled instrument identifying the exact version and hash, local authority, powers activated, powers withheld, implementing bodies, transition from v1.0, open cases, data migration, review date, and withdrawal path.

A higher version number or similar title is not automatically compatible. Material changes to rights recognition, guardianship, Indigenous authority, finance, trade, investigation, emergency response, or enforcement require a new conformance review.

## 26. Transition from v1.0

The following v1.0 mechanisms are retired or reclassified:

- BCT ecosystem-personhood ratification → rights-review support only;
- BCT control of UBES outcomes → separate activity verification and AUBI decision;
- `20+ nations by 2040` binding authority → coalition instrument only where separately enacted;
- Regional Hub authority to adopt stricter binding standards → host-law or voluntary adoption only;
- Ecological & Welfare Ethics Board conflict resolution → advisory analysis only;
- automatic rights escalation and threshold veto → review or urgent-protection request;
- court-appointed guardians assumed by framework → competent CERGTA appointment;
- automatic Bio-Credit tariff rebate or surcharge → separate finance or trade decision;
- negative passport score causing market exclusion → separate CERGTA-44 route;
- predetermined ecocide penalties and officer liability → controlling law and independent adjudication;
- automatic Biodiversity SWAT Team deployment → civilian technical assistance under CDEE/ECRC;
- automatic five-percent emergency fund release → separate financial authorization;
- pilot, date, personhood-count, and coalition targets → non-activating programme metrics.

## 27. Closing doctrine

> **The biosphere needs standing, restoration, and enforceable protection. Those aims are weakened—not strengthened—when observation, rights, money, trade, emergency response, and punishment are compressed into one institution.**

> **Biodiversity v1.1 therefore makes ecological action stronger by making every authority visible: affected peoples can know who decided, under what law, from which evidence, with what limits, until when, and how to challenge, correct, terminate, and repair it.**
