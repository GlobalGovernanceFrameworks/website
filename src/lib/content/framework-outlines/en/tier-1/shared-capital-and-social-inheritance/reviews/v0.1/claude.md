**The biggest miss: Sundown Protocol v2.1**

Sundown isn't in the source freeze, isn't in §25's interfaces, and isn't in §26's migration list — yet it is the framework in the corpus that most directly acts *on* shared-capital institutions.

Sundown §4.4 commits "Pathfinder pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, and public investment vehicles" to coordinated divestment from non-compliant corporations, with shared timelines and coordinated announcements. Its escalation ladder reaches "Investment Freeze — Pathfinder public funds divest; new investment prohibited" at stage 4. It also has a Global Transition Standards Board supplying "sovereign wealth fund restructuring" advice, and cites Norway's Government Pension Fund as its precedent case.

That is one framework prescribing investment policy for the institutions this Protocol governs, on a compliance-score trigger. It collides directly with §8.1 ("an investment manager shall optimize only within the legally constituted mandate"), §3.10 ("indicators do not command investment"), and §8.5 on external influence. Structurally it's the same defect as the GSCL Dark Chain Tariff was for Adaptive Tax: an indicator converting straight into a consequence, with the domain framework rather than the fund's constituting instrument supplying the rule.

The disposition isn't necessarily "remove it" — a fund charter may lawfully adopt an exclusion mandate, and divestment coordination among *voluntarily participating* funds is a compact, not a command. But the Protocol should say which of the two it is, and §26 should carry the migration item.

**Pensions are misplaced, not just undecided**

§4.3 folds pension funds into "worker or member capital fund" and defers to applicable pension law; open question 29 then asks when a pension fund is in scope at all. But the taxonomy is off before the question arises. A pension claim is a *deferred individual capital claim with vesting* — structurally a sibling of social inheritance in §11, not of a collective fund in §4.1. §11 doesn't mention pensions except through question 21, and the two share almost every design problem: vesting age, portability across jurisdictions, creditor protection, restricted versus unrestricted use, and the interaction with means-tested benefits.

This matters for the Protocol's own thesis. Its opening claim is that ordinary people hold little productive capital — but in most developed economies, pension entitlements are the largest broadly-held claim on productive capital that exists. A Phase 0 map that lists "public pension assets" as one bullet among seventeen will understate the answer to the very question it's asking. §16.1 should foreground them, and §11 should either claim the lifecycle-asset comparison or say explicitly why pensions are excluded.

Worth noting the corpus treats pensions in a second role too: Aegis, Aethelred, and Adaptive Tax §11.4 all treat pensions as a protected continuity interest to be shielded during transitions. Both roles are real; the Protocol engages neither directly.

**Four existing funds the migration list misses**

§26 covers Pathfinder, Bioregional Polis, Hearthstone, Treaty, AUBI, Adaptive Tax, and the Planetary Hedge Fund class. It misses:

- **Regenerative Journeys v1.1** — a "BAZ Intergenerational Trust Fund" receiving 10% of the tourism levy, 50+ year horizon, Youth Council co-approval. That is simultaneously a §4.5 commons-return fund and a §4.8 intergenerational reserve, already specified with a capitalization rule.
- **Religious & Spiritual Dialogue v1.8** — a permanent endowment capitalized through the GCF, with an explicit *Waqf* reference as a non-state long-horizon precedent. The Waqf comparison is arguably more useful to §4.8 than anything currently in it.
- **Millennium Protocol v1.0** — "quantum-linked trust funds, managed by the Global Commons Fund" for time-indexed reparations. This one probably belongs in §26.7's retirement bucket, but it should be named rather than left floating.
- **Water & Sanitation v5.2.2** — this one cuts the other way. Its **Community Water Trust Fund** and record `WAT-16` already specify legal custodian, public purpose, eligible uses, segregated accounts, procurement, beneficial-ownership disclosure, audit, conflicts, reserves, insolvency, restitution, and dissolution. That is a working community-wealth-fund charter, more operational than §6's requirements list. It's a conformance precedent — the same role Technology Governance §26.1 played for Adaptive Tax — and §6 should be checked against it rather than developed in parallel.

**Public risk and shared upside: the downside is missing**

§7.5 is well-judged. Refusing a universal equity formula is right, and §7.6 blocking retroactive claims closes the obvious abuse.

But §7.5 enumerates only what the public *return* might be. It says nothing about who bears the loss. Public equity in early-stage ventures loses money on most positions — that is the actual empirical record, and it's the strongest objection to the Mazzucato case the section is drawing on. As drafted, the Protocol requires the constituting process to "consider" the return, and requires no statement of loss tolerance, no portfolio-level accounting rule, and no prohibition on reporting the winners without the write-offs. §24.7's failure gate asks whether the instruments "produce little public return," which measures the symptom but permits selective accounting on the way there.

One addition would close it: a public-equity or shared-upside arrangement should state its expected loss rate and be evaluated at portfolio level rather than by named success. Otherwise this is the section most likely to be quoted dishonestly, by advocates rather than opponents.

**One protocol or two**

One, but the seam is visible and worth naming rather than leaving implicit.

The case for one document is real: social inheritance has to be funded, custodied, invested, and wound down by something, and that something is a shared-capital institution. §5 function separation, §6 charters, §8 mandates, §10 beneficiary rights, §18 anti-capture, and §19 transparency all serve both halves. Splitting would duplicate roughly a third of the text.

But §11 runs eleven subsections and reads like an embedded protocol — and if the split ever happens, §11 and §12 come out as a clean unit while everything else stays. I'd state the joint explicitly in §1.2: one document because the endowment and the fund share custody, fiduciary, and beneficiary machinery, and separating them would duplicate the governance core. That converts an unexamined structural choice into a defended one, which is also what makes it revisable later.
