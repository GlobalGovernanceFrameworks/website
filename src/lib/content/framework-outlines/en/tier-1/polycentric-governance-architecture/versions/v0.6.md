# Preamble

Nested government is not automatically polycentric government.

A neighbourhood, district, municipality, region, state, and global institution may appear decentralized while remaining one chain of command. If the higher node controls finance, law, appointments, information, and appeal, lower autonomy can be withdrawn whenever it becomes inconvenient. Moving the centre closer to people does not by itself prevent the centre from becoming monopolistic.

Polycentrism requires **overlapping but bounded sources of authority**. The relevant boundaries do not all follow geography:

- public infrastructure and ordinary territorial services follow place;
- rivers, aquifers, forests, migratory species, atmosphere, and other commons cross political borders;
- professions, labour standards, supply chains, and practice communities cross territorial boundaries in different ways;
- Indigenous peoples possess inherent legal, territorial, cultural, spiritual, and knowledge authority that is not created by a GGF chamber;
- rights and Treaty obligations may constrain every one of these institutions;
- Meta-Governance coordinates their interfaces but does not become a superior sovereign.

Version 0.6 therefore retains the central insight of the earlier framework: separation of powers must be **horizontal as well as vertical**.

It rejects a stronger claim made in v0.5: no constitutional arrangement makes tyranny, capture, collusion, paralysis, or error “logistically impossible.” Overlap can distribute power, preserve alternative observation channels, and make capture harder. It can also create opacity, collusion across bodies, veto markets, professional closure, fragmented accountability, hidden domination, or permanent deadlock.

The purpose of this framework is not to declare the system capture-proof. It is to make authority:

- functionally bounded;
- mutually visible without requiring total legibility;
- challengeable from several directions;
- dependent on lawful standing;
- reversible where consequence is not final;
- continuous through institutional failure;
- unable to convert its own metric or diagnosis directly into unchecked power.

The governing maxim is:

> **No authority governs merely because it observes; no observer acquires jurisdiction merely because another institution fails; and no single territorial, commons, economic, coordinating, or epistemic body may treat the whole system as its domain.**

# 1. Purpose, status, and scope

## 1.1 Purpose

This framework defines the **constitutional position and interface rules** of overlapping institutions governing:

1. territory and public infrastructure;
2. shared ecological commons;
3. labour, professions, economic practices, and provider standards;
4. Indigenous sovereign authority;
5. coordination, adjudication, and Treaty backstops.

It is intended to prevent a Bioregional Autonomous Zone, municipality, commons institution, professional body, or coordination council from becoming a mini-state that controls every relevant function inside one aligned boundary.

## 1.2 Constitutional status

This framework is an architectural specification. It does not by itself create a government, Commons Trust, guild, Indigenous authority, tribunal, tax, currency, veto, police power, or binding public duty.

Every operative institution requires a valid source of authority, such as:

- democratic law;
- constitutional mandate;
- Treaty ratification;
- Indigenous law or covenant;
- a lawful compact among affected authorities;
- a rights-of-nature charter;
- a professional or worker charter;
- a tribunal order;
- voluntary agreement where no coercive power is exercised.

## 1.3 Normative language

- **Must** and **shall** identify requirements for conformance.
- **May** identifies a permitted design option.
- **Should** identifies a strong recommendation that may require contextual adaptation.
- **Illustrative** identifies an example without current legal or institutional authority.
- **Provisional proposal** identifies a mechanism requiring co-development, authorization, and testing.

## 1.4 Scope boundaries

This framework does not determine:

- who legitimately speaks for an Indigenous people;
- the internal law or governance of any Indigenous nation;
- the truth of Traditional Knowledge, sacred knowledge, scientific claims, or spiritual interpretation;
- the specific thresholds for ecological harm;
- Hearts issuance, reserve, redemption, or exchange;
- AUBI eligibility or payment;
- the detailed labour rights of Community Providers;
- criminal liability;
- military, police, or emergency doctrine;
- the current legality of an illustrative Swedish pilot.

Those matters belong to the authorities and frameworks constitutionally responsible for them.

## 1.5 Non-certification clause

The framework may assess whether a proposed interface is internally consistent with GGF commitments. It cannot certify:

- Indigenous authority;
- ecological legitimacy;
- professional competence;
- public-law validity;
- scientific consensus;
- community consent;
- legal compliance.

Such standing must be established through the relevant lawful or community-determined process.

# 2. Core constitutional commitments

## 2.1 Plural legitimacy

Authority does not arise from one source.

The system may contain:

- democratic territorial authority;
- inherent Indigenous sovereignty;
- rights held by persons and peoples;
- fiduciary duties toward ecosystems and future generations;
- authority delegated through a compact;
- professional and worker self-governance;
- Treaty competence;
- adjudicative authority.

No institution may absorb these sources into a single theory of permission.

## 2.2 Functional polycentrism

Polycentrism requires distinct authorities with partially overlapping jurisdictions and different accountability bases.

A territorial council should not control the river merely because the river passes through its territory. A Commons Trust should not set the whole economic policy of every community using the river. A guild should not decide zoning or ecosystem thresholds merely because its members perform the work. A Meta-Governance body should not acquire any of these powers because it coordinates their dispute.

## 2.3 Subsidiarity and interdependence

Decisions should remain at the lowest capable scale unless there is a material condition that cannot be governed there, including:

- cross-boundary harm;
- shared-commons degradation;
- violation of protected rights;
- inability to maintain essential continuity;
- valid Treaty competence;
- an Indigenous territorial or covenant obligation.

Moving authority upward or sideways requires the Economic or Constitutional Subsidiarity Test applicable to the decision.

## 2.4 Minimum necessary authority

Any authority created to resolve overlap shall be limited by:

- purpose;
- object;
- geography or affected relation;
- powers;
- information;
- duration;
- review;
- appeal;
- return or closure condition.

Interdependence is not a blank cheque.

## 2.5 Decision-chain separation

Where consequences are material, the following functions should remain institutionally distinguishable:

1. observing;
2. interpreting;
3. proposing;
4. authorizing;
5. implementing;
6. adjudicating;
7. evaluating outcomes;
8. restoring, reversing, or compensating.

The same body may perform more than one function where scale and risk justify it, but the concentration shall be disclosed and independently reviewable.

## 2.6 Indicators are alerts, not judgments

No score, dashboard, participation rate, veto frequency, ecological indicator, trust measure, wealth ratio, or algorithmic pattern directly establishes:

- capture;
- legitimacy;
- consent;
- guilt;
- institutional failure;
- authority transfer;
- dissolution;
- a financial penalty.

Indicators may trigger inquiry, convening, a State Estimate, or an authorized audit.

## 2.7 Protected continuity

Disputes, vetoes, sanctions, charter suspension, exit, or institutional dissolution shall protect, to the greatest extent consistent with safety and rights:

- life and bodily security;
- essential water, food, shelter, healthcare, care, and energy;
- AUBI Layer 1;
- lawfully held personal balances and earned compensation;
- continuity of essential Community Providers;
- access to challenge and appeal;
- Indigenous and community data control;
- records needed for restoration and accountability.

## 2.8 Protected non-transfer

A valid interface outcome may be that knowledge, data, authority, or meaning **does not cross the boundary**.

A community or authority may instead provide:

- consent;
- refusal;
- a condition;
- a bounded conclusion;
- a veto;
- an attestation;
- a statement that a threshold has or has not been met.

Protected knowledge need not become public merely because another institution wants to audit the decision.

## 2.9 Legitimate unresolved states

Not every disagreement has a single authoritative resolution.

A valid outcome may be:

- continued negotiation;
- maintenance of the status quo;
- precautionary suspension;
- separate local implementations;
- stable coexistence;
- federation with limited interoperability;
- a tribunal referral;
- lawful refusal;
- recognition that no institution currently possesses authority to decide.

# 3. Constitutional topology

## 3.1 Territorial public authority — the “Where”

Territorial authorities govern place-based public functions within valid jurisdiction.

Possible forms include:

- neighbourhood councils;
- municipalities;
- BAZ assemblies;
- regional public authorities;
- states;
- Treaty parties acting collectively within delegated competence.

Typical responsibilities may include:

- land use and zoning;
- housing and local infrastructure;
- roads and public space;
- ordinary local services;
- local safety within law;
- public budgets;
- local provider chartering;
- democratic participation.

A territorial authority does not automatically control:

- cross-boundary ecosystems;
- Indigenous territories or law;
- professional standards beyond its competence;
- external communities affected by its decisions;
- shared planetary commons.

## 3.2 Commons institutions — the “What is shared”

Commons institutions govern ecological or material relations that cross territorial boundaries or require fiduciary stewardship beyond ordinary ownership.

Possible objects include:

- rivers and watersheds;
- aquifers;
- forests and habitat corridors;
- fisheries and migratory species;
- coastal and ocean systems;
- atmosphere and climate-related commons;
- shared knowledge or digital infrastructure where separately chartered.

A Commons Trust or equivalent body shall identify:

- the commons governed;
- its ecological or functional boundary;
- affected territorial authorities;
- affected Indigenous peoples and rights;
- downstream, upstream, future, and non-human interests;
- powers and prohibitions;
- evidence methods;
- Guardian roles;
- funding;
- challenge and appeal;
- review and dissolution rules.

A commons institution may set or enforce duties within its charter. It does not become a general territorial government.

## 3.3 Practice, labour, and professional institutions — the “How”

Practice institutions govern standards arising from work, profession, craft, provider operation, or economic practice.

Possible forms include:

- worker guilds;
- unions and syndicates;
- professional colleges;
- Fractal Labor Parliament bodies;
- cooperative federations;
- Community Provider associations;
- accreditation and safety bodies.

Their valid functions may include:

- minimum labour and safety standards;
- professional competence;
- ethical practice;
- worker representation;
- collective bargaining;
- portable capability credentials;
- provider capacity coordination;
- proposals concerning compensation.

They do not automatically control:

- Hearts issuance or redemption;
- territorial budgets;
- zoning;
- commons thresholds;
- public criminal sanctions;
- access to AUBI Layer 1.

Work in Liberation governs their labour and provider architecture. Financial Systems governs financial settlement.

## 3.4 Indigenous sovereign authority — inherent jurisdiction, not a GGF chamber

Indigenous peoples and nations possess authority that arises outside the GGF.

Their authority may concern:

- territory and land relations;
- law and governance;
- consent;
- treaties and covenants;
- cultural continuity;
- sacred sites and relations;
- knowledge and data;
- community membership and representation;
- stewardship obligations;
- remedies and restitution.

The GGF shall not create a universal Indigenous Wisdom Council, impose a nomination or sortition formula, assign representation quotas, appoint “Traditional Knowledge proxies,” or certify who speaks for an Indigenous people.

An affected people may choose to act through:

- its existing government;
- elders or knowledge holders;
- a council of nations;
- a territorial authority;
- a project-specific consent body;
- a covenant institution;
- another Indigenous-determined form.

The institution’s standing remains specific to the people, territory, issue, and process that constituted it.

## 3.5 Guardians and rights-of-nature roles

A rights-of-nature or commons charter may establish Guardians with fiduciary responsibility toward an ecosystem or non-human relation.

Guardians may include persons with:

- ecological expertise;
- long-term place-based observation;
- Indigenous authority or knowledge where the affected people choose participation;
- downstream or future-generation standing;
- legal fiduciary responsibility.

No universal composition is required.

Guardian authority shall be limited to the chartered object. A Guardian does not become a general representative of “nature,” Indigenous peoples, science, or future generations.

## 3.6 Meta-Governance — the interface, not a fifth power centre

Meta-Governance may:

- map jurisdictional overlap;
- convene affected authorities;
- maintain interface records;
- facilitate negotiation and mediation;
- support plural state estimates;
- identify possible blind spots;
- host bounded pilots where authorized;
- monitor the integrity of the coordination process.

It may not by itself:

- override a territorial, commons, practice, Indigenous, Treaty, or tribunal authority;
- determine capture;
- transfer jurisdiction;
- impose sanctions;
- command emergency operations;
- dissolve institutions.

## 3.7 Treaty institutions and tribunals

Treaty institutions possess only competences activated under Treaty for Our Only Home.

Tribunals may decide disputes where valid jurisdiction exists, including rights, Treaty obligations, due process, externalities, and charter compliance. They shall not determine the metaphysical validity of sacred or Traditional Knowledge or replace Indigenous law in matters reserved to Indigenous jurisdiction.

# 4. Horizontal and vertical separation of powers

## 4.1 Horizontal separation

No territorial, commons, practice, Indigenous-interface, or coordinating body should control every relevant function in a consequential decision.

Horizontal checks may include:

- commons review of territorial projects;
- territorial ratification of public expenditure;
- worker refusal of unsafe or exploitative conditions;
- Indigenous consent or covenant conditions;
- tribunal review;
- public challenge;
- independent evidence and outcome evaluation.

## 4.2 Vertical separation

Neighbourhood, BAZ, bioregional, state, and Treaty scales should not form one automatic hierarchy.

A higher scale may act only where:

- authority has been delegated;
- a valid shared-commons or rights competence applies;
- the lower level requests support;
- a lawful failure-to-act threshold is met;
- a tribunal or Treaty provision authorizes intervention.

## 4.3 No unilateral cross-domain action

A decision affects another domain when it materially changes:

- the commons;
- another territory;
- Indigenous rights or territory;
- labour rights or professional safety;
- protected personal or community rights;
- Treaty obligations.

The initiating authority shall then issue a Polycentric Interface Record and invite every authority with valid standing.

## 4.4 Redundancy without duplication

Redundancy can reduce single-point failure when multiple institutions observe or constrain the same risk from different positions.

It becomes waste or confusion when bodies duplicate the same mandate, data, or veto without distinct accountability.

Every overlapping institution should state:

- what unique signal it preserves;
- what power it constrains;
- what failure it can detect;
- what it cannot decide;
- how conflict with another body is resolved.

## 4.5 No capture-proof claim

The architecture does not make capture impossible.

Capture may occur through:

- collusion among several bodies;
- control of shared data infrastructure;
- professional gatekeeping;
- donor or contractor dependence;
- manipulation of appointments;
- suppression of dissent;
- strategic veto exchange;
- fragmentation that makes responsibility untraceable;
- false claims of Indigenous, ecological, technical, or public standing.

The architecture is successful when capture is harder to coordinate, easier to challenge, less able to monopolize every remedy, and more reversible—not when it is declared impossible.

# 5. Jurisdiction mapping and decision classes

## 5.1 Jurisdiction map

Every polycentric arrangement shall maintain a public, accessible jurisdiction map stating:

- the institutions present;
- their authority source;
- objects and territories governed;
- powers;
- prohibitions;
- information held;
- interfaces;
- review and appeal routes;
- continuity arrangements.

Protected Indigenous, personal, cultural, or security information need not appear in the public map.

## 5.2 Decision Class I — primarily local matters

A local territorial authority normally decides matters whose material effects remain within its jurisdiction and do not infringe protected rights or reserved authority.

Other bodies may advise but do not gain veto merely because they have an interest.

## 5.3 Decision Class II — voluntary coordination

Authorities may form compacts for:

- shared services;
- mutual aid;
- joint procurement;
- interoperability;
- research;
- voluntary standards;
- pooled capacity.

The compact shall define entry, exit, costs, data, decision rights, disputes, and dissolution.

## 5.4 Decision Class III — cross-boundary externalities

Where one authority’s action causes material harm outside its jurisdiction, affected authorities gain standing.

The process shall identify:

- harm;
- causal uncertainty;
- affected parties;
- available mitigation;
- burden allocation;
- rights;
- authority for restraint or remedy.

## 5.5 Decision Class IV — shared commons

The commons follows ecological or functional boundaries rather than political convenience.

A valid commons institution may possess binding authority if constituted through law, compact, Treaty, rights-of-nature arrangement, Indigenous law, or another legitimate source.

## 5.6 Decision Class V — fundamental rights and international crimes

Rights and international-crime jurisdiction are governed by applicable law and Treaty. Polycentric autonomy does not protect a local institution from lawful review for rights violations.

## 5.7 Decision Class VI — Indigenous territory, knowledge, culture, and sacred relations

State or BAZ membership does not replace affected-nation consent.

Where an action implicates Indigenous territory, rights, knowledge, culture, sacred relations, or covenant authority, the interface shall follow Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing.

# 6. Cross-boundary Commons Trusts and equivalent institutions

## 6.1 Why commons cross borders

Watersheds, aquifers, fisheries, forests, habitat corridors, atmosphere, coastlines, and migratory species do not conform to administrative borders.

If each territory governs only the segment inside its border, cumulative extraction and downstream harm may remain ungoverned. A cross-boundary commons institution creates a venue in which the relation itself is represented.

## 6.2 Charter requirements

A Commons Trust or equivalent institution shall specify:

1. commons object and boundary;
2. constituting authorities;
3. affected peoples and territories;
4. Indigenous standing and consent where relevant;
5. Guardian roles;
6. scientific, local, and long-term observation channels;
7. extraction, pollution, restoration, or access powers;
8. humanitarian and rights minima;
9. funding;
10. transparency and protected knowledge;
11. dispute and appeal;
12. emergency authority;
13. review, amendment, and dissolution;
14. restoration obligations.

## 6.3 Guardian disagreement

Disagreement among Indigenous authorities, knowledge holders, scientists, residents, workers, and Guardians shall be surfaced rather than averaged into a false consensus.

The process may use:

- joint deliberation;
- separate evidence statements;
- independent replication;
- seasonal or adaptive limits;
- precautionary suspension;
- a bounded jury or tribunal where legally authorized;
- an Indigenous determination within Indigenous jurisdiction;
- continued unresolved status.

A sortition jury shall not decide whether sacred or Traditional Knowledge is metaphysically true. It may decide a legally assigned question using the authority and evidence rules applicable to that question.

## 6.4 Precautionary default

Where proposed activity risks serious or irreversible harm and evidence remains materially uncertain, the burden may remain with the proponent.

The default may be:

- denial;
- delay;
- a smaller reversible pilot;
- seasonal restriction;
- stronger monitoring;
- restoration bond;
- limited authorization.

Precaution shall not become a universal pretext for indefinite institutional obstruction. The record shall state the harm feared, uncertainty, review date, and evidence that could change the decision.

## 6.5 Rights and material minima

Commons enforcement shall not use deprivation of basic water, food, health, shelter, or essential energy as an ordinary bargaining instrument.

Restrictions on non-essential use, extraction, or commercial access require lawful authority, notice, proportionality, challenge, and protection of affected populations.

# 7. Practice, labour, and economic governance

## 7.1 Guilds and practice bodies

The source framework used “Guilds/Syndicates” for the economic-practice layer. v0.6 retains the category while allowing several institutional forms.

A practice body may:

- propose minimum compensation standards;
- establish professional and safety requirements;
- coordinate training and credentials;
- represent workers;
- bargain collectively;
- refuse unsafe or exploitative work;
- propose provider-capacity measures.

## 7.2 Price and compensation handshake

Where public budgets or Hearts-funded services are involved, a practice body may propose compensation standards while the relevant territorial or commissioning authority decides public expenditure within law.

The negotiation shall disclose:

- worker costs and hidden labour;
- material and safety requirements;
- provider operating costs;
- public affordability;
- scarcity and capacity;
- effects on access;
- alternatives.

Neither side may use AUBI Layer 1, personal balances, or essential services as leverage against workers or residents.

## 7.3 Anti-cartel safeguards

Practice self-governance can become professional closure or monopoly.

Safeguards may include:

- open and proportionate membership criteria;
- recognition of equivalent competence;
- appeal against certification denial;
- public-interest participation;
- transparent compensation reasoning;
- anti-discrimination;
- provider plurality;
- periodic review;
- independent competition or public-interest oversight where applicable.

## 7.4 Worker rights

No polycentric architecture overrides the Worker Bill of Rights in Work in Liberation.

Workers retain rights concerning:

- voluntary participation;
- refusal of unsafe work;
- compensation;
- scheduling and rest;
- representation;
- complaint;
- anti-retaliation;
- privacy;
- exit;
- challenge to algorithmic matching or evaluation.

## 7.5 Financial boundary

Practice bodies do not issue, redeem, freeze, or alter Hearts or Leaves unless Financial Systems expressly assigns that function.

A strike, boycott, or refusal may be a lawful labour action. It is not authority to alter another person’s balance or exchange rate.

# 8. Indigenous sovereignty interface

## 8.1 Recognition rather than integration

Indigenous authority is not a fourth chamber in a GGF-designed constitution.

The polycentric system recognizes that an Indigenous people may exercise territorial, legal, ecological, economic, cultural, and spiritual authority in an integrated form that does not match the territorial–commons–practice distinction.

The GGF distinction is an interface tool for GGF institutions. It shall not fragment Indigenous governance.

## 8.2 Affected-nation standing

Where a decision implicates an Indigenous people, the interface shall identify:

- the affected people or peoples;
- the authority recognized through their process;
- the object of consent or refusal;
- territorial and knowledge implications;
- internal plurality or contested standing;
- non-participating communities;
- conditions, duration, and withdrawal.

## 8.3 No proxy authority

Where no Indigenous authority has chosen to participate, the system shall not appoint allied ethicists, diaspora figures, neighbouring communities, scholars, or “Traditional Knowledge proxies” to impersonate consent.

Ecological and ethical governance may continue through other legitimate institutions, but it shall not be labelled Indigenous authority.

## 8.4 Covenant and veto

An Indigenous authority may, through its law or a valid covenant, grant, condition, suspend, or withdraw authorization.

A veto or suspension shall identify, to the extent permitted by protected knowledge:

- the authority;
- object;
- scope;
- prohibited or conditioned act;
- consequence;
- review or restoration pathway.

The consequence should target the relevant activity, permit, charter authority, or covenant authorization. It shall not automatically erase personal balances, AUBI Layer 1, wages, essential providers, or unrelated third-party rights.

## 8.5 Protected knowledge

The authority may rely on knowledge that is not disclosed to the other institutions. A bounded attestation may be sufficient where the governing covenant or law recognizes it.

Public transparency applies to the institution’s authority, procedure, and consequence—not necessarily to the sacred or protected basis.

# 9. Polycentric decision process

## 9.1 Normal operations

Each institution acts independently within its valid jurisdiction.

Routine decisions do not require every other institution’s approval.

## 9.2 Overlap trigger

An overlap process begins when a proposed decision materially affects another valid domain.

The initiating body prepares a Polycentric Interface Record stating:

- proposal;
- authority;
- decision class;
- affected domains;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- rights;
- urgency;
- requested decision;
- deadline;
- appeal route.

## 9.3 Standing review

Participation in the interface depends on standing, not general interest alone.

Standing may arise from:

- territorial effect;
- rights;
- Indigenous authority;
- commons charter;
- worker or professional responsibility;
- Treaty competence;
- contractual or compact obligation;
- direct material impact.

## 9.4 Negotiation and mediation

The first response should normally be direct negotiation among affected authorities.

Meta-Governance may facilitate without imposing a settlement.

## 9.5 State Estimate Record

Where factual or causal disagreement is material, each major interpretation should be recorded separately, including:

- claimed state;
- observation source;
- model or reasoning;
- uncertainty;
- blind spots;
- missing populations;
- alternative explanations;
- evidence that could falsify or change the estimate.

## 9.6 Decision and reasons

The authorized body shall identify:

- jurisdiction;
- decision class;
- reasons;
- dissent;
- conditions;
- duration;
- review;
- restoration obligations.

# 10. Deadlock and conflict resolution

## 10.1 Deadlock is not automatically failure

Deadlock may indicate:

- incompatible rights;
- uncertainty;
- an under-specified compact;
- lack of authority;
- insufficient capacity;
- strategic obstruction;
- a legitimate refusal;
- a decision that should not proceed.

The goal is not always to force a decision.

## 10.2 Escalation ladder

A compact may use an escalation sequence such as:

1. direct negotiation;
2. facilitated mediation;
3. independent technical or community review;
4. participatory or representative deliberation;
5. bounded sortition jury where authorized;
6. arbitration or tribunal;
7. maintenance of status quo or precautionary suspension;
8. lawful exit, redesign, or federation.

Timeouts may advance a matter to review. They do not automatically create authority in the next body.

## 10.3 Sortition jury

A sortition jury may resolve a defined dispute only when:

- a law, charter, or compact grants it that authority;
- the question is suitable for public judgment;
- participants are selected independently and accessibly;
- affected rights and Indigenous jurisdiction are protected;
- evidence and dissent are presented fairly;
- conflicts of interest are disclosed;
- reasons are published;
- appeal or review exists where required.

## 10.4 Limits of jury authority

A jury shall not automatically:

- dissolve every institution involved in a deadlock;
- assume general government;
- invalidate Indigenous authority;
- determine criminal guilt outside lawful procedure;
- alter personal balances;
- impose permanent economic sanctions;
- decide sacred meaning or cultural authenticity.

A jury may, if the constituting law expressly permits, decide the bounded issue, require reconsideration, recommend institutional review, or activate a separately authorized recall process.

## 10.5 Institutional review and dissolution

Persistent deadlock may justify review of an institution’s mandate, composition, or procedure. Dissolution requires the authority and continuity process governing that institution.

A decision on the underlying dispute and a decision to dissolve an institution are separate decisions.

# 11. Emergency coordination

## 11.1 Emergency scope

Emergency measures apply only to an immediate or rapidly developing threat requiring action before ordinary process can operate.

Examples may include:

- flood;
- fire;
- acute contamination;
- epidemic;
- infrastructure failure;
- imminent violence.

## 11.2 Provisional authority

A local or compact emergency authority may take narrow, reversible measures within its lawful mandate.

Under Treaty, provisional cross-Treaty action shall not exceed 72 hours without the required continuation process.

## 11.3 Protected powers and rights

Emergency coordination shall not by itself:

- create permanent jurisdiction;
- suspend Indigenous sovereignty;
- permanently waive consent;
- remove AUBI Layer 1;
- compel work outside lawful emergency-service authority;
- dissolve institutions;
- convert temporary data access into permanent surveillance;
- use basic necessities as bargaining leverage.

## 11.4 Emergency Polycentric Record

The record shall state:

- threat;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- authorized body;
- measures;
- affected domains;
- rights and protected minima;
- duration;
- review;
- termination;
- restoration and compensation.

# 12. Capture, integrity, and observability

## 12.1 Capture as a hypothesis

Capture is a claim about the relationship between authority, incentives, information, and outcomes. It is not established by a single threshold.

Possible warning signs include:

- concentrated decisions;
- asymmetric transparency;
- prohibitive exit;
- self-perpetuating mandates;
- conflicts of interest;
- unexplained wealth changes;
- suppression of challenge;
- repeated source dependence;
- systematic rights violations;
- collusion across bodies;
- capture of shared infrastructure.

## 12.2 Observation Contract

Every consequential integrity-monitoring system shall state:

- decision supported;
- observations collected;
- populations and institutions covered;
- blind spots;
- source dependencies;
- prohibited uses;
- retention;
- correction;
- access;
- independent review;
- sensor-change process.

## 12.3 Observation Independence Map

The map identifies whether apparently separate signals depend on the same:

- administrative database;
- provider;
- contractor;
- sensor network;
- funder;
- Community Weaver;
- political authority;
- model;
- data standard.

Three dashboards fed by one source do not provide three independent observations.

## 12.4 Missing-population analysis

Low participation can mean exclusion, refusal, privacy, fatigue, distrust, disability barriers, language barriers, or satisfaction outside the system.

Every audit should ask who is absent and why.

## 12.5 Integrity response ladder

Indicators may lead to:

1. clarification;
2. data-quality review;
3. independent audit;
4. affected-party hearing;
5. temporary reversible safeguards;
6. authorized corrective action;
7. tribunal or recall process;
8. lawful institutional redesign or dissolution.

No algorithm moves directly from indicator to sanction.

# 13. Polycentric Conditions Dashboard

## 13.1 No composite Polycentric Health Index

v0.5 combined power distribution, ecological health, LMCI, and economic equality into one score. v0.6 retires that composite.

A system can perform well on one dimension while failing badly on another. Aggregation can hide veto domination, ecological harm, coercive participation, or unequal rights behind an acceptable total.

## 13.2 Separate dimensions

### A. Authority distribution

- concentration of consequential decisions;
- jurisdiction overlap;
- unreviewed unilateral action;
- diversity of veto and challenge routes;
- dependence on one secretariat or platform.

### B. Rights and legitimacy

- due process;
- refusal and exit;
- Indigenous consent;
- non-discrimination;
- access to appeal;
- protection of non-participants.

### C. Commons condition

- ecological state estimates;
- restoration;
- extraction;
- uncertainty;
- downstream and future effects;
- Guardian independence.

### D. Worker and practice integrity

- worker authority;
- compensation;
- safety;
- professional closure;
- provider concentration;
- strike and complaint rights.

### E. Coordination quality

- time to identify standing;
- quality of interface records;
- unresolved disputes;
- implementation;
- burden of coordination;
- preservation of local discretion.

### F. Observability and privacy

- source independence;
- missing populations;
- correction;
- protected non-transfer;
- data minimization;
- surveillance risk.

### G. Continuity and reversibility

- essential-service continuity;
- reversibility of temporary measures;
- successful authority return;
- restoration after error;
- institutional closure without rights loss.

## 13.3 Interpretation

No target veto frequency, decision-share percentage, participation rate, or score proves health.

Patterns are interpreted through context, rights, authority, and outcomes.

# 14. Bioregional Autonomous Zones

## 14.1 BAZ as territorial container, not total government

A BAZ may provide the territorial public-authority component of a polycentric arrangement.

It does not automatically control:

- Commons Trusts crossing its boundary;
- guilds or practice institutions;
- Indigenous peoples or territories;
- Treaty institutions;
- external communities affected by its metabolism.

## 14.2 Boundary non-alignment

Territorial, commons, practice, and Indigenous boundaries may intentionally differ.

This preserves signals and responsibilities that would disappear if every function were collapsed into one map.

## 14.3 Fractal adaptation

The logical functions can exist at different scales without requiring identical institutions.

A small community may use:

- one assembly;
- separately mandated role holders;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- external appeal;
- periodic independent review.

A larger BAZ may support distinct councils and secretariats.

Scale thresholds should reflect workload, risk, competence, and independence—not population alone.

## 14.4 Capacity-first principle

Where a community cannot staff independent institutions, the first response should be:

- shared services;
- rotating roles;
- inter-BAZ support;
- independent external review;
- temporary delegation;
- capacity grants.

The response should not be permanent centralization by default.

# 15. Urban metabolic federations

## 15.1 Cities as metabolic nodes

Cities depend on watersheds, foodsheds, energysheds, sewersheds, labour systems, digital infrastructure, and distant ecological relations.

Urban polycentrism therefore maps the flows connecting the city to producer, steward, and affected communities.

## 15.2 Federation compact

An urban–rural or inter-BAZ metabolic compact should state:

- flows covered;
- providers and affected communities;
- ecological limits;
- infrastructure responsibilities;
- financial settlement;
- solidarity obligations;
- humanitarian minima;
- data and observation;
- disputes;
- exit and continuity.

## 15.3 No automatic reputation sanctions

A payment or ecological dispute shall not automatically:

- lower Hearts exchange rates;
- increase trade prices across the network;
- trigger capital flight;
- isolate the defaulting population;
- publish a generalized trust score.

Authorized remedies may include notice, mediation, limited service conditions, escrow, restoration duties, lawful financial claims, or tribunal enforcement.

## 15.4 Biophysical leverage and rights

Physical control over water, food, energy, or transport creates power. It is not merely “accounts payable enforcement.”

Any restriction requires:

- lawful authority;
- proportionality;
- humanitarian minima;
- protection of vulnerable populations;
- notice where possible;
- challenge;
- restoration.

# 16. Technical and information architecture

## 16.1 Technology neutrality

The framework does not require blockchain, a global ledger, a central server, or a single identity system.

Possible implementations include:

- ordinary public records;
- federated databases;
- paper and offline processes;
- signed attestations;
- distributed ledgers where justified;
- local data stores;
- interoperable APIs.

## 16.2 Separation without fragmentation

Separate authorities may maintain separate records. Cross-interface systems should exchange only the data needed for the shared decision.

Separation shall not make it impossible for affected people to understand who decided, on what basis, and how to appeal.

## 16.3 Institutional transparency and personal privacy

Public institutions should disclose authority, budgets, decisions, conflicts, and reasons.

This does not justify total legibility of:

- workers;
- residents;
- Indigenous communities;
- informal carers;
- dissenters;
- sacred knowledge;
- personal economic activity.

## 16.4 Algorithmic limits

Pattern-recognition systems may flag possible anomalies. They may not independently determine:

- capture;
- fraud;
- Indigenous standing;
- ecosystem harm;
- professional misconduct;
- sanctions;
- jurisdiction;
- dissolution.

# 17. Swedish pilot pathway

## 17.1 Status of this section

This section preserves the source framework’s Swedish pilot orientation. It is an architectural proposal, not current legal advice.

Any implementation requires current verification of:

- municipal competence;
- administrative and procurement law;
- environmental law;
- constitutional protections;
- Sámi rights and representative standing;
- labour and cooperative law;
- data protection;
- Treaty and EU obligations.

## 17.2 Pilot principle

Begin with low-coercion, reversible interfaces rather than declaring a municipality to be a fully constituted BAZ.

Suitable pilot domains may include:

- watershed monitoring;
- joint restoration planning;
- cross-municipal emergency exercises;
- public-space stewardship;
- worker consultation in a municipal service;
- participatory review of one cross-boundary infrastructure decision.

## 17.3 Illustrative pilot sequence

### Stage 1 — jurisdiction and relationship mapping

- map territorial authorities;
- identify the commons;
- identify affected Indigenous peoples and rights;
- identify workers and providers;
- map existing legal authority;
- identify absent or unrepresented populations.

### Stage 2 — voluntary interface

- create a non-binding coordination forum;
- agree an Observation Contract;
- test shared evidence and disagreement records;
- establish accessible public participation;
- protect non-participation.

### Stage 3 — bounded compact

- authorize one shared function;
- define costs, powers, and exit;
- establish independent review;
- test a limited Guardian or worker role where lawful;
- protect continuity.

### Stage 4 — evaluation

- evaluate outcome, rights, burden, source independence, and reversibility;
- publish dissent;
- decide whether to end, revise, federate, or expand.

### Stage 5 — formalization

Any transfer of public authority requires the law and democratic process applicable at that time. Indigenous participation or consent remains separately determined by affected Indigenous authorities.

## 17.4 Lake Mälaren illustration

A Lake Mälaren pilot could explore a cross-municipal commons interface involving:

- water quality;
- fish and habitat conditions;
- extraction and pollution pressures;
- upstream and downstream municipalities;
- scientists and long-term local observers;
- affected workers and users;
- Indigenous rights or knowledge where an affected Indigenous authority identifies standing and chooses engagement.

The framework does not presume that Sámi representatives should be appointed as generic Guardians for every Swedish watershed. Standing must be established rather than inferred from identity alone.

## 17.5 Pilot success

Success is not the number of councils created.

A pilot succeeds when it:

- clarifies authority;
- improves a real outcome;
- preserves rights;
- reveals disagreement honestly;
- avoids compulsory legibility;
- remains affordable;
- returns or closes authority cleanly;
- generates evidence useful for the next decision.

# 18. Installation, transition, and closure

## 18.1 Installation record

Before activation, each institution shall publish or preserve as appropriate:

- constituting authority;
- mandate;
- members or selection process;
- affected parties;
- rights;
- powers;
- prohibitions;
- funding;
- data;
- challenge;
- review;
- continuity;
- closure.

## 18.2 Existing institutions

Polycentrism should normally begin by mapping and connecting existing institutions rather than replacing them all at once.

Possible transitions include:

- an existing watershed body receiving a stronger charter;
- worker cooperatives forming a federation;
- a municipality delegating one shared service;
- a rights-of-nature Guardian arrangement;
- an Indigenous–public-authority covenant;
- a BAZ pilot using existing public-law forms.

## 18.3 Bypasses and experiments

A temporary institution shall operate under the Bypass Charter requirements in Meta-Governance.

Possible outcomes include:

- closure;
- reintegration;
- federation;
- stable coexistence;
- bounded transfer;
- lawful succession.

## 18.4 Dissolution

Institutional closure shall distinguish:

- ending the institution;
- preserving valid protocols;
- transferring records;
- protecting rights;
- maintaining essential services;
- resolving assets and liabilities;
- restoring authority;
- compensating harm.

# 20. Stress tests and open design questions

## 20.1 Coordinated capture

Can territorial, commons, practice, and data institutions collude while appearing formally separate?

Required tests:

- funding dependence;
- shared contractors;
- appointment overlap;
- data-source overlap;
- retaliation against challengers;
- provider and media concentration;
- external appeal viability.

## 20.2 Veto market

Can actors trade or withhold vetoes for unrelated advantage?

Required safeguards:

- object-specific veto authority;
- reasons and protected attestations;
- conflict disclosure;
- anti-retaliation;
- review;
- prohibition on unrelated personal benefit.

## 20.3 Professional closure

Can a guild exclude newcomers, migrants, informal practitioners, disabled workers, or rival approaches?

Required tests:

- membership pathways;
- equivalence recognition;
- appeal;
- subgroup outcomes;
- competence versus protectionism.

## 20.4 Commons domination

Can ecological institutions impose severe human burdens without representing affected populations?

Required tests:

- distributional impact;
- rights minima;
- Indigenous standing;
- worker and livelihood transition;
- alternatives;
- restoration and solidarity.

## 20.5 Territorial populism

Can a majority territorial body override minorities, future interests, workers, or downstream communities?

Required tests:

- rights review;
- externality analysis;
- affected-party standing;
- independent adjudication.

## 20.6 Meta-Governance expansion

Can the coordinator become indispensable, own the shared data, define every interface, or benefit from diagnosing fragmentation?

Required tests:

- authority source;
- data portability;
- alternative coordinators;
- sunset;
- budget incentives;
- refusal and exit.

## 20.7 Sortition legitimacy

Does a jury possess enough standing, time, access, independence, and review to decide the assigned question?

A random sample is not automatically legitimate or uncapturable.

## 20.8 Emergency normalization

Are temporary emergency interfaces becoming routine government?

Track:

- cumulative duration;
- repeated renewal;
- powers retained;
- data retained;
- rights affected;
- restoration completed.

## 20.9 Small-community role collapse

When several functions are held by the same people, can conflicts of interest be managed through external review, rotation, and transparent role switching?

## 20.10 Migratory and planetary commons

How should authority be constituted where the commons crosses many legal systems and no single affected community can authorize the whole arrangement?

This remains a major design dependency for domain-specific frameworks.

# Conclusion

The central contribution of Polycentric Governance Architecture remains the distinction among the **where**, the **shared what**, and the **how** of collective life—and the recognition that Indigenous sovereignty cannot be reduced to one chamber inside that architecture.

Version 0.6 makes four constitutional corrections.

First, overlap is a protection, not a guarantee. It can preserve independent signals and constrain unilateral action, but it can also create collusion, opacity, veto exchange, or paralysis.

Second, the institutions do not all derive authority from the same system. Indigenous sovereignty is recognized rather than installed. Commons authority requires a charter. Practice authority does not become monetary authority. Meta-Governance remains an interface.

Third, diagnosis and consequence are separated. A metric can raise a question. A jury can decide a bounded issue where authorized. A Guardian can exercise a chartered power. None of these mechanisms automatically dissolves chambers, isolates economies, changes exchange rates, or transfers jurisdiction.

Fourth, the architecture protects the possibility that the correct outcome is not integration. A community may refuse. Two systems may coexist. A commons decision may remain precautionarily suspended. A pilot may close. An institution may return authority.

The framework’s final principle is therefore:

> **Polycentrism does not eliminate power. It prevents one map of power from becoming the only map, and it builds lawful interfaces through which overlapping authorities can constrain, challenge, and coordinate one another without collapsing into a single sovereign centre.**
