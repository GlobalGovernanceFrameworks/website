# Executive summary

The Aurora Accord establishes a constitutional and operational framework for governing data relationships without creating one authority over the world’s data. It protects digital dignity, Indigenous data sovereignty, relational privacy, collective authority, public-interest research, portability, security, ecological responsibility, and effective remedy while preserving lawful plurality among states, Indigenous peoples, communities, public institutions, trusts, cooperatives, researchers, workers, and individuals.

Aurora v1.3 retains the source framework’s strongest commitments:

- data must not be treated merely as an extractable commodity;
- individuals and communities require meaningful agency over collection, inference, use, transfer, retention, and model training;
- Indigenous data sovereignty cannot be reduced to ordinary stakeholder consultation;
- custodians of data carry enforceable duties;
- interoperability should expand exit and agency rather than centralize custody;
- cybersecurity and ecological costs are governance questions, not technical afterthoughts;
- data harms require accessible complaint, correction, restitution, and justice routes;
- AI-training provenance and downstream influence require explicit governance;
- low-resource and offline participation must remain possible.

The revision rejects the idea that these goals require a vertically integrated Global Digital Governance Council. No institution under this Accord may simultaneously control standard-setting, accreditation, supervision, crisis response, adjudication, sanctions, public-data custody, and emerging-technology governance.

The Accord is governed by five constitutional rules.

1. **No framework-to-law conversion.** This text does not itself create taxes, offences, sanctions, compulsory certification, emergency access, model-shutdown authority, tribunal jurisdiction, or a universal identity system.
2. **No data operation without provenance.** Every consequential operation must identify its valid source of authority, purpose, necessity, affected parties, applicable law, duration, review route, and transfer limits.
3. **Classification does not decide ownership or access.** Data may carry overlapping personal, relational, collective, public-interest, evidentiary, ecological, confidential, security-sensitive, and Indigenous-restricted claims.
4. **Protected non-transfer is a legitimate outcome.** A person or community may lawfully refuse digitization, disclosure, registry inclusion, cross-border transfer, publication, AI training, or archival retention where applicable authority permits.
5. **Justice remains separate from administration.** Complaints may be supported administratively, but binding findings, coercive remedies, fines, injunctions, and model restrictions require lawful jurisdiction and the due-process architecture of Justice Systems.

Aurora v1.3 therefore operates as a federated interface architecture. It supports lawful rule-makers, Indigenous authorities, technical standards bodies, accreditation institutions, independent supervisors, public-interest stewards, cyber-response networks, justice institutions, and local custodians without turning any of them into a universal data sovereign.

# 1. Purpose, scope, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

The Accord provides shared principles, interface requirements, records, and institutional boundaries for data governance across jurisdictions and domains. Its purpose is to protect persons and communities from extraction, surveillance, manipulation, exclusion, insecurity, and historical data colonialism while enabling proportionate research, public administration, scientific cooperation, accessibility, and innovation.

## 1.2 Scope

The Accord applies, where validly adopted, to the collection, generation, observation, inference, combination, storage, access, use, publication, licensing, transfer, model training, retention, correction, deletion, archival preservation, and disposal of data.

It covers data used or produced by:

- public authorities;
- private enterprises;
- cooperatives and associations;
- research institutions;
- public-interest trusts and repositories;
- AI and statistical systems;
- health, education, labour, justice, financial, and emergency systems;
- environmental and infrastructure sensors;
- GGF-related pilots and institutions.

## 1.3 Non-authority clause

The Accord does not by authorship or declaration:

- create public jurisdiction;
- impose a tax or compulsory levy;
- create an offence or penalty;
- establish tribunal jurisdiction;
- authorize surveillance, search, seizure, access freezing, or emergency disclosure;
- compel certification or accreditation;
- create a global data custodian;
- represent an Indigenous people;
- establish a universal identity, wallet, credential, or ledger;
- authorize model shutdown, trade restriction, or service denial;
- override domestic, Indigenous, treaty, contractual, or constitutional rights.

A provision becomes binding only through a valid source of law or authority identified under Section 2.

## 1.4 Relationship to knowledge governance

Aurora governs data rights, lawful bases, custody, access, lifecycle, security, transfer, and remedy. The Digital Commons Framework governs the interpretation, curation, contestation, preservation, versioning, and retirement of shared knowledge.

The boundary is functional rather than categorical. A Traditional Knowledge record, scientific dataset, oral history, or model output may require both:

- an Aurora determination concerning authority, consent, custody, access, and transfer; and
- a Digital Commons determination concerning meaning, context, attribution, challenge, and stewardship.

Where the interfaces conflict, neither framework automatically prevails. The competent authorities must record the conflict, applicable law, protected interests, and review route in a **Data–Knowledge Interface Record**.

# 2. Sources of authority and applicable law

## 2.1 Authority provenance

Every consequential data operation must have an **Authority Provenance Record** identifying at least one independently valid basis, such as:

- individual consent or contract, where consent is legally and structurally valid;
- domestic constitutional, statutory, regulatory, or judicial authority;
- Treaty authority validly activated under Treaty;
- an Indigenous people’s own law, protocol, institution, or decision;
- a lawful compact, charter, trust, cooperative rule, professional duty, or organizational mandate;
- a court order issued by a body with jurisdiction;
- a narrowly defined vital-interest or emergency basis recognized by applicable law;
- another lawful basis whose scope and reviewability are publicly stated.

GGF authorship, technical competence, funding, certification, public popularity, pilot success, dashboard coverage, or institutional convenience are not sources of authority.

## 2.2 Applicable-law mapping

Before cross-jurisdictional processing, the responsible body must identify:

- territorial and personal jurisdiction;
- applicable public, private, Indigenous, labour, consumer, research, archival, and sectoral law;
- conflicts among applicable rules;
- mandatory protections that cannot be contracted away;
- competent supervisory and justice bodies;
- available remedies and recognition rules;
- onward-transfer constraints;
- unresolved legal uncertainty.

A compatibility label such as “GDPR+” may be used only after independent legal verification and must never substitute for jurisdiction-specific analysis.

## 2.3 Authority is operation-specific

Authority to collect does not automatically authorize inference. Authority to infer does not authorize publication. Authority to store does not authorize cross-border transfer. Authority to train a model does not authorize every downstream deployment. Authority to preserve evidence does not authorize public disclosure.

Each materially distinct operation requires its own lawful basis, purpose, limits, and review route.

## 2.4 No implied public-interest override

Claims of innovation, security, research, ecological benefit, public health, efficiency, truth, or global importance do not themselves create authority. A public-interest basis must be established through valid law or another competent source and must remain necessary, proportionate, time-bounded, reviewable, and compatible with protected non-transfer.

# 3. Data relationships and classification

## 3.1 Overlapping classification

Data must be classified through a **Data Classification and Rights Matrix**. A record may carry more than one classification simultaneously.

Relevant classes include:

- **personal data:** relates to an identified or identifiable person;
- **relational data:** concerns two or more persons or records a relationship among them;
- **inferred data:** generated through prediction, classification, scoring, or association;
- **collective data:** represents or materially affects a group, community, population, lineage, profession, territory, or shared practice;
- **Indigenous-restricted data:** governed by an Indigenous people’s law, protocol, authority, or protected relationship;
- **public-authority data:** created or held through public functions;
- **public-interest data:** has a validly established public benefit that may justify bounded access;
- **environmental and ecological data:** describes ecosystems, species, land, water, climate, or ecological relations;
- **evidentiary data:** retained for investigation, adjudication, accountability, or historical truth;
- **confidential or privileged data:** protected by professional, legal, research, commercial, diplomatic, or safety duties;
- **security-sensitive data:** could expose critical infrastructure, vulnerable persons, emergency capacity, or protective systems;
- **neural, biometric, genetic, intimate, or highly sensitive data:** carries heightened dignity, discrimination, autonomy, or bodily risks;
- **operational data:** required for a bounded service or system function;
- **synthetic or model-generated data:** produced by models but potentially linked to real persons, communities, or sources.

## 3.2 Classification is not disposition

A label does not automatically determine ownership, access, portability, deletion, publication, licensing, or training eligibility. Those questions require a rights-and-authority analysis.

## 3.3 Relational and multi-subject claims

Where data concern several persons or groups, the responsible body must identify:

- each affected subject or class of subject;
- conflicting access, correction, deletion, portability, and publication claims;
- risks of exposing third parties;
- representation and internal-plurality questions;
- technical means of separation, redaction, aggregation, or selective portability;
- the competent decision-maker and appeal route.

No person may unilaterally export, publish, or delete shared communications, family records, social graphs, genetic information, or group media where that action would materially violate another person’s rights without an applicable legal basis.

## 3.4 Inferred data

People and communities possess rights concerning consequential inferences even where they did not supply the underlying data. Responsible bodies must disclose the existence, purpose, primary input categories, material effects, correction route, and retention period of consequential inferred profiles, subject to legitimate security and anti-gaming limits.

# 4. Persons, groups, communities, and affected parties

## 4.1 Individual rights

Depending on applicable law and context, individuals should have accessible rights to:

- notice and intelligible explanation;
- access and obtain a usable copy;
- correct inaccuracies;
- object to or restrict processing;
- withdraw consent;
- request deletion or lifecycle remedy;
- obtain portability where technically and legally appropriate;
- challenge consequential inference or automated use;
- receive breach notification and remedy;
- use offline or non-digital channels;
- avoid retaliation for exercising rights.

## 4.2 Collective and community claims

Collective claims require a **Community Authority and Internal-Plurality Record** identifying:

- the claimed community;
- basis of membership and affectedness;
- authority relied upon;
- decision procedure;
- dissenting or minority positions;
- diaspora, displaced, adopted, mixed, and contested identities;
- overlapping community claims;
- individual rights within the collective record;
- review and succession arrangements.

No organization, state agency, platform, Elder, council, cooperative, or self-appointed representative may be presumed to speak for a whole community merely because it has visibility or institutional status.

## 4.3 Children and persons under dependency

Children, patients, workers, students, tenants, migrants, detainees, benefit recipients, and persons dependent on essential services require heightened protection because refusal may be costly or impossible. Consent obtained in these contexts must be supplemented by necessity, minimization, purpose limitation, independent oversight, and non-retaliation.

## 4.4 Workers

Workers have rights concerning workplace monitoring, productivity scoring, biometric systems, safety data, algorithmic management, disciplinary records, and model training on work products. Collective bargaining, worker representation, occupational safety, and whistleblower protections remain applicable.

## 4.5 Standing and representation

Complaints and public-interest claims may be brought by affected individuals, authorized representatives, worker bodies, Indigenous authorities, community organizations, public-interest advocates, or other entities where valid law grants standing. Representation must be contestable and cannot extinguish an affected person’s independent rights without lawful basis.

# 5. Indigenous data sovereignty and non-creation

## 5.1 Recognition, not creation

Indigenous data sovereignty arises from Indigenous peoples’ own sovereignty, law, jurisdiction, relationships, and institutions. The GGF, Aurora Accord, Treaty bodies, states, platforms, researchers, and funders do not create or certify that sovereignty.

## 5.2 No universal Indigenous data authority

Aurora does not establish a permanent Indigenous Data Sovereignty Council as supreme authority for all Indigenous data. It recognizes Indigenous-created councils, nations, confederations, repositories, protocols, and bilateral or multilateral interfaces where the relevant peoples authorize them.

## 5.3 Affected-nation standing

A decision affecting an Indigenous people’s territory, citizens, language, knowledge, cultural expression, genetic material, sacred relations, ecological observations, archives, or identity must first identify the affected people and its own authority and consent processes.

Participation by one Indigenous nation cannot authorize the use of another nation’s data or knowledge.

## 5.4 Protected non-transfer

An Indigenous authority may designate data, knowledge, or metadata as:

- not to be digitized;
- not to be collected;
- locally held only;
- non-exportable;
- non-public;
- non-commercial;
- non-trainable;
- restricted by season, role, lineage, place, ceremony, or purpose;
- subject to return, deletion, repatriation, or community-controlled custody.

A protected non-transfer determination is not a failure of interoperability.

## 5.5 Non-engagement

Indigenous peoples may decline Aurora participation without being represented through a proxy, assigned an empty seat, classified as non-compliant, or denied unrelated rights and services.

## 5.6 Attestation boundaries

Where an external system must verify that a valid Indigenous authorization exists, it should receive the least information necessary. A bounded attestation may confirm authorization, restriction, expiry, or contact point without disclosing protected decision processes, identities, or knowledge.

# 6. Purpose, necessity, minimization, and lawful basis

## 6.1 Purpose specification

Before collection or generation, the responsible body must document:

- the specific purpose;
- affected persons and communities;
- expected benefit and possible harm;
- why less data or a less intrusive method is insufficient;
- categories and sources;
- recipients and processors;
- retention and disposal;
- cross-border movement;
- inference and model-training uses;
- complaint and remedy routes.

Broad purposes such as “innovation,” “service improvement,” “security,” “research,” or “public good” are insufficient without operational definition.

## 6.2 Necessity and proportionality

Processing must be reasonably necessary for the stated purpose and proportionate to the rights and risks involved. Convenience, speculative future value, or the possibility that data may later be useful does not establish necessity.

## 6.3 Data minimalism

Systems must minimize:

- fields collected;
- precision and granularity;
- duration of retention;
- persons with access;
- linkability across domains;
- geographic reach;
- model and inference reuse;
- replication and backup proliferation;
- energy, water, hardware, and labour burden.

## 6.4 Secondary use

A materially new purpose requires renewed authority analysis. Compatibility must consider reasonable expectations, sensitivity, power imbalance, original context, downstream effects, collective claims, and available refusal.

## 6.5 Prohibited circumvention

A body may not avoid data protections by purchasing brokered data, obtaining inferred profiles, using public sources, outsourcing to a processor, generating synthetic derivatives, or combining several non-identifying datasets to recreate identifying information.

# 7. Consent, refusal, withdrawal, and power imbalance

## 7.1 Conditions for valid consent

Consent must be:

- specific;
- informed in accessible language;
- freely given;
- affirmative;
- purpose-bound;
- granular where uses differ materially;
- revocable without disproportionate penalty;
- documented without creating an unnecessary identity trail;
- renewed where conditions materially change.

## 7.2 Consent is not always sufficient

Consent does not legalize processing that is unlawful, discriminatory, exploitative, excessively risky, contrary to another person’s rights, or outside the authority of the party giving consent.

## 7.3 Structural coercion

Consent is presumptively weakened where refusal threatens:

- employment or promotion;
- healthcare;
- education;
- housing;
- public benefits;
- justice access;
- political participation;
- communication essential to social life;
- basic financial services;
- physical safety.

In these contexts, the responsible body must establish an independently valid lawful basis, strict necessity, minimal scope, and a non-digital or non-invasive alternative where feasible.

## 7.4 Withdrawal

Withdrawal stops future consent-based processing after a reasonable implementation period. It does not automatically erase lawful evidence, settled transactions, another person’s rights, or records required by a valid legal hold. The body must explain what stops, what remains, why it remains, and when remaining copies expire.

## 7.5 Community consent

Collective consent cannot be reduced to a dashboard click by one representative. It requires a valid authority and internal-plurality record, an identified scope, affected-member protections, expiry, and review.

## 7.6 No dark patterns

Interfaces may not manipulate agreement through bundling, fatigue, asymmetrical button design, misleading language, repeated pressure, default openness, or withholding core functionality unrelated to the requested data use.

# 8. Data lifecycle

## 8.1 Lifecycle stages

Every data system must govern separately:

1. collection or observation;
2. generation and inference;
3. validation and correction;
4. active use;
5. internal sharing;
6. external transfer;
7. publication or release;
8. model training and evaluation;
9. archival preservation or legal hold;
10. backup and disaster recovery;
11. restriction and quarantine;
12. deletion, return, or destruction;
13. residual influence and derivative remediation.

## 8.2 Retention schedules

Retention must be based on purpose, law, risk, evidence needs, cultural restrictions, and subject rights. “Indefinite,” “historical value,” or “future research” requires specific justification and competent authorization.

## 8.3 Correction

Systems must preserve the ability to correct factual errors, contextualize disputed claims, append later outcomes, and separate allegation from finding. Audit integrity must not freeze false or misleading content.

## 8.4 Deletion and forgetting

Deletion requests must distinguish:

- deletion from active source systems;
- removal from indexes and search;
- deletion from processors and replicas;
- backup expiry and restoration controls;
- termination of future use;
- removal from evaluation and retrieval systems;
- model unlearning or retraining where technically and proportionately feasible;
- output suppression or correction;
- derived-profile correction;
- preservation under a valid legal hold;
- residual influence that cannot presently be removed.

No body may promise complete erasure where it cannot verify it.

## 8.5 Archives and evidence

Archival or evidentiary preservation requires a valid basis, access limits, retention review, integrity protection, and separation from ordinary reuse. Preserved evidence is not automatically available for research, publication, or model training.

## 8.6 Disposal

Disposal must cover digital copies, physical media, credentials, cryptographic keys, vendor systems, logs, caches, indexes, and decommissioned hardware. Where physical destruction has ecological costs, secure reuse and circular handling should be preferred when compatible with confidentiality.

# 9. Identity and credential separation

## 9.1 No universal identity layer

Aurora prohibits making one GGF identifier, decentralized identifier, biometric, wallet, Love Ledger identity, or credential a prerequisite for exercising data rights, receiving AUBI, working, voting, obtaining healthcare, accessing justice, or using public services.

## 9.2 Function separation

Identity systems must distinguish:

- proof of personhood;
- account authentication;
- age or eligibility attestation;
- professional authority;
- organizational role;
- transaction authorization;
- consent receipt;
- pseudonymous participation;
- anonymous access;
- audit accountability.

These functions should not be linked by default.

## 9.3 Minimal disclosure

Where a person needs to prove an attribute, systems should request the least information necessary. A proof that a person is over a threshold should not reveal exact birth date; a proof of authorization should not disclose unrelated identity or affiliation.

## 9.4 Offline and low-documentation access

Rights processes must include pathways for persons without smartphones, stable addresses, government documents, biometrics, reliable connectivity, literacy, or legal status. Human review and community-supported access must not become inferior or punitive channels.

## 9.5 Anti-linkage rule

Identifiers used in health, work, justice, finance, civic participation, education, and social services may not be linked merely because linkage is technically convenient. Cross-domain linkage requires specific authority, necessity, and review.

# 10. Fiduciary and stewardship duties

## 10.1 Role-specific duties

Aurora replaces one universal Data Fiduciary category with role-specific obligations for:

- controllers and decision-makers;
- processors and technical service providers;
- public authorities;
- trustees and repository custodians;
- researchers;
- model developers and deployers;
- data brokers;
- employers and platform operators;
- archives and evidence custodians;
- community and cooperative stewards.

## 10.2 Core duties

Where applicable, duties include:

- loyalty to the protected purpose and affected parties;
- care and competent security;
- minimization;
- non-exploitation;
- transparency and explainability;
- conflict disclosure;
- purpose limitation;
- correction and lifecycle management;
- accessibility;
- non-retaliation;
- preservation of protected non-transfer;
- cooperation with lawful supervision and remedy.

## 10.3 No duty laundering

A controller cannot avoid responsibility by contracting with a processor, certification provider, cloud vendor, data cooperative, model developer, or trust. Contracts must allocate operational duties but cannot extinguish mandatory rights.

## 10.4 Stewardship charters

A public-interest trust, community repository, cooperative, or archive must have a **Data Fiduciary or Stewardship Charter** defining:

- constituting authority;
- purpose;
- beneficiaries and affected parties;
- decision rights;
- access tiers;
- financial conflicts;
- Indigenous and community interfaces;
- security and retention;
- complaints and justice referral;
- transfer, succession, and dissolution.

# 11. Federated public-interest data stewardship

## 11.1 No presumed Global Data Commons

Data do not become a global commons merely because they concern climate, health, science, infrastructure, or humanity. A public-interest repository or trust must be separately constituted by competent authorities and must define its domain, contributors, users, access rules, and remedies.

## 11.2 Federated architecture

Public-interest stewardship should use a network of domain-specific, regional, national, Indigenous, municipal, research, and community repositories. Data may remain local while queries, aggregates, models, or bounded outputs move under controlled interfaces.

## 11.3 Access tiers

A steward may use tiers such as:

- open aggregate access;
- registered public-interest access;
- controlled research access;
- secure enclave access;
- community- or Indigenous-authorized access;
- emergency-only access;
- non-transferable local use;
- closed or non-digitized status.

## 11.4 Public funding and openness

Public funding creates transparency and public-benefit duties but does not automatically authorize unrestricted publication, commercialization, re-identification, or AI training.

## 11.5 Commons exit and dissolution

Contributors and constituting authorities must know what happens if a repository closes, changes purpose, loses authorization, or is transferred. Data must not silently pass to a successor institution, creditor, acquirer, or vendor.

# 12. Interoperability, portability, and the right to exit

## 12.1 Interoperability by consent and authority

Interoperability should permit people and institutions to move services, records, and relationships without surrendering custody to a central platform. It must remain purpose-specific, secure, semantically meaningful, and compatible with local equivalents.

## 12.2 Portability rights

Portability should provide usable, structured, commonly supported formats where feasible. It may include content, settings, transaction histories, social connections, or service records, subject to third-party and collective rights.

## 12.3 Relational safeguards

Before exporting relational data, systems must consider:

- other participants’ privacy;
- message and group context;
- block and safety settings;
- confidential or privileged content;
- copyright and authorship;
- community restrictions;
- re-identification risk.

Selective export, redaction, references, or consent invitations may be preferable to full copying.

## 12.4 No throttling or hostage-taking

Platforms may not use hidden fees, degraded formats, unreasonable delay, artificial API limits, retaliatory account restrictions, or proprietary dependencies to frustrate lawful exit.

## 12.5 Equivalent local implementation

A system may satisfy interoperability requirements through an equivalent local or federated method. Aurora does not require central registration, one API provider, or one technical architecture.

## 12.6 Continuity

Exit must preserve lawful claims, essential records, balances, evidence, worker rights, and service continuity. Portability does not authorize deletion of records another party lawfully needs.

# 13. Cross-border data flows and government access

## 13.1 Transfer record

Each material cross-border transfer requires a **Cross-Border Data Transfer Record** stating:

- exporter, importer, processors, and onward recipients;
- data classes;
- purpose and lawful basis;
- applicable laws;
- government-access risks;
- security controls;
- localization or enclave options;
- retention;
- subject and community remedies;
- suspension and return procedures.

## 13.2 Adequacy and safeguards

Transfer may rely on adequacy, treaty, law, contract, binding organizational rules, explicit authorization, or another valid mechanism. Formal paperwork is insufficient where practical access, surveillance, discrimination, or remedy risks remain excessive.

## 13.3 Government access

State requests for data require explicit authority, necessity, scope, minimization, retention, independent authorization where applicable, audit, and remedy. Secret access must not become a hidden exception to Aurora rights.

Where notice is lawfully delayed, the delay must be time-bounded and reviewed. Aggregate transparency reporting should disclose request volumes and categories without compromising active safety needs.

## 13.4 Conflicting laws

Where one jurisdiction compels disclosure that another prohibits, the custodian must:

- notify competent authorities where lawful;
- challenge overbroad demands where possible;
- minimize disclosure;
- preserve evidence of the conflict;
- consider suspension, localization, encryption, or service redesign;
- provide delayed notice and remedy when permitted.

## 13.5 Localization

Localization may protect sovereignty and reduce access risk, but it can also enable domestic surveillance, market exclusion, or technical dependency. Decisions must identify the problem localization solves and assess less restrictive alternatives.

# 14. AI-training data and model governance

## 14.1 Training-data provenance

Model developers and deployers must maintain a **Training Data Provenance and Lawful Use Record** describing:

- source categories and acquisition methods;
- applicable lawful bases;
- excluded and protected sources;
- licenses and restrictions;
- personal, relational, collective, and Indigenous claims;
- geographic and temporal coverage;
- filtering and deduplication;
- known quality and representation limits;
- downstream use constraints;
- withdrawal and remediation processes.

## 14.2 Layered transparency

Transparency must be layered.

### Public layer

Publish meaningful source categories, major repositories, acquisition routes, legal bases, exclusions, geographic and linguistic coverage, known limitations, and contact routes without exposing protected sources.

### Supervisor or auditor layer

Provide sufficiently detailed records for lawful independent review, including sampling, contracts, provenance evidence, risk assessment, and corrective actions.

### Protected layer

Personal data, Traditional Knowledge, confidential research, security-sensitive sources, litigation material, and vulnerable-community data remain protected and may be reviewed only by authorized persons under strict controls.

## 14.3 No universal public registry

Aurora does not require publication of every dataset, URL, record, or contributor. Transparency must not become a directory for extraction or re-identification.

## 14.4 Withdrawal and remediation

Where a source is found unlawful or authorization is withdrawn, the responsible body must assess:

- stopping future ingestion;
- removing source copies;
- changing retrieval indexes;
- model unlearning;
- retraining;
- fine-tuning or suppression;
- blocking specific outputs;
- correcting derived profiles;
- notifying deployers;
- residual influence and technical limits.

## 14.5 Model restrictions

A model restriction, suspension, or shutdown requires valid legal authority, evidence, notice, defence, proportionality, continuity analysis, appeal, and protection for users who depend on the service. Aurora administrators and standards bodies cannot impose shutdowns by certification decision alone.

## 14.6 Generated data

Model-generated data may still be personal, defamatory, discriminatory, confidential, or culturally restricted. “Synthetic” does not remove responsibility where outputs reproduce or infer protected information.

# 15. Public, environmental, and ecological data

## 15.1 Public-interest stewardship

Environmental and public datasets should support climate adaptation, disaster response, research, ecological protection, and democratic accountability. Access must nevertheless account for safety, territorial authority, re-identification, and ecological harm.

## 15.2 Ecological sensitivity

Data concerning sacred sites, endangered species, nesting areas, migration routes, water sources, vulnerable habitats, illegal-extraction targets, or restoration zones may require delayed, aggregated, restricted, or locally controlled access.

## 15.3 Indigenous territorial knowledge

Environmental data gathered on or about Indigenous territories do not become global public goods by being ecological. Affected-nation authority, consent, and protected non-transfer apply.

## 15.4 Sensor governance

Environmental sensing requires an **Observation Data Governance Record** defining:

- purpose and decision supported;
- location and affected communities;
- fields and precision;
- ownership and maintenance;
- access and publication;
- model use;
- error and missingness;
- decommissioning;
- whether the sensor creates surveillance of persons or communities.

## 15.5 Precaution without epistemic monopoly

A competent authority may restrict publication or use where credible ecological harm is plausible. Precaution must be evidence-based, reviewable, and unable to create indefinite secrecy or one institution’s monopoly over environmental knowledge.

# 16. Neural, biometric, genetic, and intimate data

## 16.1 Heightened protection

Neural, biometric, genetic, reproductive, sexual, health, emotional, behavioural, and intimate data require heightened protection because misuse can affect bodily autonomy, identity, dignity, discrimination, family members, and freedom of thought.

## 16.2 Neural data

BCI and neural-data systems must protect:

- mental privacy;
- cognitive liberty;
- freedom from compelled inference;
- continuous and context-sensitive consent;
- local or device-level processing where feasible;
- immediate pause and deletion controls;
- prohibition of secondary employment, insurance, policing, advertising, or political uses without separate lawful authority;
- rights concerning decoded or inferred mental content.

## 16.3 Biometrics

Biometric systems require necessity, high security, spoofing and error assessment, demographic performance analysis, fallback access, and protection against function creep. A person must not be denied essential services merely because biometric processing fails or is refused where a less intrusive alternative is feasible.

## 16.4 Genetic and familial data

Genetic data create claims for biological relatives, populations, and communities. Individual consent alone may be insufficient for publication, commercial licensing, or population-level inference.

## 16.5 Anti-compulsion

Employers, schools, insurers, platforms, governments, and service providers may not compel intimate-data processing merely by embedding it in standard terms. Compulsion requires a specific lawful basis, strict necessity, and independent safeguards.

---

# 17. Security and resilience

## 17.1 Outcome-oriented security

Aurora requires risk-based security outcomes, not one permanent technical stack. Required outcomes include:

- confidentiality;
- integrity;
- availability;
- authenticity;
- recoverability;
- least privilege;
- segmentation;
- vulnerability management;
- secure development;
- incident detection and response;
- supply-chain assurance;
- human and organizational resilience.

## 17.2 Proportional profiles

Security controls must be proportionate to sensitivity, scale, threat, dependency, and resources. Small community repositories and critical cross-border infrastructure may use different controls while meeting appropriate outcomes.

## 17.3 Technology neutrality

Encryption, decentralized identifiers, zero-knowledge proofs, secure multi-party computation, blockchains, hardware security modules, federated learning, and other tools may be used where appropriate. None is constitutionally mandatory merely because it is named in an appendix.

## 17.4 Equivalent controls

Local, low-tech, offline, paper, air-gapped, community-managed, or manually reviewed systems may satisfy Aurora where they provide equivalent protection and accessibility.

## 17.5 Security audits

Auditors must be independent, competent, conflict-disclosed, and subject to accreditation and appeal. Penetration testing and red-team activity require authorization, scope, safety controls, evidence handling, and protection from secondary exposure.

## 17.6 No certification cliff

Failure to meet a security profile may trigger support, remediation, narrowed processing, or lawful restriction. It must not automatically deny persons justice, essential services, public benefits, or access to their own data.

---

# 18. Incident response and breach notification

## 18.1 Incident priorities

Incident response must prioritize:

1. safety and containment;
2. preservation of essential services;
3. protection of affected persons and communities;
4. evidence integrity;
5. accurate notification;
6. correction and restitution;
7. independent review and learning.

## 18.2 Layered notification

Notification timing and detail depend on risk. Immediate public disclosure may worsen exploitation, reveal defensive gaps, expose protected populations, or compromise investigation. Responsible bodies must distinguish:

- direct notice to affected persons;
- notice to Indigenous or community authorities;
- confidential notice to supervisors and incident responders;
- public summary;
- delayed technical disclosure;
- final after-action report.

## 18.3 Content of notice

Notice should state what happened, what data and persons may be affected, likely consequences, containment steps, actions recipients can take, available support, correction and remedy routes, and remaining uncertainty.

## 18.4 Restitution

Depending on harm and lawful authority, restitution may include identity protection, account restoration, financial compensation, service continuity, data correction, deletion, credit or reputation repair, community-controlled remediation, and independent monitoring.

## 18.5 After-action review

Reviews must examine technical, organizational, governance, labour, funding, and vendor causes. They should not treat individual blame as a substitute for systemic correction.

---

# 19. Emergency data governance

## 19.1 Authority source

Emergency access, disclosure, linkage, retention, or restriction requires authority from Treaty, domestic law, Indigenous law, or another competent legal instrument. A data standards, accreditation, supervision, or coordination body cannot declare or extend its own emergency jurisdiction.

## 19.2 Emergency record

Every emergency operation requires an **Emergency Data Access and Continuity Record** stating:

- declaring authority;
- crisis class and evidence;
- purpose;
- data and persons affected;
- necessity and less intrusive alternatives;
- permitted users;
- duration and expiry;
- independent review;
- notice or delayed-notice rules;
- continuity protections;
- deletion, unlinking, and restoration after the emergency;
- appeal and remedy.

## 19.3 Narrow provisional action

Where delay risks serious and imminent harm, applicable law may permit narrow provisional action. The action must be reversible where possible and cannot create permanent data linkage, new institutional jurisdiction, or an indefinite archive.

## 19.4 Access freezes

A cyber incident may justify temporary segmentation or access restriction for security. Restrictions must preserve emergency care, justice, essential payments, accessibility, lawful evidence, and affected persons’ ability to obtain urgent help.

## 19.5 Restoration

Emergency datasets, linkages, credentials, and exceptional permissions must expire, be deleted or returned, or receive new ordinary-law authorization. Post-emergency convenience is not a lawful basis for retention.

---

# 20. Audit records, institutional memory, and protected non-transfer

## 20.1 Event integrity without immutable content

Audit systems should preserve trustworthy evidence that an event occurred while minimizing permanent personal or protected content. Techniques may include append-only event hashes, signed attestations, segregated logs, access-controlled records, and independently witnessed corrections.

## 20.2 Correction and expungement

An audit system must support:

- correction of inaccurate metadata;
- annotation of disputed events;
- sealing or expungement where law permits;
- deletion of personal content while retaining a minimal proof of lawful deletion;
- separation of identity from event evidence;
- retention review;
- role-based access;
- protected non-transfer.

## 20.3 Institutional memory

Institutional memory should preserve mandates, decisions, uncertainty, dissent, authority provenance, incidents, changes, and unresolved questions. It should not become a universal behavioural record or reputation system.

## 20.4 No Love Ledger linkage

Data stewardship, audit compliance, civic identity, rights, justice, employment, or public-service access may not depend on Love Ledger participation or contribution histories.

## 20.5 Records transfer

Institutional merger, succession, vendor change, insolvency, or dissolution requires an **Institutional Transfer, Exit, and Dissolution Record**. No archive or dataset transfers automatically to a successor.

---

# 21. Standard-setting, accreditation, supervision, and functional separation

## 21.1 No vertically integrated GDGC

Aurora replaces the proposed Global Digital Governance Council with separated functions.

## 21.2 Lawful public rule-making

Binding public rules are adopted by bodies with valid legislative, regulatory, Treaty, Indigenous, or compact authority. Aurora may supply model provisions but cannot enact them.

## 21.3 Technical standards network

A plural network of standards bodies may develop interoperability, security, accessibility, provenance, and portability specifications. Participation must be transparent, conflict-aware, accessible to low-resource actors, and open to equivalent implementations.

## 21.4 Accreditation

Accreditation bodies determine whether auditors, certifiers, or technical providers meet published competence and independence criteria. Accreditation does not create public jurisdiction.

## 21.5 Certification

Certification may provide evidence of conformity with a stated profile. It is not proof of legality, ethical legitimacy, Indigenous consent, or absence of harm. Certification decisions require explanation, correction, appeal, and protection against conflicts of interest.

## 21.6 Independent supervision

Supervisors may investigate, order administrative correction, or refer matters only where valid law grants those powers. They must not adjudicate criminal liability or impose sanctions outside their jurisdiction.

## 21.7 Cyber coordination

Cyber-response networks coordinate information, voluntary assistance, and existing lawful institutions. They cannot create emergency powers through technical expertise.

## 21.8 Meta-Governance interface

Meta-Governance may map interfaces, convene competent authorities, support mediation, and maintain coordination records. It may not set binding data rules, certify fiduciaries, decide disputes, declare emergencies, or impose sanctions.

---

# 22. Complaints, justice referral, and remedies

## 22.1 Accessible complaints

Every participating system must provide accessible channels for notice, correction, objection, restriction, deletion, portability, breach claims, and protected non-transfer requests. Channels must support disability access, multiple languages, offline submission, representatives, and non-retaliation.

## 22.2 Administrative resolution

Administrative bodies may provide information, facilitate correction, request voluntary remediation, and issue non-binding findings where authorized. They must disclose when a complainant needs a court, tribunal, labour body, Indigenous authority, consumer authority, or another forum.

## 22.3 Justice referral

Binding disputes follow Justice Systems. A **Justice Referral and Remedy Record** must identify:

- claimant and standing;
- respondent;
- applicable law;
- jurisdiction;
- evidence preserved;
- interim protection requested;
- remedies sought;
- confidentiality and protected-knowledge restrictions;
- appeal and enforcement route.

## 22.4 Remedies

Possible remedies, where lawfully authorized, include:

- notice and explanation;
- access and correction;
- restriction or deletion;
- return or repatriation;
- termination of processing;
- model remediation;
- compensation;
- restitution and benefit sharing;
- service restoration;
- anti-retaliation orders;
- security improvements;
- public correction;
- bounded monitoring;
- injunction or lawful model restriction.

## 22.5 Proportionality and continuity

Remedies must consider innocent users, workers, essential services, evidence preservation, public safety, and community continuity. A platform or model shutdown is never an automatic response to non-compliance.

# 23. Data reparations and historical extraction

## 23.1 Scope

Data reparations may address colonial archives, coerced census records, extracted language corpora, misappropriated Traditional Knowledge, genomic exploitation, discriminatory profiling, surveillance, uncompensated commercial reuse, or other historical data harms.

## 23.2 Community-defined harm

Affected peoples and communities should define the relevant harms and desired remedies through their own valid processes. An external tribunal or review panel cannot presume one universal reparations formula.

## 23.3 Remedy forms

Remedies may include:

- return or repatriation;
- deletion or restricted custody;
- correction and contextualization;
- access restoration;
- control of future use;
- attribution;
- licensing and benefit sharing;
- compensation;
- infrastructure and capacity funding;
- support for community archives;
- cessation of model use;
- public acknowledgment;
- institutional reform.

## 23.4 No purchase of consent

Reparative funding must not be conditioned on Treaty participation, data sharing, creation of an Aurora-compatible council, public release, or AI-training access.

## 23.5 Finance

Funding requires a valid legal, trust, settlement, philanthropic, or public-investment basis. Aurora does not impose a data tax or guarantee an impact-bond repayment stream.

---

# 24. Ecological and labour footprint of data systems

## 24.1 Material accountability

Data systems depend on energy, water, land, minerals, hardware, logistics, maintenance, and labour. Responsible bodies should assess these impacts rather than treating digital processing as immaterial.

## 24.2 Environmental requirements

Binding carbon, water, siting, waste, biodiversity, and supply-chain duties require valid environmental authority. Aurora may provide model assessment records and interoperability requirements but cannot set one global net-zero deadline by itself.

## 24.3 Data minimization as ecological design

Reducing collection, precision, duplication, retention, model size, retraining frequency, and unnecessary computation can reduce both rights risks and ecological burden.

## 24.4 Worker protections

Security, content moderation, data labeling, hardware production, mining, logistics, and data-centre work must comply with labour rights, occupational safety, fair compensation, collective organization, and whistleblower protection.

## 24.5 No burden export

High-resource institutions may not meet sustainability targets by exporting water use, mineral extraction, e-waste, dangerous work, or surveillance-intensive labour to lower-resource communities.

---

# 25. Finance and public investment boundaries

## 25.1 No self-authorized taxation

Aurora does not create a Global Data Taxation Framework. Participating jurisdictions may adopt lawful taxes, fees, penalties, settlements, or contributions under their own authority and may coordinate them through separately negotiated instruments.

## 25.2 No Hearts rewards for compliance

Data rights, certification, openness, public services, or regulatory treatment may not depend on Hearts awards, Love Ledger ratings, or a single ethical-data score.

## 25.3 Public investment

Public and philanthropic investment may support:

- low-resource security capacity;
- community-controlled and Indigenous data infrastructure;
- accessibility and offline tools;
- public-interest repositories;
- legal aid and complaint support;
- research on privacy-preserving methods;
- ecological improvements;
- data reparations under valid authority.

## 25.4 Funder safeguards

Funders may not control standards, pilot selection, community authority, audit conclusions, procurement, data access, or future regulatory treatment. Funding agreements must disclose conditions, conflicts, data rights, and exit terms.

## 25.5 No certification-conditioned fundamental access

A small or low-resource institution should receive support and proportional requirements. Loss of certification may restrict a specific risky operation but cannot automatically remove access to justice, essential public-interest data, or unrelated services.

# 26. Pilots, ratification, and implementation

## 26.1 Genesis alignment

Pre-ratification work follows Genesis Protocol. GGF contributors may research, publish, facilitate, and support voluntary pilots but possess no inherent public authority.

## 26.2 Pilot constitutional contract

Each pilot requires a contract or lawful mandate specifying:

- host authority;
- purpose and scope;
- participants and affected communities;
- rights and refusal;
- Indigenous and community interfaces;
- data classes and lifecycle;
- identity requirements;
- security profile;
- evaluation;
- funding and vendors;
- complaints and remedy;
- withdrawal, failure, and wind-down;
- non-conversion of pilot participation into Treaty consent.

## 26.3 Phase sequence

Implementation should proceed through:

1. voluntary research and local pilots;
2. independent evaluation and failure publication;
3. negotiation by lawfully mandated participants;
4. ratification or adoption through valid jurisdictional processes;
5. institution-specific first seating;
6. separate activation of coercive, fiscal, or emergency powers;
7. review, amendment, coexistence, transfer, or dissolution.

## 26.4 No holdout punishment

Non-participating states, peoples, communities, platforms, or institutions are not automatically sanctioned or subjected to trade restrictions. Restrictions require an independent legal basis, due process, necessity, proportionality, and continuity analysis.

## 26.5 Interoperable coexistence

Participants may adopt partial, sectoral, local, bilateral, or federated interfaces where lawful. Full integration into one global architecture is not the default measure of success.

## 26.6 Failure can stop scaling

A pilot may conclude that a mechanism is unsafe, unjust, unaffordable, culturally incompatible, non-transferable, or unnecessary. That finding may justify narrowing, redesign, pause, or closure rather than pressure to demonstrate success.

---

# 27. Evaluation, amendment, transfer, and dissolution

## 27.1 Multidimensional evaluation

Aurora institutions should publish separate indicators concerning:

- rights access;
- consent quality and refusal;
- security and resilience;
- breach harm;
- cross-border remedy;
- Indigenous non-interference;
- protected non-transfer;
- accessibility and offline inclusion;
- data minimization;
- worker and ecological impacts;
- funder and vendor concentration;
- institutional independence;
- unresolved disputes and missing populations.

No composite score creates jurisdiction, authorizes sanctions, or proves legitimacy.

## 27.2 Observation versus decision

Dashboards, audits, anomaly alerts, satisfaction measures, and missingness reports may trigger inquiry. They do not determine guilt, consent, lawfulness, emergency, certification withdrawal, or institutional dissolution without an authorized process.

## 27.3 Amendment

Before entry into force, voluntary procedures may be amended under their own charter. Binding Accord obligations are amended through the constituting legal instrument and Treaty where applicable.

Technical profiles may evolve through transparent standards processes, but a standards update cannot silently expand legal authority or invalidate equivalent local implementations.

## 27.4 Transfer

Functions, records, certifications, repositories, and contracts transfer only under lawful authority, continuity safeguards, data-subject and community notice, and protected non-transfer review.

## 27.5 Dissolution

Dissolution must address:

- active rights requests and cases;
- data custody, return, deletion, or preservation;
- workers and contractors;
- funds and liabilities;
- credentials and access keys;
- vendors and cloud systems;
- public communication;
- archival records;
- successor authority or legitimate absence of a successor.

Dissolution does not automatically transfer functions to Meta-Governance, another GGF body, a funder, a vendor, or a successor trust.

# Final governing principles

The Aurora Accord shall be interpreted according to the following principles:

> **Data governance allocates rights, duties, purposes, and remedies; it does not create one sovereign owner of information.**

> **No operation is legitimate merely because data are available, technically accessible, publicly funded, environmentally relevant, or useful to a model.**

> **Indigenous data sovereignty is exercised by Indigenous peoples through their own authorities and cannot be represented by a GGF-created proxy.**

> **Interoperability should make exit and cooperation possible without requiring central custody, universal identity, or forced disclosure.**

> **Auditability must coexist with correction, forgetting, expungement, protected knowledge, and lawful deletion.**

> **Emergency access must come from law outside the emergency actor and must end when the emergency basis ends.**

> **Technical standards may support lawful governance; they do not create jurisdiction.**

> **Where legitimate authority is absent, unresolved, or refused, protected non-transfer and non-action remain valid constitutional outcomes.**
