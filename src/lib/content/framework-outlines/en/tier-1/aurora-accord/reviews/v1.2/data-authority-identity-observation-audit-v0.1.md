# Aurora Accord Data Authority, Identity, Observation, and Protected Non-Transfer Audit v0.1

## Can a global data-governance framework protect dignity, collective authority, interoperability, and public-interest research without becoming a universal data sovereign?

**Status:** Internal bounded constitutional and architectural audit  
**Primary object:** Global Data Governance & Sovereignty Framework / Aurora Accord v1.2  
**Compatibility corpus:** Constitutional Interface Specification v0.1; Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3; Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing v1.1; Integrated Meta-Governance v1.5; Genesis Protocol v0.9; Justice Systems Framework v1.4  
**Audit date:** 2026-08-01  
**Recommended successor:** Aurora Accord v1.3

---

# Executive summary

## Overall verdict

Aurora Accord v1.2 identifies a genuinely load-bearing problem. Every constitutional, economic, ecological, justice, and coordination framework depends on data: identity records, evidence, sensor readings, public accounts, consent records, financial transactions, institutional memory, AI-training provenance, emergency information, and protected community knowledge. The source correctly rejects default extraction, recognizes personal and collective claims, emphasizes minimization and portability, and treats Indigenous data sovereignty, cybersecurity, redress, and ecological cost as constitutional rather than merely technical concerns.

Those commitments should survive.

The framework is nevertheless **not compatible with the revised constitutional stack**. It repeatedly turns technical stewardship into public jurisdiction and then concentrates that jurisdiction in a vertically integrated global regime. The Global Digital Governance Council sets ethical and technical standards, charters fiduciaries, approves auditors, coordinates emergencies, mediates disputes, regulates cross-border flows, develops emerging-technology governance, and controls certification consequences. A singular Indigenous Data Sovereignty Council is designed as the supreme global authority for Indigenous data. A Global Data Commons Trust is expected to steward public and environmental data on behalf of humanity. A Digital Justice Tribunal supplies enforcement. Regional councils adapt but remain subordinate. Taxation, certification, access restrictions, model shutdowns, emergency freezes, and trade sanctions complete the chain.

The framework therefore risks creating the institution it is designed to prevent: a **global data sovereign** that determines what data is, who may control it, which technical systems are acceptable, when consent may be overridden, which records must persist, which models may operate, and which actors may participate in the digital commons.

> **Aurora v1.2 is strongest as a rights, fiduciary, interoperability, and lifecycle framework. It is weakest where it assumes one global authority may classify, certify, tax, override, freeze, adjudicate, and technically standardize the world’s data systems.**

## Central audit claim

> **The Aurora Accord should govern relationships, duties, interfaces, and remedies around data. It should not declare all data to be owned by one of three sovereigns, create a universal identity layer, establish one global data regulator, appoint one Indigenous proxy authority, or convert technical standards into legal jurisdiction.**

## Readiness verdict

**Amber-red: structurally revise to v1.3 before any binding certification, cross-border enforcement, emergency override, mandatory identity, global-trust custody, taxation, or AI-model shutdown mechanism is piloted.**

Voluntary technical pilots may continue where they are independently authorized, rights-protecting, locally owned, reversible, and do not imply Treaty accession or global jurisdiction. The current document should not be treated as a ready international agreement or an operational mandate.

## Required constitutional shift

Aurora v1.3 should move from a vertically integrated global regime to a **federated data-rights and stewardship architecture** built around:

1. valid legal and institutional authority for each consequential action;
2. rights and duties rather than universal ownership claims;
3. plural personal, relational, collective, public, ecological, evidentiary, and protected-data categories;
4. affected-community and affected-nation authority rather than proxy councils;
5. purpose limitation, necessity, minimization, and lifecycle accountability;
6. layered disclosure rather than universal public registries;
7. federated stewardship rather than one Global Data Commons Trust;
8. functionally separated standard-setting, accreditation, supervision, incident response, adjudication, and enforcement;
9. technology-neutral, risk-based security requirements;
10. correction, deletion, expungement, protected non-transfer, and legitimate preservation;
11. independent justice under Justice Systems v1.4;
12. emergency access under Treaty v1.3 rather than GDGC self-authorization.

# 1. Audit basis and method

## 1.1 Primary source

Aurora v1.2 presents data as a sacred trust and proposes an international agreement establishing global rules for personal, community, cultural, public, environmental, and AI-related data. Its main institutions are a Global Digital Governance Council, an Indigenous Data Sovereignty Council, a Global Data Commons Trust, a Digital Justice Tribunal, and Regional Data Stewardship Councils. Its mechanisms include fiduciary certification, portability, taxation, AI-training-data registries, deletion rights, emergency sharing, cross-border rules, reparations, cybersecurity requirements, and emerging-technology protocols.

This audit evaluates that architecture as written. It does not assume that the Accord has been ratified, that the proposed institutions exist, that its legal comparisons have been independently verified, that its technical mandates are current, or that Indigenous peoples, states, communities, companies, or data subjects have accepted its authority.

## 1.2 Compatibility standard

The revised constitutional stack now establishes several controlling rules:

- GGF frameworks do not create legal authority by authorship or branding;
- powers require a valid source of law, Treaty competence, compact, trust, Indigenous authority, organizational mandate, or individual consent;
- Indigenous sovereignty is recognized, not created, aggregated, or represented through a GGF-appointed proxy;
- Meta-Governance coordinates but does not regulate or adjudicate;
- observation, classification, diagnosis, adjudication, remedy, and enforcement must remain separated;
- protected non-transfer is a valid outcome;
- public visibility does not equal permission to copy, train, commercialize, or infer;
- personal material rights and civic participation cannot depend on one identity or ledger system;
- emergency powers must be narrow, temporary, independently reviewed, and unable to create permanent jurisdiction;
- courts and remedies must follow Justice Systems v1.4;
- consequential technical requirements must be authorized, proportionate, contestable, accessible, and reversible where possible.

## 1.3 Audit tests

The review applies twelve tests.

### A. Authority-provenance test

What valid source authorizes classification, collection, access, certification, taxation, emergency override, cross-border transfer, adjudication, sanction, or model shutdown?

### B. Data-relationship test

Does the framework distinguish individual, relational, inferred, collective, public, ecological, evidentiary, operational, and protected claims, or reduce them to ownership categories?

### C. Indigenous-non-creation test

Does it recognize affected peoples’ own authorities and protocols, or construct a single global Indigenous data authority?

### D. Functional-separation test

Are standard-setting, supervision, accreditation, incident response, mediation, adjudication, and enforcement separated?

### E. Consent-and-power test

Is consent specific, informed, accessible, revocable, non-coerced, and capable of addressing collective and downstream uses? Are other lawful bases bounded rather than hidden behind consent rhetoric?

### F. Identity-and-access test

Can people exercise rights without one universal identifier, wallet, ledger, biometric, credential, or documentation system?

### G. Lifecycle test

Are collection, inference, access, retention, correction, portability, deletion, archival preservation, model training, backups, and disposal governed separately?

### H. Protected-non-transfer test

Can a person or community legitimately refuse digitization, disclosure, registry inclusion, cross-border transfer, AI training, publication, or archival retention?

### I. Security-and-technology-neutrality test

Are security requirements risk-based and outcome-oriented, or does the framework constitutionalize one technical stack?

### J. Emergency-data test

Can a data authority expand access, freeze rights, disclose records, or retain data under emergency language without lawful declaration and independent review?

### K. Justice-and-remedy test

Are data harms referred to valid courts and remedial systems, with standing, evidence, defence, appeal, proportionality, and continuity protections?

### L. Failure-and-exit test

Can institutions, communities, and individuals withdraw, migrate, delete, preserve locally, or reject integration without losing basic rights or being classified as non-compliant holdouts?

# 2. What Aurora v1.2 is trying to solve

Aurora should be read sympathetically before it is criticized. It addresses six genuine failures.

## 2.1 Extraction without meaningful agency

Contemporary platforms, data brokers, employers, governments, and model developers can collect, combine, infer, and monetize information in ways that individuals and communities cannot realistically observe or contest.

## 2.2 Collective and Indigenous harms

Individual consent models do not adequately address data that represents a people, territory, language, community, lineage, sacred site, ecological relation, or historically extracted archive.

## 2.3 Cross-border fragmentation

Data flows across jurisdictions whose laws, remedies, security expectations, and state-access powers differ. Interoperability without safeguards enables extraction; localization without portability can entrench domestic control.

## 2.4 AI-training opacity

Large-scale training can sever data from provenance, consent, purpose, context, and downstream accountability. Deletion from a source repository does not automatically remove influence from a trained model.

## 2.5 Security externalities

Poor security practices expose people and institutions beyond the organization that chose the risk. Small organizations may lack capacity, while universal compliance regimes can exclude them.

## 2.6 Ecological and material costs

Data infrastructure consumes energy, water, land, minerals, hardware, and labour. Data minimization is therefore both a dignity and ecological principle.

These are real constitutional design objects. The problem is not that Aurora addresses them. The problem is that its proposed solutions often depend on a central authority whose jurisdiction, representativeness, and technical competence are assumed rather than constituted.

# 3. Foundational distinction: data governance is not data sovereignty

## 3.1 Source architecture

Aurora uses sovereignty language for individuals, communities, Indigenous peoples, regions, and global data governance. It also describes the Accord as establishing the foundational rules of the digital age.

## 3.2 Architectural problem

“Sovereignty” carries different meanings across these contexts:

- an individual possesses rights, agency, dignity, privacy, and control interests;
- an Indigenous people may possess inherent political and legal sovereignty;
- a state possesses public jurisdiction under constitutional and international law;
- a community may hold collective governance rights through custom, association, cooperative law, trust, or compact;
- a data custodian possesses duties, not sovereignty over data subjects;
- a standards body possesses delegated technical authority, not general public jurisdiction.

Collapsing these into one term obscures conflicts rather than resolving them. An individual’s access right may conflict with another person’s privacy. A community’s cultural restriction may conflict with a member’s personal copy. A public-health authority may have a lawful but bounded processing basis without individual opt-in. A court may preserve evidence that a data subject wants deleted. An Indigenous nation’s authority cannot be reduced to a stakeholder veto inside a global council.

## 3.3 Required correction

Aurora v1.3 should use a **Data Rights, Duties, and Authority Matrix** rather than treating sovereignty as one generalized form of control. Each data operation should identify:

- affected persons and collectives;
- claimed rights and duties;
- source of legal or customary authority;
- purpose and necessity;
- applicable jurisdiction;
- decision-maker;
- challenge and remedy;
- duration;
- transfer limits;
- protected non-transfer status.

The term **Indigenous data sovereignty** should be retained where Indigenous peoples use it for their own authority. The GGF should not generalize that authority into a universal institutional template.

# 4. The three-fold commons classification is too coarse

## 4.1 Source architecture

Aurora divides data into:

1. personal data belonging to the individual;
2. community and cultural data belonging to the collective;
3. public and environmental data stewarded for humanity and the planet.

This is a useful first refusal of the idea that all data is interchangeable. It is not sufficient as an operational rights system.

## 4.2 Personal data are often relational

A message, family photograph, genetic sequence, transaction, social graph, workplace record, household-energy profile, or location trace may concern several people. One person cannot always port, publish, delete, or license the record without affecting others.

Inferred data create another problem. A risk score, predicted sexuality, political profile, inferred health condition, or network classification may never have been “generated by” the person, yet may affect them more consequentially than the raw data.

## 4.3 Collective data are not owned by a self-evident collective

The source does not establish:

- who defines the community;
- who may represent it;
- how members and non-members are identified;
- how internal dissent is protected;
- how overlapping communities resolve claims;
- how diaspora, displaced, adopted, mixed, or contested identities are treated;
- whether an organization, government, Elder, council, cooperative, or assembly may consent;
- whether collective authority survives individual objection;
- whether an individual may access data about themselves inside a protected collective archive.

“Belongs to the collective” can therefore transfer power from corporations to an unreviewed internal authority.

## 4.4 Public data are not automatically safe commons

“Public” may describe legal status, accessibility, funding source, observation location, publication history, or claimed social value. It does not mean unrestricted reuse is harmless.

Environmental and public datasets can expose:

- sacred places;
- endangered species;
- migration routes;
- critical infrastructure;
- shelters and humanitarian facilities;
- undocumented populations;
- small communities vulnerable to re-identification;
- genomic groups;
- military or civil-defence systems;
- politically targeted minorities.

Public genomic sequences are especially unsuitable as an uncomplicated example of a global public good because sequence data can carry individual, familial, population, and community implications.

## 4.5 Recommended classification architecture

Aurora v1.3 should permit overlapping classifications rather than exclusive buckets:

- personal and directly identifying;
- relational and multi-subject;
- inferred or derived;
- household and familial;
- community-governed;
- Indigenous-restricted;
- sacred or intentionally unrecorded;
- professional or organizational;
- public administrative;
- environmental and ecological;
- public-interest research;
- evidentiary and legal-hold;
- security-sensitive;
- humanitarian and conflict-sensitive;
- synthetic and model-generated;
- anonymized or statistically transformed;
- metadata and audit records.

Classification should not itself determine access. It identifies relevant claims for a separate purpose, authority, necessity, and risk analysis.

# 5. The data–knowledge boundary is useful but too clean

## 5.1 Source boundary

Aurora assigns access, consent, portability, and fiduciary handling to data governance, while the Digital Commons governs meaning, interpretation, preservation, contestation, and knowledge evolution.

## 5.2 Why the distinction helps

The division prevents Aurora from becoming the sole institution governing truth, archives, epistemic dispute, and cultural meaning. It also gives technical access rules a clearer scope.

## 5.3 Why the boundary cannot be categorical

Data are never entirely prior to meaning. Collection categories, measurement instruments, labels, missingness, aggregation, file structure, ontology, translation, and provenance shape interpretation. Traditional Knowledge cannot be stripped into “data” whose access is governed separately from its meaning and authority. A dataset may be harmless in one context and culturally destructive in another. A model-training corpus can reproduce meanings even when the original records are not displayed.

## 5.4 Interface requirement

The two frameworks need a **Data–Knowledge Interface Record** specifying:

- data object and knowledge context;
- provenance and ontology;
- authorities over access and interpretation;
- protected contextual information;
- acceptable and prohibited transformations;
- citation, attribution, and benefit-sharing requirements;
- model-training status;
- dispute route;
- withdrawal and retirement consequences.

Neither framework should claim exclusive jurisdiction merely because the object has been called “data” or “knowledge.”

# 6. The Global Digital Governance Council concentrates incompatible functions

## 6.1 Source powers

The GDGC sets global ethical and technical standards, charters and audits fiduciaries, approves cybersecurity auditors, governs interoperability and cross-border flows, coordinates cyber responses, mediates data conflicts, develops emerging-technology frameworks, and participates in certification consequences and emergency access decisions.

## 6.2 Functional-concentration problem

These functions include:

- legislative or standard-setting power;
- accreditation;
- licensing or certification;
- supervision;
- investigation;
- crisis coordination;
- mediation;
- technology assessment;
- access control;
- referral for sanctions;
- quasi-constitutional interpretation.

A body cannot credibly mediate disputes about standards it wrote, investigate compliance with audits it approved, and coordinate enforcement consequences without strong separation.

## 6.3 Capture does not disappear through multi-stakeholder composition

Multi-stakeholder representation can broaden input. It does not answer:

- what law creates the council;
- which actors are Parties;
- which decisions bind non-participants;
- how seats are allocated;
- whether corporations vote on duties imposed on themselves;
- how conflicts are disclosed and adjudicated;
- how marginalized participants are resourced;
- what appeal exists;
- whether regional or local authorities may adopt equivalent standards;
- how the council is dissolved.

## 6.4 Recommended separation

Aurora v1.3 should distinguish:

1. **Accord Assembly or authorized public rule-maker** — adopts binding rules only within conferred Treaty competence;
2. **Technical Standards Forum** — develops voluntary or delegated standards through open processes;
3. **Accreditation Network** — recognizes qualified independent auditors and certifiers;
4. **Data Rights and Access Secretariat** — administrative support, complaints routing, education, and records;
5. **Cyber Incident Coordination Network** — voluntary and legally authorized operational coordination;
6. **Independent Supervisory Authorities** — investigate under applicable law;
7. **Justice institutions** — determine jurisdiction, liability, remedies, and sanctions under Justice Systems v1.4;
8. **Meta-Governance interface** — convenes competent actors but does not regulate.

No body should control the whole chain.

# 7. The Indigenous Data Sovereignty Council conflicts with recognition-not-creation

## 7.1 Source architecture

Aurora creates a permanent Indigenous Data Sovereignty Council as the supreme authority on Indigenous data and Traditional Knowledge. It grants the council absolute veto power within the GDGC and gives it authority to audit all GDGC decisions.

## 7.2 Directional strength

The framework correctly recognizes that ordinary individual-consent and state-regulation models cannot govern Indigenous data, knowledge, language, territories, or cultural relations. It also attempts to prevent global institutions from overriding Indigenous interests.

## 7.3 Constitutional contradiction

The GGF cannot determine:

- that one council represents Indigenous peoples globally;
- who sits on it;
- what makes it supreme;
- which data are Indigenous;
- which nation has standing;
- whether one council may veto for another people;
- whether a transnational body may override an affected nation’s own decision;
- whether an audit pass rate determines validity.

An absolute global veto is simultaneously too broad and too weak. It is too broad where a council claims authority over peoples it does not represent. It is too weak where an affected nation’s rights are reduced to a seat or veto inside a body whose basic jurisdiction the GGF created.

## 7.4 Required replacement

Aurora v1.3 should:

- remove the universal council as a GGF-created institution;
- recognize Indigenous-created councils, networks, confederations, trusts, and nation-specific data authorities where they exist;
- require affected-nation standing for decisions concerning that people’s data, territory, language, law, knowledge, identity, or sacred relations;
- record non-engagement as a protected boundary;
- allow bounded attestations without compelled disclosure;
- permit protected non-transfer and intentional non-digitization;
- require community-controlled storage, deletion, succession, and breach protocols;
- prevent public visibility from becoming permission to scrape or train;
- route disputes through nation-specific agreements and valid justice institutions.

Indigenous participation must not function as a certification seal for the global data regime.

# 8. The Global Data Commons Trust assumes a commons that has not been constituted

## 8.1 Source architecture

The Global Data Commons Trust is expected to steward public and environmental data for research and public good, potentially using blockchain, DAOs, smart contracts, or modular civic technology.

## 8.2 Authority problem

A trust can govern only assets and duties lawfully placed within it. The phrase “global data commons” does not establish:

- title or custodianship;
- donor authority;
- affected-party consent;
- Indigenous territorial claims;
- state secrecy and public-record law;
- scientific-repository duties;
- ecological-risk restrictions;
- rights of persons represented in the data;
- withdrawal and correction rights;
- which jurisdiction supervises the trustee.

## 8.3 Centralization risk

One global trust creates a high-value target and a political choke point. Even federated storage can be centrally governed through schemas, credentials, APIs, contractual terms, and certification.

## 8.4 Recommended architecture

Replace the singular trust with a **Federated Public-Interest Data Stewardship Network**. Each node should have:

- a valid constituting instrument;
- a defined dataset or domain;
- provenance and authority records;
- affected-party and affected-community standing;
- purpose and permitted-use rules;
- access and security tiers;
- protected non-transfer rules;
- correction and withdrawal procedures;
- benefit and burden allocation;
- ecological and security review;
- independent supervision;
- interoperability without forced central custody.

The network may publish a catalogue of available resources without publishing protected content or implying universal openness.

# 9. Aurora cannot create or control the Digital Justice Tribunal

## 9.1 Source architecture

The source assigns the Tribunal jurisdiction over digital dignity crimes, mass surveillance, cyberattacks, systemic data violations, sovereignty disputes, AI-training transparency, public-interest deletion overrides, reparations, and misuse of commons data. It contemplates fines and model shutdowns.

## 9.2 Conflict with Justice Systems v1.4

Aurora is a domain framework. It cannot itself create:

- offences;
- criminal jurisdiction;
- a court;
- rules of evidence;
- standing;
- model-shutdown authority;
- public-interest overrides;
- fines;
- sanctions;
- enforcement bodies.

Those require Treaty law, domestic law, compact authority, or a separately ratified protocol and must follow Justice Systems v1.4.

## 9.3 Required treatment

Aurora v1.3 should define:

- substantive data rights and duties proposed for lawful adoption;
- administrative complaint pathways;
- investigation and evidence-preservation standards;
- referral criteria;
- remedy options;
- a Justice Referral Record.

A valid court then determines jurisdiction, applicable law, evidence, defence, remedy, appeal, and enforcement. “Model shutdown” should be an exceptional, proportionate judicial or lawfully authorized regulatory remedy, with consideration of affected users, safety functions, workers, customers, research, and less restrictive alternatives.

# 10. Regional councils do not yet create polycentrism

## 10.1 Source architecture

Regional Data Stewardship Councils adapt global principles, mediate disputes, and implement localized policies while maintaining GDGC standards. Matters affecting more than two regions escalate globally.

## 10.2 Problems

- Geographic count does not establish jurisdiction.
- Two-region impact may be trivial; one-region impact may affect fundamental rights.
- “Alignment” can turn adaptation into implementation of centrally fixed policy.
- Regional councils can become unreviewed regulators or mediators.
- Indigenous, national, municipal, sectoral, and commons jurisdictions do not map neatly onto regions.

## 10.3 Required replacement

Use a **Polycentric Data Interface Record** that identifies:

- data operation;
- affected jurisdictions and communities;
- local, national, Indigenous, sectoral, commons, and Treaty authority;
- interoperability need;
- conflict-of-law issue;
- transfer route;
- competent supervisory and judicial bodies;
- unresolved boundaries.

Escalation should depend on valid jurisdiction, material externality, rights impact, and Treaty competence—not region count.

# 11. Data fiduciary duties need a duty architecture, not one global certification

## 11.1 Valuable insight

The source correctly shifts responsibility from individuals clicking consent notices to institutions holding power over data. Duties of loyalty, care, confidentiality, security, explanation, and non-exploitation can be stronger than formal consent alone.

## 11.2 Universal-fiduciary problem

Not every data holder has the same duty. A doctor, public statistical office, cooperative, social platform, court, archivist, employer, research consortium, Indigenous repository, model developer, and emergency responder occupy different relationships.

“Prioritize data subjects’ interests” is also incomplete where:

- several subjects have conflicting interests;
- a community has collective restrictions;
- a court must preserve evidence;
- public health processing is lawfully necessary;
- an archive protects historical accountability;
- environmental data support public safety;
- a fiduciary owes duties to several beneficiaries.

## 11.3 Certification risk

A GDGC certificate can become:

- a market-entry licence;
- a reputational monopoly;
- a procurement gate;
- a data-access passport;
- an indirect sanction;
- a barrier to small or local systems;
- a defence against liability despite actual harm.

## 11.4 Recommended architecture

Aurora v1.3 should define role-specific fiduciary and stewardship duties, including:

- loyalty or purpose fidelity;
- care and security;
- confidentiality;
- conflict disclosure;
- minimization;
- non-retaliation;
- explanation;
- correction;
- portability;
- deletion or lawful retention;
- benefit and burden fairness;
- special duties toward children, workers, patients, migrants, and vulnerable groups;
- collective and Indigenous obligations;
- breach response;
- record preservation.

Certification should be optional unless lawfully required. Where required, it needs plural accredited certifiers, transparent criteria, proportional pathways, mutual recognition, challenge, appeal, conflict controls, and no denial of essential public rights merely because an institution lacks a label.

# 12. Consent dashboards do not solve the consent problem

## 12.1 Source promise

Aurora imagines users and communities granting, reviewing, porting, and revoking permissions through dashboards, cooperatives, personal vaults, or open-source portals.

## 12.2 Consent can be structurally invalid

Consent is not meaningful where:

- access to work, healthcare, education, housing, credit, public benefits, or essential communication depends on agreement;
- the future uses are unknowable;
- data concern several people;
- community authority is contested;
- interfaces manipulate choices;
- withdrawal imposes severe cost;
- data have already been combined, inferred, sold, or used for model training;
- surveillance is continuous and invisible;
- a child or dependent person cannot meaningfully refuse.

## 12.3 Consent is not the only lawful basis

Some processing may rely on contract, legal duty, vital interest, public task, legitimate interest, research authorization, employment law, or judicial order. Aurora should not hide these bases behind the language of consent. It should name them, narrow them, and make them contestable.

## 12.4 Required consent architecture

A Consent and Lawful Basis Record should specify:

- actor and authority;
- precise purpose;
- data and inference categories;
- required and optional processing;
- power relationship;
- affected third parties;
- duration;
- recipients and onward transfer;
- AI-training and derivative-use status;
- refusal consequences;
- withdrawal mechanics;
- what withdrawal can and cannot technically undo;
- complaint and remedy;
- collective or Indigenous authorization where relevant.

Consent interfaces should support refusal as easily as acceptance and should not become universal identity or surveillance infrastructure.

# 13. Portability and the Right to Exit require relational safeguards

## 13.1 Source strength

Portability can reduce lock-in, support competition, and allow people to move between services without abandoning their social history or creative work.

## 13.2 Risks

One-click export of social graphs, messages, photographs, transactions, or workplace histories may transfer other people’s data. Open APIs can increase scraping and attack surfaces. Portability can become theoretical where formats are technically open but semantically incompatible. A platform may export data without exporting moderation context, provenance, accessibility settings, or community governance.

## 13.3 Required correction

A portability right should distinguish:

- data supplied by the person;
- observed data;
- inferred data;
- jointly created data;
- third-party data;
- public posts;
- private communications;
- legal and moderation records;
- credentials and reputation;
- content licences;
- community-governed data.

Transfer must protect third parties, preserve provenance, avoid exporting hidden risk labels as facts, and provide accessible formats. Equivalent local or self-hosted implementations should qualify without mandatory connection to a global platform.

# 14. AI-training transparency cannot mean universal public dataset registries

## 14.1 Source architecture

Aurora requires public, auditable registries of AI-training datasets and authorizes fines or model shutdowns for non-compliance.

## 14.2 Transparency conflict

A fully public registry can itself expose:

- personal information;
- protected Traditional Knowledge;
- confidential business data;
- copyrighted works;
- security-sensitive collections;
- vulnerable communities;
- dataset locations;
- model attack surfaces;
- research participants.

A registry entry may also falsely imply that naming a dataset establishes lawful use.

## 14.3 Required layered disclosure

Aurora v1.3 should require a **Training Data Provenance and Lawful Use Record** with disclosure tiers:

### Public layer

- broad source categories;
- collection periods;
- jurisdictions;
- major languages and populations;
- lawful-basis categories;
- known exclusions;
- rights and complaint routes;
- evaluation and limitation summary.

### Regulator or independent-auditor layer

- detailed provenance;
- contracts and licences;
- consent and community-authority records;
- data-protection assessments;
- opt-out and deletion handling;
- sampling and filtering methods;
- security controls.

### Protected layer

- confidential, personal, Indigenous-restricted, security-sensitive, or legally privileged details accessible only under valid authority.

Transparency must include model-generated and synthetic data, data transformations, deduplication, filtering, labeling, and downstream fine-tuning—not only dataset names.

# 15. Deletion, forgetting, and model influence need separate remedies

## 15.1 Source architecture

Aurora promises permanent deletion across systems, including AI-training sets, unless a public-interest override applies.

## 15.2 Technical and legal overstatement

Deletion has different meanings:

- removal from an active database;
- deletion from indexes and caches;
- expiry of access rights;
- deletion from backups after rotation;
- preservation under legal hold;
- de-identification;
- archival restriction;
- removal from future training;
- retraining or machine unlearning;
- suppression of specific outputs;
- correction of an inferred profile;
- de-listing from search.

Once a model has been trained, deleting a source record may not remove its statistical influence. Promising universal permanent deletion can create false assurance.

## 15.3 Required remedy ladder

Aurora v1.3 should provide a Model Data Withdrawal and Remediation Record considering:

1. stop future collection;
2. stop onward transfer;
3. remove source data from active systems;
4. schedule backup expiry;
5. prohibit future training or fine-tuning;
6. correct labels and derived profiles;
7. evaluate feasible machine unlearning or retraining;
8. suppress memorized or harmful outputs;
9. document residual influence and uncertainty;
10. compensate or provide other remedy where removal is impossible;
11. preserve evidence only under valid legal hold;
12. permit challenge and independent review.

Public-interest retention decisions belong to valid law and justice institutions, not GDGC discretion.

# 16. Immutable audit trails conflict with correction and protected non-transfer

## 16.1 Source architecture

The technical appendix recommends blockchain or equivalent tamper-proof logging for all data access and transactions.

## 16.2 Core conflict

Auditability requires evidence that an event occurred. It does not require immutable storage of personal content, identifiers, cultural knowledge, allegations, or access details forever.

Immutable systems can conflict with:

- correction;
- expungement;
- rehabilitation;
- witness protection;
- sealed proceedings;
- child privacy;
- Indigenous deletion or return;
- security incident containment;
- lawful erasure;
- changed consent;
- data minimization.

## 16.3 Corrected architecture

Use **verifiable event integrity** rather than immutable personal records:

- append-only or signed event metadata where justified;
- off-chain or separately controlled content;
- revocable keys;
- correction links;
- retention schedules;
- pseudonymous and role-limited identifiers;
- sealed or protected logs;
- deletion of content while preserving proof of compliant deletion;
- independent audit without universal public visibility.

Blockchain, conventional databases, paper logs, secure hardware, or federated systems may all be appropriate depending on risk and context.

# 17. Universal identity infrastructure must be prohibited

## 17.1 Source architecture

Aurora promotes decentralized identifiers, zero-knowledge proofs, consent dashboards, data vaults, audit trails, and cross-framework infrastructure.

## 17.2 Identity-linkage risk

Even decentralized identifiers can become mandatory credentials linking:

- public benefits;
- justice access;
- AUBI;
- health;
- work;
- voting;
- education;
- travel;
- platform participation;
- reputation.

A technically decentralized identifier can still be institutionally centralized through schemas, issuers, revocation lists, wallets, or acceptance rules.

## 17.3 Identity function separation

Aurora v1.3 should require:

- no universal GGF identity;
- no Love Ledger or Hearts identity function;
- purpose-specific credentials;
- minimal disclosure;
- anonymous and pseudonymous options where lawful;
- offline and low-documentation access;
- stateless and displaced-person pathways;
- separation of authentication from reputation;
- no cross-domain linkage without independent authorization;
- credential correction and appeal;
- local alternatives and interoperability;
- explicit prohibition on biometric compulsion except under narrowly valid law.

A person must not need to become legible to the whole GGF ecosystem to exercise one right.

# 18. The proposed security stack is too prescriptive and brittle

## 18.1 Source architecture

Aurora mandates or strongly prescribes post-quantum algorithms, QKD, DIDs, zero-knowledge proofs, secure multiparty computation, immutable logs, endpoint detection, specific network controls, VPNs, automated incident response, 24-hour recovery, 48-hour public reporting, and universal adoption dates.

## 18.2 Problems

- Risk differs across systems.
- Some techniques may be unavailable, immature, unaffordable, inappropriate, or insecure in implementation.
- QKD requires specialized infrastructure and does not replace ordinary security.
- “Quantum-resistant” algorithms and standards evolve.
- Mandatory automation can create new failure modes.
- Public reporting within forty-eight hours can expose vulnerabilities or ongoing investigations.
- Universal recovery objectives can be unrealistic for small or conflict-affected systems.
- Prescriptive standards favour vendors capable of certification.
- Security requirements can become a pretext for excluding local systems.

## 18.3 Required replacement

Aurora v1.3 should establish outcome-oriented security duties:

- risk assessment;
- threat modeling;
- least privilege;
- segmentation;
- secure development;
- patch and vulnerability management;
- encryption appropriate to risk;
- resilient backups;
- incident detection and response;
- supply-chain security;
- independent testing;
- human and organizational safeguards;
- recovery and continuity;
- proportional disclosure;
- accessible alternatives;
- periodic standards revision.

Technical profiles may offer reference implementations, but no single stack should become constitutional law.

# 19. Emergency data powers are self-authorizing

## 19.1 Source architecture

Aurora invokes Meta-Governance crisis tiers, permits rapid sharing, access-right freezes, system lockdowns, GDGC-approved emergency access, mandatory response measures, and seventy-two-hour sunsets extendable by a GDGC supermajority.

## 19.2 Authority defect

A data council cannot create emergency authority merely because a cyberattack, pandemic, conflict, or infrastructure failure occurs. It cannot suspend access rights, override consent, compel sharing, or authorize forensic access without valid law or delegated authority.

## 19.3 Self-extension problem

Allowing the GDGC to extend its own emergency measures conflates:

- incident operator;
- emergency declarer;
- rights restrictor;
- reviewer;
- extension authority.

## 19.4 Required emergency architecture

Each emergency data action should require an **Emergency Data Access and Continuity Record** stating:

- lawful declaration and authority;
- crisis class;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- data and systems affected;
- purpose and necessity;
- less intrusive alternatives;
- authorized users;
- duration and automatic expiry;
- independent extension authority;
- individual and collective rights minima;
- Indigenous and local interfaces;
- access logging;
- notice or delayed-notice justification;
- deletion and restoration after the emergency;
- appeal and remedy;
- after-action review.

Before Treaty authority is active, Aurora may coordinate voluntary assistance and referrals only. Meta-Governance may convene; it cannot supply emergency jurisdiction.

# 20. Incident transparency must not become secondary harm

## 20.1 Source promise

Rapid public reporting is intended to ensure accountability after cyberattacks and data breaches.

## 20.2 Risks

Immediate public detail can:

- reveal unpatched vulnerabilities;
- identify victims;
- expose Indigenous or community data holdings;
- compromise law-enforcement or forensic work;
- enable copycat attacks;
- violate contractual or legal duties;
- create misinformation before facts are known.

## 20.3 Layered incident disclosure

Incident response should separate:

- immediate affected-person notice where protective action is possible;
- confidential notification to competent authorities;
- public preliminary notice with uncertainty;
- protected technical indicators shared with trusted responders;
- later full report after containment;
- corrections and residual-risk updates.

Delay must be justified and reviewable, but fixed public-reporting clocks should not override safety.

# 21. Cross-border flows require conflict-of-law architecture

## 21.1 Source architecture

Aurora promises consent, security, regional sovereignty, and interoperable standards for cross-border data movement.

## 21.2 Missing questions

The framework does not fully specify:

- which law applies;
- how state access requests are handled;
- whether onward transfer is allowed;
- how adequacy or equivalence is assessed;
- who bears liability;
- where a person may seek remedy;
- how localization demands are reviewed;
- how cloud replication and backups count;
- how conflict, occupation, sanctions, or regime change affect custody;
- how Indigenous territorial authority interacts with national law;
- what happens when two lawful obligations conflict.

## 21.3 Required transfer architecture

A Cross-Border Data Transfer Record should identify:

- exporter, importer, processor, and sub-processors;
- data and classification;
- affected persons and communities;
- source and destination jurisdictions;
- lawful basis;
- purpose;
- security and retention;
- government-access environment;
- onward-transfer limits;
- local storage or escrow requirements;
- complaint and remedy;
- suspension trigger;
- conflict-of-law procedure;
- Indigenous or community authorization;
- emergency return or deletion.

No regional or global council should mediate a legal conflict unless the parties validly consent or a court has jurisdiction.

# 22. Government access is a missing constitutional object

Aurora focuses heavily on corporate extraction but less clearly on state access. A complete framework must govern:

- law-enforcement requests;
- intelligence access;
- national-security orders;
- public-health demands;
- tax and benefits administration;
- immigration and border systems;
- emergency services;
- courts and evidence preservation;
- foreign-government requests;
- secret orders and delayed notice.

Aurora v1.3 should require a Government Data Access Record with valid authority, necessity, scope, minimization, duration, independent approval where required, notice rules, secrecy review, challenge, audit, and remedy. “Public interest” must not be a GDGC-defined residual override.

# 23. Data taxation and Hearts rewards conflict with the revised economic stack

## 23.1 Source architecture

Aurora proposes a global data tax, penalties for hoarding, fixed revenue allocations, Indigenous infrastructure percentages, data reparations funds, impact bonds, and Hearts rewards for open sourcing and high algorithmic-accountability ratings.

## 23.2 Authority problem

The Accord cannot self-authorize taxation. Treaty v1.3 requires separate activation for compulsory revenue. Financial mechanisms belong under Financial Systems v3.4 and valid domestic or Treaty law.

## 23.3 Incentive problem

Rewarding “ethical data stewardship” with Hearts can create surveillance and metric pressure. Open-sourcing a dataset may be unethical where it contains personal, collective, protected, or security-sensitive information. A rating by one office can become an economic gate.

## 23.4 Required correction

Move tax rates, revenue scenarios, bonds, and allocations into a separate Data Finance and Reparations Protocol. Aurora should state principles:

- polluter or extractor contribution where lawfully adopted;
- no sale of consent;
- no Hearts or Love Ledger requirement;
- no economic reward for disclosing protected data;
- independent fiduciary administration;
- affected-community participation;
- transparent use of funds;
- no funder authority over data governance;
- justice-based reparations rather than certification bonuses.

# 24. Data reparations require legal and community authority

## 24.1 Source strength

The framework correctly recognizes that historical archives, censuses, language recordings, social-media extraction, research collections, and digitized cultural materials can embody continuing harm.

## 24.2 Current gaps

The proposed hybrid tribunal and global review panels do not establish:

- claimant standing;
- applicable law;
- evidence standards;
- collective representation;
- limitation periods;
- successor responsibility;
- non-monetary remedies;
- protected evidence;
- appeal;
- fund custody;
- interaction with Indigenous law.

## 24.3 Required reparations architecture

A Data Reparations Claim Record should permit remedies including:

- return or transfer of custody;
- deletion;
- restricted access;
- correction of records;
- public acknowledgment;
- community-controlled archives;

# 25. Ecological data governance requires precaution without universal openness

Aurora rightly links digital systems to energy, water, minerals, hardware, land, and biodiversity. But environmental data should not be treated as a single globally open class.

Aurora v1.3 should distinguish:

- planetary monitoring data suitable for broad use;
- local environmental observations;
- Indigenous territorial knowledge;
- endangered-species and habitat-sensitive records;
- critical water and infrastructure data;
- commercially generated environmental claims;
- regulatory evidence;
- model outputs and uncertainty.

Access decisions should consider ecological harm from disclosure, not only ecological benefit from openness. Environmental standards and data-center caps require valid environmental and energy authority, not merely data-governance competence.

# 26. Neural and biometric data require a stronger protected category

## 26.1 Source strength

The BCI provision correctly recognizes continuous consent and user control as essential.

## 26.2 Missing protections

Neural data can reveal or be used to infer attention, emotion, intention, health, identity, disability, political or spiritual experience, and cognitive patterns. Biometric systems can become irreversible identifiers.

## 26.3 Required neural-data protocol

Aurora v1.3 should establish:

- mental privacy and cognitive liberty;
- prohibition on covert neural inference;
- purpose-specific and continuously revocable consent;
- no employer, insurer, school, creditor, police, or platform compulsion except under narrowly valid law and stringent review;
- no secondary training use by default;
- no transfer of raw neural data where derived local computation suffices;
- right to local processing;
- correction of inferences;
- protection against identity linking;
- deletion and model-remediation duties;
- special rules for children and dependent persons;
- emergency use limits;
- independent justice and remedy.

“Continuous consent” must not mean continuous prompting that normalizes surveillance.

# 27. Epistemic justice cannot be administered by a data regulator

Aurora’s commitment to dismantling knowledge hierarchies is important. But a data authority cannot decide that different knowledge systems are “equally valid,” determine Traditional Knowledge authenticity, or translate cultural authority into certification.

Its legitimate obligations are narrower:

- do not erase provenance;
- do not force disclosure;
- preserve ontology and context;
- enable affected authorities to govern access;
- disclose uncertainty and missingness;
- prevent extraction and decontextualization;
- support plural evidence under Justice Systems v1.4;
- preserve protected non-transfer.

Epistemic disagreement belongs to the relevant knowledge, scientific, Indigenous, public, or judicial process—not to GDGC truth administration.

# 28. Observation infrastructure must not become a universal sensor layer

The revised constitutional stack depends on Observation Contracts, State Estimate Records, and Independence Maps. Aurora should supply secure, rights-respecting data interfaces for those records. It must not decide:

- what variables every institution must observe;
- which model is authoritative;
- that missing data imply non-compliance;
- that more collection is always better;
- that a dashboard creates jurisdiction;
- that cross-framework interoperability permits cross-purpose reuse.

Each consequential observation system needs:

- decision purpose;
- variables and proxies;
- authority;
- affected populations;
- collection and inference limits;
- uncertainty;
- independence;
- retention;
- contestability;
- sensor-change governance;
- protected non-observation and non-transfer.

Learning can require deleting sensors, narrowing data, or accepting uncertainty.

# 29. The GDPR+ claim and legal readiness require external verification

Aurora describes itself as exceeding GDPR and proposes a global compatibility certification. This internal architectural audit does not verify current European, national, sectoral, cybersecurity, AI, competition, consumer, research, employment, health, criminal, or international law.

Aurora v1.3 should avoid claiming legal superiority by label. A legal compatibility assessment must be:

- jurisdiction-specific;
- dated and versioned;
- conducted by qualified independent reviewers;
- explicit about unresolved conflict;
- limited to defined provisions;
- non-substitutive for regulator or court decisions;
- regularly updated.

“GDPR+” may remain a design aspiration or mapping label, not a universal certificate of legal compliance.

# 34. Recommended Aurora Accord v1.3 architecture

## Proposed title

**Aurora Accord v1.3 — Data Rights, Fiduciary Duties, Federated Stewardship, and Digital Sovereignty Interfaces**

Replace exclusive ownership buckets with overlapping rights and risk classes.

Recognize Indigenous-created authorities, affected-nation standing, protected non-transfer, non-engagement, and bounded attestation.

Collection, correction, portability, deletion, backups, archives, legal holds, model influence, and disposal.

Domain-specific trusts and repositories with valid constituting authority.

### 12. Interoperability and portability

Open standards, relational safeguards, semantic compatibility, local equivalents, and contestable audits.

### 13. Cross-border flows and government access

Conflict-of-law, adequacy, onward transfer, state access, remedy, localization, and suspension.

### 14. AI-training data and model governance

Layered provenance, lawful use, protected sources, withdrawal, unlearning, output remedy, and audit.

### 15. Public, environmental, and ecological data

Access tiers, ecological security, Indigenous territorial knowledge, and public-interest use.

### 16. Neural, biometric, and intimate data

Mental privacy, cognitive liberty, non-inference, local processing, and anti-compulsion protections.

### 17. Security and resilience

Risk-based, outcome-oriented, technology-neutral controls and accessible profiles.

### 18. Incident response and breach notification

Layered notification, containment, correction, restitution, and after-action review.

### 19. Emergency data governance

Treaty-aligned authority, necessity, independent review, local interfaces, and restoration.

### 20. Audit records, institutional memory, and protected non-transfer

No vertically integrated GDGC.

Administrative support plus Justice Systems v1.4 routes.

### 23. Data reparations and historical extraction

Return, custody, deletion, compensation, benefit sharing, and community-defined remedies.

### 24. Ecological and labour footprint of data systems

Valid environmental authority, worker rights, energy, water, hardware, and supply-chain duties.

### 25. Finance and public investment boundaries

Genesis v0.9 sequence, reversible pilots, non-member status, and no coercive holdout policy.

Separate metrics, no automatic authority, continuity, portability, and legitimate closure.

### 28. Appendices and operational records

Reusable records rather than mandatory technology.

# 36. Falsification gates

Aurora v1.3 should not be treated as constitutionally ready unless it can pass these tests.

## Gate 1 — No framework-to-law conversion

A framework rule is not enforceable until independently adopted through valid law, Treaty authority, compact, trust, or consent.

## Gate 2 — No universal ownership classification

No data object is assigned to one owner merely because it is labeled personal, community, public, or environmental.

## Gate 3 — No Indigenous proxy sovereignty

No GGF-created council represents or vetoes for non-participating Indigenous peoples.

## Gate 4 — No vertically integrated data sovereign

No institution controls standard-setting, certification, supervision, crisis response, adjudication, and enforcement.

## Gate 5 — No consent theatre

A dashboard click does not legalize processing under coercion, opacity, or severe power imbalance.

## Gate 6 — No universal civic or economic identity

Data rights, justice, AUBI, work, voting, health, or public services do not require one GGF credential or ledger.

## Gate 7 — No mandatory disclosure of protected data

Transparency requirements preserve personal, Indigenous, community, confidential, evidentiary, and security restrictions.

## Gate 8 — No public-data default openness

Public funding or environmental relevance does not automatically authorize unrestricted access or AI training.

## Gate 9 — No immutable personal-data requirement

Audit integrity can coexist with correction, expungement, deletion, sealed records, and protected non-transfer.

## Gate 10 — No deletion fiction

The framework distinguishes source deletion, backup expiry, future-use prohibition, model remediation, and residual influence.

## Gate 11 — No self-authorized emergency override

A data institution cannot suspend access, override consent, or extend its own powers without external lawful authority and review.

## Gate 12 — No universal security stack

Equivalent risk-based controls and low-tech or local implementations remain possible.

## Gate 13 — No certification gate to fundamental rights

Lack or loss of certification does not by itself deny justice, essential services, or protected public-interest access.

## Gate 14 — No automatic model shutdown

A model restriction or shutdown requires valid jurisdiction, evidence, proportionality, defence, and continuity analysis.

## Gate 15 — No political or metric-conditioned Hearts reward

Data rights and duties do not depend on Love Ledger participation, Hearts awards, or one ethical rating.

## Gate 16 — No hidden government-access exception

State access is governed by explicit authority, necessity, notice, review, minimization, and remedy.

## Gate 17 — No interoperability through forced centralization

Systems may remain local or federated while supporting purpose-specific exchange.

## Gate 18 — No observation-to-authority conversion

Data quality, dashboard coverage, anomaly alerts, or missingness do not create jurisdiction or determine non-compliance.

## Gate 19 — Failure can stop scaling

Pilots may conclude that a mechanism is unsafe, unjust, unaffordable, non-transferable, or unnecessary without pressure to produce a successful global rollout.

# 38. Final assessment

Aurora v1.2 deserves credit for understanding that data governance is not a narrow privacy compliance exercise. Data architecture determines who can be seen, remembered, scored, paid, judged, represented, predicted, trained upon, excluded, and forgotten. It also recognizes that individual consent alone cannot repair collective extraction, Indigenous data colonialism, platform lock-in, opaque AI training, cyber insecurity, or the ecological burden of digital infrastructure.

Its mistake is to answer the scale of the problem with a comparably scaled center. The GDGC, universal Indigenous council, global trust, tribunal, certification system, taxation mechanism, emergency stack, and mandatory technical architecture together create a global data constitution inside a domain framework. Consent becomes a dashboard attached to a regime that has already decided the categories, standards, institutions, technologies, exceptions, and remedies.

The revision should instead constitutionalize restraint:

- no data operation without valid authority and purpose;
- no universal owner for mixed and relational data;
- no Indigenous authority created by the GGF;
- no global regulator controlling the full decision chain;
- no public-interest claim without standing and review;
- no interoperability that requires central custody;
- no auditability that forbids correction or forgetting;
- no emergency access created by the emergency actor;
- no technical stack elevated into permanent law;
- no identity system spanning every domain;
- no AI transparency rule that exposes protected sources;
- no deletion promise that conceals residual model influence;
- no data commons without a valid constituting instrument;
- no justice remedy outside Justice Systems v1.4;
- no scaling obligation when protected non-transfer or non-digitization is the legitimate result.

The governing principle for Aurora v1.3 should be:

> **Data governance allocates duties, protects rights, and enables bounded interoperability among valid authorities. It does not create one authority over the world’s data.**

The personal-data principle should be:

> **A person has enforceable rights concerning data about them, but relational and public-interest conflicts require law, proportionality, and remedy rather than a fiction of singular ownership.**

The Indigenous-data principle should be:

> **Indigenous data authority is exercised by the affected peoples through their own laws and institutions; it is not delegated to a GGF-created global council.**

The technical principle should be:

> **Security and interoperability requirements specify outcomes, risks, and rights. They do not constitutionalize one vendor, ledger, identifier, cryptographic method, or architecture.**

The final readiness test should be:

> **Could a person remain pseudonymous, an Indigenous nation decline engagement, a community prohibit digitization, a public dataset remain restricted, a small institution use an equivalent low-tech security system, a court preserve evidence, a trained model retain uncertain residual influence, and a cyber emergency occur—while the framework still responds lawfully without inventing global ownership, centralizing identity, exposing protected information, or letting the data regulator extend its own power?**

Where the answer is yes, Aurora is becoming a legitimate data-governance accord.

Where the answer is no, digital sovereignty has become another name for central legibility and control.
