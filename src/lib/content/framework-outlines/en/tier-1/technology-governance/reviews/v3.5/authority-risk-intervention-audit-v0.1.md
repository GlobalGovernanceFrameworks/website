# Technology Governance Authority, Risk Classification, Intervention, and Cognitive Sovereignty Audit v0.1

## Can a cross-cutting technology framework govern systemic risk, AI, cybersecurity, community refusal, and public-interest innovation without becoming a universal regulator or an automated moral authority?

**Status:** Internal bounded constitutional and architectural audit  
**Primary object:** Technology Governance Implementation Framework v3.5  
**Compatibility corpus:** Constitutional Interface Specification v0.1; Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3; Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing v1.1; Integrated Meta-Governance v1.5; Polycentric Governance Architecture v0.6; Genesis Protocol v0.9; Justice Systems Framework v1.4; Aurora Accord v1.3; AUBI v2.5; Financial Systems v3.4; Nested Economies v3.2; Work in Liberation v2.0  
**Audit date:** 2026-08-01  
**Recommended successor:** Technology Governance Framework v3.6

---

# Executive summary

## Overall verdict

Technology Governance v3.5 identifies a real constitutional gap. Technologies cross sectors, jurisdictions, infrastructures, and timescales faster than conventional regulatory systems can coordinate. The source correctly emphasizes refusal, cognitive liberty, accessibility, cybersecurity, labour justice, lifecycle effects, open-source maintenance, community review, Indigenous authority, and the need to govern AI as a cross-cutting force rather than as a narrow software category.

Those commitments should survive.

The framework is nevertheless **not compatible with the revised constitutional stack**. It presents itself as the GGF's “constitutional parent” for technology governance, governs “all technologies,” creates a Global Technology Council with standard-setting, risk-classification, crisis, certification, referral, audit, and funding roles, and equips that architecture with circuit breakers, pattern-detection systems, moratorium pathways, emergency triggers, sanctions, economic rewards, and automated “immune responses.”

The result is not merely coordination. It is a proposed global technology sovereign.

Several mechanisms are especially concerning because they convert contestable social diagnoses into technical or legal power:

- “parasitic pattern” detection;
- “system sanity validation”;
- “intelligence honor” classification;
- “cognitive ecology” certification;
- “shame algorithm” detection;
- “worth validation overrides”;
- “value distribution rebalancers”;
- automatic circuit breakers triggered by satisfaction indices, social division, mental-health signals, or algorithmic findings.

The underlying concerns—coercive platforms, surveillance, addictive design, pathologization, extraction, artificial scarcity, and cognitive homogenization—are legitimate. But the proposed categories are not self-interpreting facts. They are moral, political, psychological, economic, and epistemic judgments. Giving a technical council or automated system authority to classify and override technologies on those grounds would reproduce the very control architecture the framework intends to resist.

The document also relies on institutions and mechanisms that the revised stack has retired or bounded:

- a universal Earth Council and Indigenous veto proxy;
- Meta-Governance as final conflict resolver;
- a mandatory GGF Minimum Viable Security Stack;
- a vertically integrated Digital Justice Tribunal pathway;
- Hearts and Leaves incentives conditioned on certification or ideological metrics;
- Love Ledger contribution tracking;
- automatic emergency activation;
- GGF-wide technical certification as a route to market and fund access.

> **Technology Governance v3.5 is strongest as a catalogue of public-interest design concerns and weakest where it turns those concerns into general jurisdiction, certification, crisis, or automated intervention power.**

## Central audit claim

> **A technology-governance framework may provide shared risk concepts, assessment methods, interface records, public-interest standards, community-review tools, horizon scanning, and coordination support. It may not classify every technology, create binding law, determine moral or cognitive legitimacy, impose moratoria, suspend deployments, condition basic material rights, or exercise emergency powers without independently valid authority, due process, and domain-specific jurisdiction.**

## Readiness verdict

**Amber-red: revise to v3.6 before any binding certification, compulsory TRRT classification, automated circuit breaker, crisis activation, economic reward, model restriction, cross-border sanction, or technology moratorium is operationalized.**

Non-coercive starter kits, voluntary assessments, community technology review, open-source maintenance support, accessibility work, rights-preserving design guidance, and lawfully authorized local pilots may continue if they are clearly described as voluntary and do not imply GGF jurisdiction.

## Required constitutional shift

The successor should move from:

> **one constitutional parent governing all technologies**

into:

> **a federated technology-risk and public-interest interface framework that helps competent authorities and affected communities assess technologies, coordinate across domains, preserve refusal, and design lawful interventions without creating a universal regulator.**

The successor should separate at least seven functions:

1. horizon scanning and research;
2. voluntary technical standard-setting;
3. risk and impact assessment;
4. community and affected-party review;
5. lawful regulatory decision-making;
6. emergency and cybersecurity coordination;
7. adjudication, enforcement, and remedy.

No institution should control the full chain.

# 1. Audit basis and method

## 1.1 Source characterization

Technology Governance v3.5 is not a narrow sector framework. It describes itself as a Tier 1 constitutional framework, the GGF's “constitutional parent” for technology governance, and the primary framework for AI governance. It claims authority across all technologies through the Technology Risk and Responsibility Tiering System (TRRT), while delegating specialized domains to biotechnology, data, security, space, health, labour, and other frameworks.

It contains four different kinds of content:

- constitutional claims about authority and institutional position;
- regulatory mechanisms such as classification, certification, moratoria, and sanctions;
- public-interest design principles such as accessibility, refusal, security, and lifecycle responsibility;
- implementation programmes such as regional AI adaptation, open-source software commons, community review boards, and regenerative technology funding.

These categories need different authority bases. A design guide can be published voluntarily. A certification can be private, contractual, public, or compulsory. A moratorium requires legal authority. A crisis response may require operational control over systems. A sanction requires valid law and adjudication. v3.5 often places them in one framework without preserving those distinctions.

## 1.2 Compatibility standard

The revised constitutional stack establishes the following constraints:

- GGF frameworks are not self-executing law;
- authority must be traced to a Treaty provision, statute, Indigenous authority, compact, contract, property right, emergency law, or other valid source;
- Meta-Governance coordinates interfaces but does not supervise all frameworks or resolve legal conflicts;
- Indigenous authority is recognized, not created or aggregated into a universal GGF council;
- risk indicators and models may alert, but may not automatically determine capture, legitimacy, guilt, jurisdiction, or intervention;
- Justice Systems v1.4 separates law, jurisdiction, interim protection, adjudication, enforcement, appeal, and remedy;
- Aurora Accord v1.3 separates technical standards, data rights, cyber coordination, supervision, identity, and justice;
- AUBI Layer 1 is unconditional and cannot be used as a political or certification incentive;
- Financial Systems governs Hearts, Leaves, public funds, and settlement architecture;
- Genesis v0.9 prohibits pre-ratification creation of coercive or constitutional power;
- Polycentric Governance v0.6 preserves distinct territorial, commons, practice, and Indigenous jurisdictions;
- Work in Liberation governs labour rights, worker participation, platform work, and technology-related employment transition.

## 1.3 Audit tests

### A. Authority-provenance test

Does each consequential action identify the legal, contractual, Indigenous, technical, or property authority from which it derives? Does the framework distinguish recommendation from binding rule?

### B. Scope-and-domain test

Can the framework explain which technology, use, actor, territory, system, and harm it governs without claiming general authority over “all technologies”?

### C. Risk-classification test

Does risk classification evaluate use context, scale, reversibility, affected parties, deployment environment, controls, uncertainty, and cumulative effects rather than assigning one inherent tier to a technology?

### D. Functional-separation test

Are research, standard-setting, classification, certification, regulation, emergency action, adjudication, enforcement, funding, and evaluation institutionally separated?

### E. Observation-to-intervention test

Do metrics and automated detectors produce alerts and state estimates, or do they directly pause, override, redistribute, sanction, or classify?

### F. Cognitive-and-epistemic-neutrality test

Does the framework protect cognitive liberty without empowering institutions to decide which perceptions, critiques, emotional states, intelligences, or worldviews are “sane,” “honoured,” or socially valid?

### G. Indigenous-non-creation test

Does the framework identify affected Indigenous authorities and their laws, or rely on a universal Earth Council, satisfaction score, or GGF-defined FPIC proxy?

### H. Emergency-authority test

Are cyber and technology emergency powers lawfully constituted, narrowly scoped, independently reviewed, time-bounded, reversible, and unable to extend themselves?

### I. Justice-and-remedy test

Are moratoria, shutdowns, sanctions, trade restrictions, API revocations, compensation, and enforcement routed through valid law, jurisdiction, evidence, due process, appeal, and continuity protections?

### J. Data-and-identity test

Are technology governance records, AI training, identity tools, dashboards, provenance, and telemetry aligned with Aurora Accord v1.3 and protected non-transfer?

### K. Economic-non-coercion test

Do certification, funding, Hearts, Leaves, AUBI, API access, and public procurement avoid turning political or moral alignment into material dependency?

### L. Failure-and-exit test

Can a community refuse a technology, a developer contest classification, a pilot end without scaling, and the framework itself dissolve or narrow without loss of rights, data, essential services, or lawful claims?

# 2. What v3.5 is trying to preserve

The source should be read sympathetically. It addresses several real failures in current technology governance.

## 2.1 Technology as world-building

Technology is not neutral infrastructure. It shapes choice, attention, labour, mobility, identity, ecological demand, institutional memory, and access to public life. The source is right that technical architecture can silently become constitutional architecture.

## 2.2 Refusal and disconnection

The framework's strongest distinctive contribution is its insistence that people and communities need real alternatives to digitally enforced participation. The right to disconnect, refuse, use offline alternatives, and avoid systems requiring ethical compromise should remain central.

## 2.3 Cognitive liberty and accessibility

The source correctly recognizes that technology can pathologize, exclude, overwhelm, addict, rank, or homogenize users. Accessibility, non-addictive design, disability rights, neurodiversity, cognitive freedom, and protection from manipulative interfaces are legitimate governance objects.

## 2.4 Labour and economic power

Algorithmic wage theft, platform extraction, uncompensated maintenance, dependency on proprietary infrastructure, and unequal access to compute are real institutional problems. These need labour, competition, public investment, procurement, and commons responses.

## 2.5 Lifecycle and ecological effects

Technology governance cannot stop at software behaviour. Mining, energy, water, supply chains, data centres, e-waste, repairability, and hardware longevity belong in lifecycle analysis.

## 2.6 Cybersecurity and resilience

Cross-framework cyber coordination is necessary. Critical systems need incident response, continuity, recovery, disclosure, evidence preservation, and community resilience.

## 2.7 Open-source maintenance

The “tragedy of the maintainer” is an important design object. Shared public-interest software needs durable funding, governance, security, accessibility, and succession—not only publication under an open-source licence.

## 2.8 Anticipatory governance

Horizon scanning, sandboxes, pre-deployment assessment, reversible pilots, and staged evidence can reduce the pressure to regulate only after irreversible harm occurs.

The problem is not that v3.5 addresses these concerns. The problem is that it repeatedly treats the GGF framework and its institutions as the authority entitled to define, classify, certify, pause, reward, or prohibit technology across domains.

# 3. Document identity and readiness claims

## 3.1 Constitutional language exceeds outline maturity

The document calls itself a constitutional framework and an operationally enhanced primary AI-governance framework. Yet many central institutions, standards, thresholds, and technical systems remain names rather than constituted legal objects.

Examples include:

- Global Technology Council;
- Cybersecurity and Resilience Council;
- System Sanity Oversight Panel;
- Tech Crisis Response Unit;
- Ethical Circuit Breaker;
- TRRT;
- Regenerative Tech Fund;
- Office of Algorithmic Accountability;
- GGF-Aligned Technology Certification;
- Regenerative Multiplier;
- Parasitic Pattern Detection Protocol;
- Intelligence Honor Protocol.

A name, composition formula, or high-level mandate does not establish authority, evidence rules, appeal, liability, review, funding, or lawful scope.

## 3.2 Source references are partly obsolete

The framework relies on earlier forms of the Treaty, Meta-Governance, Indigenous framework, Aurora Accord, Justice Systems, AUBI, and financial architecture. Those have since been revised. Several source institutions and powers are no longer valid assumptions.

## 3.3 Pilot readiness is overstated

Voluntary design pilots may be possible. Binding pilots involving classification, compelled disclosure, emergency containment, certification exclusion, model restrictions, worker surveillance, Indigenous knowledge, or sanctions are not ready until the missing legal and procedural architecture exists.

## 3.4 Required status statement

v3.6 should clearly distinguish:

- voluntary design guidance;
- technical standards;
- private or contractual certification;
- public procurement requirements;
- statutory regulation;
- Treaty obligations;
- Indigenous law and consent;
- emergency authority;
- judicial remedies.

# 4. The “constitutional parent” claim is incompatible with plural authority

## 4.1 Source architecture

v3.5 describes itself as the GGF's constitutional parent for technology governance and the primary AI-governance framework. It provides universal principles, bodies, tools, risk tiers, standards, crisis coordination, and handoffs across specialized domains.

## 4.2 Constitutional problem

A parent framework implies hierarchy. It suggests that biotechnology, data, security, space, health, labour, disability, education, ecology, and local technology governance derive validity from Technology Governance or remain subordinate to its classifications.

But technology-related authority is distributed:

- a health regulator governs medical safety;
- a labour authority governs algorithmic employment decisions;
- an Indigenous nation governs technology affecting its territory, knowledge, and people;
- a data-protection authority governs data processing;
- a court governs remedies;
- a municipality governs local procurement and public systems;
- a technical standards body develops voluntary specifications;
- a security authority acts under separately constituted emergency or criminal law;
- a community may refuse participation without acquiring regulatory power over others.

No single framework is the parent of all these authorities.

## 4.3 Required replacement

v3.6 should describe itself as a **cross-domain interface framework**. It may provide common records, shared risk dimensions, coordination practices, public-interest standards, and referral rules. It should not claim residual jurisdiction whenever technology crosses domains.

# 5. “All technologies” is not a governable jurisdiction

## 5.1 Source scope

The framework states that it governs all technologies through TRRT, with specialized frameworks receiving Tier 4 or domain-specific handoffs.

## 5.2 Category problem

“Technology” can include:

- a hand tool;
- a spreadsheet;
- a social platform;
- a medical device;
- a foundation model;
- an irrigation system;
- a voting system;
- a weapon;
- a genomic process;
- a bridge;
- a community radio station;
- a legal database;
- an industrial control system.

These do not form one legal subject. The same technical component can be harmless in one context and catastrophic in another.

## 5.3 Jurisdiction must attach to acts and relationships

Governance should identify:

- who develops, deploys, operates, procures, uses, modifies, or withdraws the technology;
- where the activity occurs;
- who is affected;
- what legal interests are engaged;
- what scale and duration apply;
- whether participation is voluntary or necessary for public life;
- whether the system is reversible;
- what data, labour, environmental, security, and rights dependencies exist.

## 5.4 Required scope rule

The framework should never claim authority merely because an object is technological. It should identify the independent domain and authority that make intervention lawful.

# 6. The Global Technology Council concentrates incompatible functions

## 6.1 Source powers

The GTC and its sub-councils are assigned roles in:

- global standard-setting;
- technology evaluation;
- risk classification;
- moratorium recommendations;
- crisis coordination;
- specialized-framework handoffs;
- lifecycle oversight;
- certification;
- funding;
- community review integration;
- open-source commons governance;
- AI governance;
- cyber coordination;
- emerging-technology anticipation.

The Cybersecurity and Resilience Council also receives veto and certification powers based on “parasitic patterns” and cognitive diversity.

## 6.2 Multi-stakeholder composition does not cure concentration

The proposed percentages may diversify participation, but do not resolve functional conflict. A body can be representative and still combine legislative, regulatory, executive, technical, fiscal, epistemic, and quasi-judicial powers.

## 6.3 Accountability bodies are dependent on the same architecture

Community Tech Review Boards, youth delegates, Earth Council review, public dashboards, and Indigenous audits are all embedded in the same GGF system. They do not necessarily provide independent legal review, standing, remedy, or appeal.

## 6.4 Required separation

v3.6 should separate:

### A. Technology Foresight and Public-Interest Research Network

Non-binding horizon scanning, evidence synthesis, scenario testing, and public-interest research.

### B. Technology Standards Forum

Voluntary, transparent, technology-neutral standards developed with affected parties and domain experts. A standard becomes binding only through a valid legal or contractual act.

### C. Independent Assessment and Accreditation Network

Accredits assessors under published competence and conflict rules. It does not regulate deployment or adjudicate disputes.

### D. Domain regulators and competent authorities

Make binding decisions under their own law and jurisdiction.

### E. Community and affected-party review bodies

Exercise rights of notice, participation, challenge, refusal, and local authorization where applicable. They do not become universal regulators.

### F. Cyber incident coordination function

Coordinates voluntary and lawfully delegated response without acquiring criminal, emergency, or infrastructure command power.

### G. Justice institutions

Determine unlawful conduct, interim measures, remedies, and sanctions under Justice Systems v1.4.

# 7. TRRT classifies technologies rather than governed uses

## 7.1 Source architecture

TRRT assigns technologies to four tiers—local/low risk, regional/medium risk, global/systemic risk, and high-stakes/specialized—with corresponding GTC review, security, audit, handoff, and enforcement consequences.

## 7.2 Inherent-risk error

Risk is not solely a property of a technology. It emerges from an interaction among:

- capability;
- use;
- deployment scale;
- affected population;
- institutional context;
- operator incentives;
- security posture;
- data and resource dependencies;
- reversibility;
- concentration;
- cumulative effects;
- available alternatives;
- response capacity.

A language model used privately for drafting differs from the same model used for benefits eligibility, military targeting, medical diagnosis, or political persuasion. A blockchain used for a public timestamp differs from one used as mandatory civic identity or financial settlement.

## 7.3 One tier can conceal multiple risks

A technology may be low-risk in one dimension and high-risk in another. A community application may be locally small but highly coercive if necessary for housing or welfare. A global open-source library may have broad reach but low direct authority. A medical device may affect few people yet involve irreversible bodily harm.

## 7.4 Required replacement

Replace TRRT with a **Technology Use, Dependency, and Impact Profile** containing separate dimensions rather than one global tier:

- legal and constitutional significance;
- physical and bodily risk;
- rights and dignity risk;
- data and surveillance risk;
- cognitive and behavioural influence;
- ecological and material burden;
- labour and economic power;
- security and dual-use potential;
- concentration and infrastructural dependency;
- scale, duration, reversibility, and uncertainty;
- effects on Indigenous rights and jurisdiction;
- effects on children and other protected groups;
- essential-service dependence;
- cross-boundary externalities;
- available alternatives and exit.

A domain authority may use these dimensions to classify a governed use under its own law. The framework itself should not assign a universal legal tier.

# 8. Risk classification must remain contestable

## 8.1 State estimates, not constitutional facts

Risk assessments are models. They depend on evidence, assumptions, values, timescales, and observation channels. They can be incomplete or strategically manipulated.

## 8.2 Required plural assessment

High-consequence assessments should include:

- the proposed use and alternatives;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- affected-party accounts;
- independent technical analysis;
- labour, disability, ecological, and Indigenous perspectives where relevant;
- dissenting state estimates;
- conflicts of interest;
- missing populations and unobserved effects;
- proposed monitoring and stop conditions;
- challenge and appeal routes.

## 8.3 No automatic re-tiering

Annual or continuous reclassification may be useful as an alert. It should not automatically change legal duties, suspend deployment, or trigger sanctions without an authorized decision.

# 9. Standards, certification, procurement, and law are distinct

## 9.1 Source conflation

v3.5 treats GGF standards, certification, API access, funding, justice enforcement, and trade consequences as an integrated compliance pathway.

## 9.2 Four different instruments

A successor must distinguish:

1. **voluntary standard** — adopted by choice;
2. **private certification** — contractual representation with defined scope;
3. **public procurement condition** — applies to specified purchases under procurement law;
4. **binding regulation** — applies under valid public law and due process.

## 9.3 Certification risks

Certification can become:

- a barrier to entry;
- a market cartel;
- a political loyalty signal;
- a substitute for liability;
- a false assurance of safety;
- a gate to funding, APIs, or public services;
- a way to exclude small or non-Western systems.

## 9.4 Required certification record

Every certification scheme should state:

- source of authority;
- voluntary or compulsory status;
- scope and exclusions;
- assessor independence;
- evidence requirements;
- duration;
- surveillance and data collection;
- correction and appeal;
- treatment of equivalent local standards;
- liability and non-reliance;
- accessibility and cost;
- consequences of refusal or loss.

# 10. Moratoria and circuit breakers require lawful intervention architecture

## 10.1 Source mechanisms

The GTC may recommend moratoria, while an Ethical Circuit Breaker may pause deployments based on indices, social division, mental-health effects, autonomous violations, or cyber incidents.

## 10.2 A pause can be coercive

Pausing a technology may affect:

- workers and livelihoods;
- patients;
- communications;
- transport;
- energy;
- public benefits;
- safety systems;
- contractual rights;
- speech and association;
- critical infrastructure.

Even a temporary pause is not merely technical. It is an exercise of authority.

## 10.3 Required intervention classes

v3.6 should distinguish:

- operator-initiated safety stop;
- contractually pre-authorized pause;
- public regulator interim order;
- judicial injunction;
- infrastructure operator containment;
- community refusal within its own jurisdiction;
- advisory moratorium recommendation;
- legislative prohibition.

Each has a different source, evidence threshold, duration, review, and continuity duty.

## 10.4 Circuit-breaker safeguards

Any compulsory pause requires:

- valid authority;
- defined harm class;
- evidence threshold;
- narrow scope;
- necessity and proportionality;
- essential-service continuity;
- notice where possible;
- reasons and uncertainty;
- maximum duration;
- independent review;
- appeal;
- resumption or safe wind-down criteria;
- compensation rules where lawful.

# 11. Crisis Governance Mode is self-activating and overbroad

## 11.1 Source triggers

The source includes automatic activation after a confirmed cyberattack and other triggers such as a drop in FPIC satisfaction, social division, mental-health crises, or autonomous technology violations.

## 11.2 Metric-to-emergency conversion

A satisfaction index, social conflict indicator, or mental-health trend cannot by itself establish an emergency. Such signals may reflect:

- genuine harm;
- political disagreement;
- measurement error;
- selection bias;
- reporting changes;
- economic stress;
- unrelated events;
- strategic manipulation;
- culturally different response patterns.

## 11.3 Wrong approval actors

GTC supermajority, Earth Council ratification, and Planetary Health Assembly extension do not automatically create lawful emergency authority. The responsible actor depends on the jurisdiction and subject.

## 11.4 Required replacement

Technology emergencies should follow Treaty v1.3, Aurora Accord v1.3, applicable domestic law, infrastructure contracts, and domain-specific protocols. The framework may supply an **Emergency Technology State Estimate** and coordination checklist. It should not declare, extend, or adjudicate the emergency.

# 12. Cybersecurity coordination overlaps Aurora, Shield, and Conduit

## 12.1 Source architecture

The Cybersecurity and Resilience Council oversees security-stack implementation, threat intelligence, community resilience, incident coordination, reviews, vetoes, certification, and cross-framework alignment. The Tech Crisis Response Unit diagnoses, contains, restores, shares forensic intelligence, and hands cases to Shield actors.

## 12.2 Duplication and command ambiguity

The architecture does not clearly identify:

- who owns the affected system;
- who may isolate or shut it down;
- who controls credentials and logs;
- who declares a security incident;
- who preserves evidence;
- who communicates publicly;
- who requests cross-border assistance;
- who investigates crimes;
- who authorizes intrusive measures;
- who restores service;
- who bears liability.

## 12.3 Revised stack boundary

- **Aurora Accord v1.3** governs data, logging, breach notification, identity, evidence custody, and protected non-transfer.
- **Conduit Protocol** should govern infrastructure continuity and service resilience.
- **Shield Protocol** may govern criminal or security investigation only under valid law.
- **Justice Systems v1.4** governs warrants, interim measures, evidence disputes, remedies, and sanctions.
- **Technology Governance** may coordinate technical foresight and interface records, but should not command the incident.

## 12.4 Security outcomes, not one stack

The mandatory GGF Minimum Viable Security Stack has been retired in Aurora v1.3. v3.6 should specify outcomes such as confidentiality, integrity, availability, recoverability, least privilege, segmentation, patching, supply-chain security, accessible fallbacks, and auditability, while permitting equivalent local implementations.

# 13. “Parasitic pattern” detection is not a legally determinate classifier

## 13.1 Source mechanism

The framework proposes AI-assisted detection of scarcity engineering, shame programming, and hierarchical extraction, with veto, circuit-breaker, certification, reward, and automated immune-response consequences.

## 13.2 Legitimate underlying concerns

The source is right to identify:

- artificial lock-in;
- manipulative design;
- surveillance-based extraction;
- exploitative platform economics;
- coercive reputation systems;
- dependency creation;
- value concentration;
- transfer of risk to users and workers.

## 13.3 Category ambiguity

The proposed labels can also capture legitimate arrangements:

- access control needed for safety or privacy;
- scarcity resulting from real capacity limits;
- subscription funding for maintenance;
- differential permissions;
- performance feedback;
- specialized professional hierarchy;
- centralized emergency coordination;
- intellectual property or confidentiality recognized by law;
- risk pricing;
- bounded moderation.

The question is not whether “scarcity,” “hierarchy,” or negative affect exists. It is whether a specific practice violates a defined right, duty, contract, competition rule, labour standard, data rule, safety requirement, or public law.

## 13.4 Required replacement

Replace metaphoric pattern classifiers with legally and operationally defined assessments:

- lock-in and portability;
- switching cost;
- market concentration;
- dark patterns;
- discrimination;
- surveillance;
- wage and value distribution;
- contractual unfairness;
- essential-service dependency;
- algorithmic management;
- unilateral rule change;
- accessibility;
- user control;
- repair and interoperability;
- worker and community governance.

These dimensions may support investigation. They do not automatically authorize an override.

# 14. Automated immune responses are constitutionally unacceptable

## 14.1 Source architecture

Appendix M proposes automated scarcity circuit breakers, worth-validation overrides, and value-distribution rebalancers.

## 14.2 Automatic override problem

These mechanisms would allow software to:

- invalidate access restrictions;
- alter motivational systems;
- change economic distribution;
- override operator choices;
- potentially affect contracts, property, safety, privacy, and public services.

That is automated governance, not mere monitoring.

## 14.3 Required prohibition

v3.6 should prohibit automated systems from independently:

- changing legal rights or obligations;
- redistributing funds or value;
- overriding safety or access controls;
- suspending services;
- declaring a technology unethical;
- determining psychological harm;
- removing content or functionality as a constitutional remedy;
- changing employment, eligibility, credit, or public-service status.

Automated systems may detect patterns, produce alerts, recommend tests, or execute narrowly pre-authorized operator controls under human accountability.

# 15. “System sanity” and “intelligence honor” can become epistemic policing

## 15.1 Source intention

The source seeks to protect sensitive, intelligent, neurodivergent, or critical individuals whose accurate perception of institutional dysfunction may be pathologized or suppressed.

That concern is real and worth preserving.

## 15.2 Institutional danger

A System Sanity Oversight Panel or algorithm trained to distinguish “accurate system perception” from dysfunction would need to decide:

- what the system is;
- which critique is accurate;
- which distress is evidence of insight;
- which perception is mistaken;
- who qualifies as highly intelligent or sensitive;
- what accommodation is deserved;
- when clinical, political, spiritual, or social interpretations apply.

No technology regulator should hold that authority.

## 15.3 Symmetric risk

The mechanism could be captured by either side:

- institutions could label criticism irrational;
- critics could claim epistemic immunity from evidence or accountability;
- platforms could privilege approved ideologies;
- clinicians, employers, moderators, or public bodies could use certification to validate disputed judgments.

## 15.4 Required replacement

Protect concrete rights instead:

- freedom of expression and dissent;
- non-discrimination based on disability or neurotype;
- accessibility and reasonable accommodation;
- due process before adverse automated decisions;
- explanation and appeal;
- protection from psychiatric inference without lawful basis;
- user control over personalization;
- right to opt out of manipulative ranking;
- protection from retaliation for system criticism;
- plural interface modes and reduced-stimulation options.

No authority should certify a person's perception as sane, insightful, pathological, or socially valid through this framework.

# 16. Cognitive sovereignty needs rights, not mandatory viewpoint engineering

## 16.1 Source proposals

The framework proposes interfaces that automatically present alternative viewpoints, disclose limitations, validate system perception, and meet cognitive diversity standards.

## 16.2 Alternative viewpoints are not neutral

An institution must decide:

- which alternatives count;
- how many are shown;
- how they are ranked;
- whether fringe or harmful claims are included;
- which sources are credible;
- whether the intervention aids understanding or manipulates attention.

## 16.3 Required cognitive-rights architecture

A successor should protect:

- notice of persuasive or adaptive design;
- control over personalization;
- meaningful chronological or non-ranked alternatives where feasible;
- explanation of recommendation logic;
- time and attention controls;
- accessibility;
- age-appropriate safeguards;
- non-retaliatory refusal;
- independent appeal of consequential algorithmic decisions;
- prohibition of covert psychological targeting in defined contexts;
- research access under privacy safeguards.

The framework may recommend viewpoint diversity and disclosure. It should not prescribe one official cognitive environment.

# 17. Digital well-being standards must not administer acceptable psychology

## 17.1 Source goal

The source seeks auditable non-addictive, accessible, socially cohesive technology.

## 17.2 Measurement difficulty

“Addiction,” “well-being,” and “social cohesion” cannot be reduced to one product score. High engagement can reflect value, necessity, compulsion, distress, work, caregiving, community, or entertainment. Low use can reflect healthy autonomy or exclusion.

## 17.3 Required assessment dimensions

Digital well-being assessment should distinguish:

- manipulative interface patterns;
- sleep and interruption controls;
- age and developmental safeguards;
- default notification intensity;
- user-set limits;
- compulsive reward design;
- targeted persuasion;
- harassment and safety;
- accessibility;
- labour expectations;
- essential-service dependency;
- evidence of harm;
- user heterogeneity;
- viable offline or low-stimulation alternatives.

Certification should not claim that a technology is universally “healthy” or socially cohesive.

# 18. AI governance must be use-specific and functionally separated

## 18.1 Source scope

v3.5 positions itself as the primary AI-governance framework and contains algorithmic justice, cognitive sovereignty, commons access, local adaptation, training data, model certification, and pattern-detection protocols.

## 18.2 “AI” is not one legal domain

AI may be used in:

- medicine;
- employment;
- education;
- policing;
- welfare;
- finance;
- media;
- infrastructure;
- science;
- military systems;
- private creative work.

The domain determines much of the applicable law and evidence.

## 18.3 Cross-cutting functions that belong here

Technology Governance can provide shared interfaces for:

- capability and use description;
- model and system documentation;
- incident taxonomies;
- human oversight and contestability;
- dependency and concentration assessment;
- general-purpose model handoffs;
- evaluation provenance;
- cross-domain risk coordination;
- public-interest research;
- open and reproducible testing.

## 18.4 Functions that belong elsewhere

- data rights and training provenance: Aurora Accord;
- employment decisions: Work in Liberation and labour law;
- medical use: health and medical regulation;
- justice use: Justice Systems;
- security and weapons: Shield and Aegis under separately constituted law;
- education: Educational Systems;
- cognitive or mental-health claims: relevant rights and health frameworks;
- digital sentience: Oracle Protocol only as a research and legal-status interface, not automatic status.

# 19. AI Commons and stewardship trusts require lawful acquisition

## 19.1 Source architecture

The AI Commons and Access Protocol proposes pathways for foundational models to be transitioned into Stewardship Trusts and governed as public goods.

## 19.2 Missing authority

A transition may occur through:

- voluntary donation;
- purchase;
- public investment terms;
- insolvency or restructuring law;
- antitrust remedy;
- licence condition;
- compulsory acquisition with lawful authority and compensation;
- commons development from the outset.

The framework cannot simply designate private models as commons.

## 19.3 Required model

v3.6 may propose optional stewardship forms and public-interest procurement. It should require an **AI Asset and Stewardship Authority Record** identifying ownership, licences, contributors, data obligations, security, compute, maintenance, liability, exit, and succession.

# 20. Capital-efficient AI development is useful but constitutionally mixed

## 20.1 Strong contribution

The portfolio approach—open-weight backbones, continued pre-training, adapters, retrieval, and regional capacity—is a practical alternative to assuming every region must train frontier models.

## 20.2 Risks

The source under-specifies:

- licence compatibility and downstream restrictions;
- security and model-supply-chain risk;
- local data consent and protected knowledge;
- labour and annotation conditions;
- evaluation for local languages and low-resource contexts;
- liability for specialist adapters;
- model update and dependency risk;
- compute and energy burden;
- withdrawal and deletion from local training corpora;
- proprietary API dependence;
- the distinction between open-weight and open-source.

## 20.3 Named models should not be constitutional dependencies

References to specific model families are implementation examples, not durable framework commitments.

## 20.4 RAG and “authoritative” knowledge

Retrieval from curated knowledge bases can improve grounding but also centralize epistemic authority. The source should preserve source provenance, contestability, uncertainty, Indigenous non-transfer, community control, and multiple interpretations rather than assuming a Synoptic verifier produces authoritative truth.

# 21. Indigenous sovereignty is recognized and then proxied

## 21.1 Source strengths

The framework supports technological self-determination, community rejection, local charters, FPIC, and concern for technologies affecting ecosystems and Indigenous knowledge.

## 21.2 Earth Council problem

A universal Earth Council is given veto, ratification, audit, advisory, and mediation roles. The revised Indigenous framework rejects the creation of one GGF body that speaks for Indigenous peoples globally.

## 21.3 Affected-nation standard

Technology affecting Indigenous territory, rights, law, data, knowledge, sacred relations, or community infrastructure must identify the affected people and the authority or process they recognize. A global proxy cannot consent for them.

## 21.4 Digital FPIC portal risk

A portal may support communication but cannot define consent, identity, quorum, legitimacy, or representation. Digital tools must be optional and community controlled.

## 21.5 Technology documentation incentives

Rewarding “Indigenous tech documentation” with Hearts risks incentivizing extraction, disclosure, commodification, or outsiders' documentation of protected knowledge. Funding should support Indigenous-chosen infrastructure and preservation without requiring disclosure or GGF recognition.

# 22. FPIC satisfaction is not a governance trigger

## 22.1 Source mechanism

A decline greater than twenty percent in an FPIC Satisfaction Index can trigger the Ethical Circuit Breaker or Crisis Governance Mode.

## 22.2 Measurement problem

Consent is decision-specific and authority-specific. Satisfaction is an evaluative perception. Neither can be converted into one universal index.

## 22.3 Required records

Where Indigenous rights are affected, use an **Indigenous Technology Interface Record** specifying:

- affected people or peoples;
- identified authority and legal basis;
- proposed technology and use;
- territory, data, knowledge, and ecological effects;
- consent, refusal, conditions, or unresolved status;
- language and process;
- protected non-transfer;
- benefit, burden, and remedy;
- monitoring and withdrawal;
- no proxy authority.

A breakdown may trigger notice and review. It does not automatically authorize a global pause or substitute for the affected nation's decision.

# 23. Lifecycle assessment needs material specificity and authority boundaries

## 23.1 Source strength

Lifecycle assessment is a necessary cross-domain interface.

## 23.2 Current vagueness

The source lists carbon, e-waste, resource extraction, biomimicry, circularity, and an undefined “hakuna” term. It does not establish method, boundary, comparison case, uncertainty, burden allocation, or legal consequence.

## 23.3 Required lifecycle profile

A **Technology Lifecycle and Dependency Profile** should include:

- materials and extraction origin;
- labour and community conditions;
- water and energy use;
- land and biodiversity effects;
- manufacturing and transport;
- compute and network burden;
- repairability and interoperability;
- maintenance and update horizon;
- security support duration;
- accessibility;
- disposal, reuse, and e-waste;
- supply-chain concentration;
- rebound effects;
- uncertainty and missing data;
- comparison with realistic alternatives.

## 23.4 No automatic planetary-boundary prohibition

Ecological indicators may inform lawfully constituted standards, permits, procurement, or remedies. A technology council cannot convert modelled boundary exceedance directly into universal prohibition.

# 24. Open-source software commons should be federated

## 24.1 Source contribution

The framework rightly identifies shared software as critical infrastructure and maintenance as care work.

## 24.2 Central repository risk

A GGF-maintained global repository can become:

- a single technical chokepoint;
- a canonical implementation gate;
- a security concentration;
- a trademark and governance centre;
- an implicit certification mechanism;
- a dependency for public institutions.

## 24.3 Required federation

The successor should support a federated commons with:

- multiple repositories and mirrors;
- transparent maintainership;
- reproducible builds;
- security policies;
- dependency provenance;
- accessibility;
- succession and archival rules;
- contributor rights;
- licence compatibility;
- procurement neutrality;
- independent forks;
- community exit;
- funded maintenance without contribution scoring.

## 24.4 Mandatory licensing limits

Publicly funded software may lawfully carry open-source conditions where specified in funding or procurement instruments. The framework itself cannot impose one licence on all GGF-related code without identifying ownership and authority.

## 24.5 Love Ledger and Hearts conflict

Contribution tracking and reward should not depend on Love Ledger reputation or automatic Hearts issuance. Compensation belongs under Work in Liberation, AUBI, contracts, grants, and Financial Systems.

# 25. Aurora Accord boundaries must be updated

# 26. Justice handoffs and sanctions conflict with Justice Systems v1.4

## 26.1 Source sanctions

The framework proposes public disclosure, fines, exclusion from Hearts-based funds, API revocation, interoperability exclusion, trade bans, criminal handoffs, asset effects, and Tribunal adjudication.

## 26.2 Framework rules are not offences

A violation of a GGF protocol is not automatically a legal violation. Binding duties must be adopted through valid law or contract.

## 26.3 Access and material-security sanctions

Excluding actors from essential interoperability, justice, AUBI-linked resources, public infrastructure, or basic services may harm workers, users, communities, and innocent dependants.

## 26.4 Required justice pathway

Any compulsory measure requires:

- source of law;
- jurisdiction;
- standing;
- notice;
- evidence;
- defence and response;
- independent decision;
- proportionality;
- continuity protections;
- appeal;
- remedy and correction.

## 26.5 Circuit breaker versus injunction

Where urgent harm requires a compulsory pause, use a lawfully authorized regulator or court under Justice Systems v1.4. Technology Governance may provide technical evidence and impact analysis but should not decide the order.

# 27. Economic incentives conflict with AUBI and Financial Systems

## 27.1 Source mechanisms

v3.5 links certification and preferred design patterns to Hearts, Leaves, Regenerative Multipliers, fund access, maintenance compensation, cognitive liberty rewards, and anti-parasitic premiums.

## 27.2 Political and epistemic conditionality

These incentives reward compliance with contestable classifications. They could make material advantage depend on whether a technology is judged sufficiently regenerative, sane, cognitively affirming, or anti-extractive by a GGF body.

## 27.3 AUBI boundary

AUBI Layer 1 cannot depend on technology certification, contribution, repository work, refusal networks, or political alignment. Layer 2 recognition must remain governed by AUBI and Work in Liberation with due process and no global moral score.

## 27.4 Financial authority

Funds, taxes, currencies, multipliers, and awards require Financial Systems authority and a lawful budget or contract. The Technology framework may propose funding criteria but may not issue currency or create fiscal obligations.

## 27.5 Required public-investment model

Use transparent grants, procurement, prizes, public-interest investment, cooperative finance, or contractual maintenance funding with:

- lawful source;
- published criteria;
- conflicts disclosure;
- appeal;
- no universal reputation scoring;
- no basic-rights conditionality;
- outcome and learning review;
- no automatic reward from certification metrics.

# 28. Public dashboards do not create accountability by themselves

## 28.1 Source architecture

The Public Trust Dashboard receives decision logs, cyber reports, metrics, incident details, certification, and evaluation data.

## 28.2 Transparency risks

Publication can expose:

- security vulnerabilities;
- personal data;
- whistleblowers;
- Indigenous knowledge;
- trade secrets;
- legal strategy;
- unverified allegations;
- children and vulnerable populations;
- incident-response details that aid attackers.

## 28.3 Required layered transparency

Distinguish:

- public summary;
- affected-party record;
- regulator or auditor record;
- confidential technical record;
- protected Indigenous or community record;
- sealed justice record;
- security-sensitive record.

## 28.4 Dashboard metrics are observations

Adoption, trust, FPIC satisfaction, response time, social cohesion, breach counts, and user sentiment may support evaluation. They do not prove legitimacy or authorize intervention.

## 29.1 Unrealistic targets

The source proposes zero percent parasitic-pattern replication, one hundred percent cognitive accommodation, ninety-five percent user agreement that technology honours intelligence, and mandatory flag counts in pilots.

## 29.2 Perverse incentives

Targets can encourage:

- redefining categories;
- excluding difficult users;
- suppressing negative reports;
- selecting friendly pilots;
- forcing a technology to be flagged to satisfy a milestone;
- overstating certainty;
- gaming surveys;
- performative compliance.

## 29.3 Multidimensional evaluation

v3.6 should separately track:

- legal compliance;
- rights and accessibility;
- security;
- user control;
- labour effects;
- ecological burden;
- concentration and dependency;
- incident frequency and severity;
- reversibility;
- community and affected-party experience;
- distribution of benefits and harms;
- uncertainty and missing populations;
- false-positive and false-negative intervention rates;
- pilot exit and non-scaling decisions.

No composite score should authorize certification, crisis, moratorium, or funding.

## 30.2 Constitutional conflict

# 31. Implementation conflicts with Genesis v0.9

## 31.1 Source sequence

The roadmap begins by chartering the GTC and cybersecurity council, then validates TRRT, security-stack deployment, circuit-breaker action, global standards, crisis response, fund capitalization, certification, and Tribunal enforcement.

## 31.2 Authority before ratification

The sequence creates governing institutions and consequential powers before identifying the lawful instrument that authorizes them.

## 31.3 Mandatory success milestones

A pilot is expected to classify all included technologies, flag at least three problematic systems, validate cognitive standards, and progress toward systemic enforcement. This treats the pilot as confirmation rather than falsification.

## 31.4 Required Genesis-aligned sequence

1. voluntary design and research;
2. locally authorized pilots under Pilot Constitutional Contracts;
3. independent evaluation, including non-transferability and failure;
4. formal negotiation of any binding technology protocol;
5. lawful adoption or ratification;
6. institution-specific first seating;
7. separate activation of moratorium, emergency, fiscal, investigative, or enforcement powers;
8. periodic review, narrowing, transfer, or dissolution.

## 31.5 Failure must be constitutional

Pilots must be able to conclude that:

- a classification method does not work;
- a technology should remain locally governed;
- no common standard is justified;
- the proposed institution should not be created;
- a deployment should stop;
- the framework should narrow or dissolve.

# 36. Recommended Technology Governance v3.6 architecture

## Proposed title

**Technology Governance Framework v3.6 — Technology Risk, Public-Interest Standards, Community Self-Determination, and Lawful Intervention**

## Proposed structure

### 1. Purpose, status, and non-authority

State that the framework does not legislate, classify with legal effect, certify compulsorily, impose moratoria, declare emergencies, redistribute value, represent Indigenous peoples, adjudicate, or enforce unless a separately valid instrument confers that power.

### 2. Sources of authority and applicable law

Require Authority Provenance Records for consequential actions.

### 3. Scope and governed-use description

Attach governance to uses, actors, places, dependencies, and harms rather than “technology” as a universal object.

### 4. Technology modalities and decision classes

Distinguish research, development, procurement, deployment, infrastructure operation, public service, essential service, consumer use, professional use, security use, and withdrawal.

### 5. Functional separation

Separate foresight, standards, assessment, accreditation, regulation, emergency coordination, justice, funding, and evaluation.

### 6. Technology Use, Dependency, and Impact Profile

Replace TRRT with multidimensional assessment.

### 7. Risk evidence, state estimates, and uncertainty

Require plural models, conflicts disclosure, dissent, missing populations, and challenge.

### 8. Affected-party standing and participation

Protect users, workers, communities, children, disabled persons, ecological representatives, and other affected parties.

### 9. Community technology self-determination

Support lawful local charters, refusal, adaptation, and non-digitization without universal community veto over others.

### 10. Indigenous technology interfaces

Use affected-nation authority, protected non-transfer, consent, refusal, and non-engagement.

### 11. Accessibility, cognitive liberty, and non-discrimination

Protect concrete rights without system-sanity or intelligence certification.

### 12. Digital well-being and persuasive design

Assess manipulative patterns, attention controls, age safeguards, and user autonomy without universal psychological scoring.

### 13. AI and automated-system governance interfaces

Provide shared documentation, evaluation, incident, concentration, and cross-domain handoff standards.

### 14. Data and identity interface with Aurora Accord

Defer to Aurora v1.3 and prohibit universal identity or telemetry.

### 15. Labour, maintenance, and technological transition

Integrate Work in Liberation, collective bargaining, maintainer governance, AI labour, and transition continuity.

### 16. Lifecycle, ecological, and supply-chain responsibility

Use a complete material profile and competent ecological authorities.

### 17. Interoperability, portability, repair, and anti-lock-in

Specify open interfaces and equivalent local implementations without automatic override of lawful restrictions.

### 18. Standards, certification, and accreditation

Distinguish voluntary, contractual, procurement, and legal instruments.

### 19. Public-interest technology and stewardship forms

Provide optional commons, cooperative, trust, public, and federated models with lawful asset authority.

### 20. Open-source software commons

Use federated repositories, funded maintenance, security, forks, succession, and no reputation-based compensation.

### 21. Public-interest and bioregional AI capacity

Preserve the capital-efficient portfolio with data, labour, licence, security, and evaluation safeguards.

### 22. Cybersecurity and resilience interfaces

Clarify Aurora, Conduit, Shield, system-owner, and justice responsibilities.

### 23. Incident response and technology emergencies

Use lawful declarations, state estimates, narrow coordination, independent review, and continuity.

### 24. Intervention classes and circuit-breaker boundaries

Distinguish operator stops, contract controls, regulator orders, injunctions, advisories, and legislation.

### 25. Justice, remedy, enforcement, and appeal

Defer compulsory action to Justice Systems v1.4 and valid law.

### 26. Funding, procurement, and economic boundaries

Remove Hearts/Leaves certification rewards and define lawful public-investment tools.

### 27. Observation, records, dashboards, and protected non-transfer

Use layered transparency and Aurora lifecycle rules.

### 28. Evaluation, learning, and anti-Goodhart safeguards

No composite score or required adverse finding.

### 29. Pilots, negotiation, adoption, and first seating

Align with Genesis v0.9 and Pilot Constitutional Contracts.

### 30. Amendment, transfer, sunset, and dissolution

Permit narrowing, stable coexistence, local alternatives, and lawful failure.

### 31. Operational records and appendices

Provide reusable templates rather than universal institutional mandates.

# 38. Falsification gates

The successor should not be treated as constitutionally ready unless it passes the following tests.

## Gate 1 — No framework-to-law conversion

A GGF technology principle is not enforceable unless valid law or contract independently adopts it.

## Gate 2 — No universal technology jurisdiction

Calling something technology does not give the framework authority over it.

## Gate 3 — No single legal TRRT tier

Risk remains multidimensional and use-specific.

## Gate 4 — No combined technology sovereign

No body controls standards, classification, certification, crisis, funding, adjudication, and enforcement.

## Gate 5 — No metaphoric moral veto

“Parasitic,” “regenerative,” “sane,” “honouring,” or “extractive” labels do not independently authorize intervention.

## Gate 6 — No automated immune response

Algorithms cannot redistribute value, override access, pause essential systems, or alter rights on their own.

## Gate 7 — No system-sanity authority

No panel or model certifies whether dissent, sensitivity, distress, or system criticism is psychologically or epistemically valid.

## Gate 8 — No official cognitive environment

The framework does not mandate one set of alternative viewpoints, beliefs, or interpretations.

## Gate 9 — No universal Indigenous proxy

Only affected Indigenous peoples' own authorities govern their consent, refusal, territory, knowledge, and technology interface.

## Gate 10 — No FPIC metric emergency

Satisfaction scores do not substitute for consent or trigger automatic crisis power.

## Gate 11 — No self-authorized circuit breaker

# 40. Final assessment

Technology Governance v3.5 sees something important: technology is a means by which institutions quietly decide what people can perceive, refuse, repair, own, access, and become. It is right to resist systems that make digital participation compulsory, pathologize dissent, extract unpaid labour, centralize compute and data, manipulate attention, or externalize ecological costs.

Its central mistake is to answer distributed technological power with a new integrated governance centre. The proposed Global Technology Council, TRRT, circuit breaker, crisis mode, certification system, pattern detectors, funding machinery, cyber council, Earth Council veto, MGCC harmonization, and automated immune responses collectively create a system capable of observing, classifying, rewarding, pausing, and restructuring technology across domains.

That architecture would not merely protect refusal. It could determine which refusals are valid. It would not merely protect cognitive liberty. It could certify which cognition is honoured. It would not merely oppose extraction. It could algorithmically decide what counts as extraction and rebalance value. It would not merely coordinate expertise. It could turn expertise into jurisdiction.

The revision should preserve the framework's ethical sensitivity while accepting a harder constitutional truth:

- there is no single authority over technology as such;
- risk is use-specific and contested;
- standards do not become law by being wise;
- metrics do not become emergencies by crossing a threshold;
- communities can refuse within their rights and jurisdiction without governing everyone else;
- Indigenous peoples govern their own technology relationships without a global proxy;
- automated detection cannot make moral, psychological, legal, or distributive judgments;
- compulsory intervention requires valid authority and due process;
- public-interest technology can be funded and stewarded without making GGF certification the gateway to material life;
- some technologies and standards should remain plural, local, incompatible, or undeployed.

The governing principle for v3.6 should be:

> **Technology Governance helps competent authorities and affected communities perceive technological risk, compare options, preserve refusal, and coordinate lawful action. It does not turn its own model of technological sanity into authority over technology or society.**

The intervention principle should be:

> **An assessment may justify attention, testing, negotiation, or a recommendation. It does not by itself justify a moratorium, shutdown, sanction, redistribution, emergency, or compulsory redesign.**

The cognitive-liberty principle should be:

> **Protect people from technological pathologization, manipulation, discrimination, and compulsory participation without authorizing any institution to certify the truth, sanity, intelligence, or developmental worth of their perceptions.**

The final readiness test should be:

> **Could a community reject the framework's preferred technology, a developer successfully challenge its risk assessment, an Indigenous people refuse all GGF interfaces, a regulator decline the framework's recommendation, a pilot falsify the theory, and the system still respond lawfully without escalating to certification exclusion, emergency, moral diagnosis, or automated override?**

Where the answer is yes, Technology Governance is becoming a legitimate cross-domain interface.

Where the answer is no, it remains an ethical operating system with regulatory powers.
