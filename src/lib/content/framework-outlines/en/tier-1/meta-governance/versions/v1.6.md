# Layer 1 — Meta-Governance Lite

## What is Meta-Governance?

Meta-Governance is the **coordination of governance systems**.

Health, climate, finance, migration, technology, peace, work, Indigenous sovereignty, and ecological stewardship each have their own institutions, knowledge, rights, and responsibilities. Their decisions nevertheless affect one another. Meta-Governance provides the interfaces through which those systems can:

- recognize interdependence;
- exchange bounded information;
- identify jurisdictional overlap;
- convene the authorities that already possess legitimate standing;
- compare interpretations without forcing premature consensus;
- coordinate shared action where authority has been delegated;
- learn from failure;
- support experiments;
- return or dissolve temporary coordination structures when their work ends.

Meta-Governance is **not a world government above other governments**. It does not gain power merely because a problem crosses domains or because its observers believe another institution is failing.

## The jazz ensemble analogy—revised

A jazz ensemble coordinates without every musician becoming the conductor.

- Each musician retains a distinct instrument, skill, and responsibility.
- Shared tempo and listening make joint action possible.
- A facilitator may help the ensemble hear conflicts or transitions.
- The facilitator does not own the music or acquire the instruments.
- A musician may refuse a cue that violates the agreed score or the conditions of participation.
- When the piece ends, the temporary coordination arrangement ends with it.

The analogy has limits. Governance includes rights, coercive power, unequal resources, contested jurisdiction, and people who did not choose the performance. Meta-Governance must therefore operate through constitutional authority, not harmony language alone.

## Seven quick rules

1. **Coordinate only what is genuinely interdependent.**
2. **Use the minimum authority, information, and documentation necessary.**
3. **Keep observation, interpretation, authorization, implementation, and adjudication distinct.**
4. **Treat indicators as alerts, not judgments.**
5. **Assign responsibility according to causal control, lawful jurisdiction, practical capacity, and resources.**
6. **Make acceptance, refusal, delay, incapacity, and constitutional absence visible without transferring authority by silence.**
7. **Return authority and dissolve temporary structures when the reason for them ends.**

## What Meta-Governance may do

Meta-Governance may:

- map interfaces and dependencies;
- convene affected authorities;
- facilitate negotiation and mediation;
- maintain constitutional and operational interface records;
- support plural modelling and foresight;
- identify possible blind spots;
- host bounded pilots where lawfully authorized;
- coordinate voluntary shared services;
- publish non-binding audits and dissent;
- monitor whether coordination arrangements remain accountable, timely, and causally adequate;
- record response, refusal, delay, capacity limitation, and constitutional gaps through the controlling interface;
- prepare options for authorized decision-makers;
- identify when coordination should be avoided, simplified, devolved, federated, or ended.

## What Meta-Governance may not do by itself

Meta-Governance may not, solely through its own framework or diagnosis:

- legislate;
- tax;
- impose sanctions;
- command police, military, emergency, or work forces;
- determine criminal or civil liability;
- suspend rights;
- invalidate a currency or economic charter;
- override Indigenous sovereignty or consent;
- acquire another institution’s jurisdiction;
- dissolve another institution;
- force a pilot to reintegrate;
- turn a dashboard threshold into a constitutional judgment;
- treat another institution's silence, refusal, incapacity, or missed deadline as consent or as an automatic transfer of jurisdiction;
- certify its own coordination model as legitimate merely because it conforms to GGF records.

Those actions require authority from the Treaty, a valid compact, an Indigenous authority, a democratic territorial body, a tribunal, or another institution that legitimately holds the relevant power.

## How to participate

- **Explore:** inspect maps, records, disagreements, and public reasons.
- **Contribute:** provide evidence, lived experience, technical analysis, or facilitation.
- **Co-design:** participate in a time-limited, authorized interface or pilot.
- **Challenge:** contest assumptions, methods, standing, or authority.
- **Refuse:** decline participation where the process violates rights, consent, or Accountable Coordination.

Participation status is not a hierarchy of human worth. Refusal, withdrawal, and non-participation do not imply ignorance, obstruction, or lack of solidarity.

# Layer 2 — Constitutional and Operational Architecture

# 1. Purpose, status, and constitutional position

## 1.1 Purpose

This framework governs the design and operation of **coordination interfaces among institutions that retain their own jurisdiction**.

Its purposes are to:

- reduce destructive conflict among legitimate authorities;
- enable joint action on cross-domain and cross-scale conditions;
- preserve local, professional, ecological, Indigenous, and democratic knowledge;
- expose blind spots without claiming total visibility;
- improve institutional learning without rewarding surveillance;
- enable experimentation without manufacturing permanent shadow sovereignty;
- support lawful succession and graceful dissolution;
- prevent coordination bodies from converting interdependence into self-expanding authority.

## 1.2 Constitutional subordination

This framework does not create independent constitutional authority.

Meta-Governance institutions operate only through:

1. authority expressly conferred by the Treaty for Our Only Home;
2. a lawful compact among participating authorities;
3. an Indigenous authority’s specific and continuing consent;
4. a domain framework within its valid competence;
5. a tribunal order or lawful public mandate;
6. voluntary participation where no coercive power is exercised.

Where this framework conflicts with the Treaty or the Constitutional Interface Specification, those documents govern. GMEAIA governs the operational semantics of authority effects, consequence profiles, handoffs, response states, clocks, record custody, and closure used by this framework.

## 1.3 Connector rule

Meta-Governance is a **connector, not a residual sovereign**.

A matter does not become a Meta-Governance competence merely because:

- several domains are involved;
- the problem is complex;
- the existing institutions disagree;
- a coordination council has better data;
- an audit concludes that another institution is slow, captured, or unobservable;
- the coordination body can imagine a more coherent solution.

The institution holding lawful competence retains jurisdiction unless authority is validly delegated, transferred, or jointly constituted through the procedures governing that competence.

Retained jurisdiction does not imply unlimited practical capacity. A consequential referral shall identify whether a response duty exists, its source and effect, and the applicable acknowledgment and decision clocks. Silence, refusal, timeout, or incapacity does not transfer authority by implication.

## 1.4 Normative language

- **Must** and **shall** identify requirements for conformance with this framework.
- **May** identifies a permitted option.
- **Should** identifies a strong design recommendation that may require contextual adaptation.
- **Proposal** identifies a mechanism without presently constituted authority.

## 1.5 Scope boundaries

This framework does not define:

- the substantive content of human rights or criminal law;
- Indigenous governance institutions or representatives;
- Hearts or Leaves monetary operations;
- AUBI eligibility and benefit rules;
- professional and labour standards;
- ecological thresholds;
- military or police doctrine;
- the validity of scientific, spiritual, cultural, or Traditional Knowledge claims.

It governs how authorized institutions coordinate around those subjects.

# 2. Core principles

## 2.1 Accountable Coordination

Every coordination mechanism shall be tested against four questions:

1. **Is its authority revocable and externally grounded, or self-justifying?**
2. **Is transparency meaningfully symmetric, or does the institution observe others while remaining opaque?**
3. **Are exit and challenge practically available, or prohibitively costly?**
4. **Is the mechanism self-limiting, or does institutional survival become its primary function?**

Failure of the test is an alert requiring review. It does not automatically transfer authority or dissolve the mechanism.

## 2.2 Polycentric coordination

Coordination should connect multiple centers of authority without collapsing them into one chain of command.

Relevant centers may include:

- territorial democratic authorities;
- cross-boundary Commons Trusts;
- Indigenous nations and authorities;
- labour, guild, professional, and provider institutions;
- Treaty institutions;
- tribunals;
- scientific and knowledge bodies;
- youth and future-generations institutions;
- civil society and affected-community bodies.

## 2.3 Causal subsidiarity and capability

Decisions remain at the lowest effective scale consistent with rights, shared-commons obligations, and material cross-boundary effects. Responsibility allocation shall also examine:

- control over principal causal variables;
- lawful jurisdiction;
- practical capacity;
- resources available to discharge the duty;
- upstream and cross-boundary effects.

The place where consequences appear does not establish where responsibility belongs. A body is not capable merely because it can administer or absorb consequences generated elsewhere.

A lower-capacity institution should receive assistance before losing authority. Staffing shortages, technical limitations, or unequal resources are not by themselves proof that centralization is necessary.

Where a condition exists only through relations among authorities—such as a watershed, grid, settlement network, labour market, transport corridor, or shared infrastructure—authority may be jointly constituted rather than described as a local power transferred upward.

## 2.4 Bounded coordination

No coordination body, dashboard, secretariat, council, or AI system shall be designed to obtain full visibility or control over the entire governance system.

Every consequential representation shall disclose:

- uncertainty;
- known blind spots;
- missing populations;
- source dependencies;
- competing interpretations;
- conditions under which the representation would be revised.

## 2.5 Dynamic interoperability

Institutions should be able to cooperate without adopting one ontology, technology, administrative culture, or value metric.

Interoperability may include recognition, authentication, bounded attestation, settlement, consultation, translation, or coordinated action. Permission for one does not imply permission for all.

## 2.6 Sensemaking sovereignty

Affected people and institutions retain standing to describe conditions in their own terms, submit counter-models, identify translation loss, and withhold knowledge where no overriding lawful requirement exists.

A global or technical model does not become authoritative merely because it is standardized, quantified, or computationally sophisticated.

## 2.7 Epistemic plurality

Consequential coordination should use multiple genuinely independent observation and modelling channels. Their disagreement shall be surfaced rather than averaged away when the disagreement is decision-relevant.

Plurality is not satisfied by several models trained on the same data, managed by the same institution, or rewarded by the same objective.

## 2.8 Cognitive equity and universal access

Coordination processes shall support synchronous, asynchronous, visual, oral, narrative, embodied, supported, and low-technology participation where relevant.

Accessibility is not an optional accommodation added after institutional design. It is part of whether authority is legitimate and whether the system can observe its own effects.

## 2.9 Human authority and bounded automation

AI and automated systems may support analysis, translation, anomaly detection, simulation, and administrative work. They may not independently make ethical, constitutional, jurisdictional, identity, consent, sanction, or dissolution judgments.

## 2.10 Planetary stewardship and rights

Coordination shall respect the Treaty’s rights, commons, ecological, and intergenerational obligations. Meta-Governance does not define those obligations but must not design interfaces that make them unenforceable.

## 2.11 Economic sufficiency

Coordination infrastructure should serve human and ecological flourishing rather than institutional expansion, transaction volume, or indefinite economic growth.

## 2.12 Peace and regeneration

Mediation, prevention, restorative processes, and nonviolent resolution are preferred where consistent with rights and safety. Coordination must not use harmony language to suppress legitimate conflict, resistance, or accountability.

## 2.13 Learning without surveillance

Institutional learning shall be judged by improved detection, correction, adaptation, and outcomes—not by sensor count, data volume, or the breadth of centralized visibility.

## 2.14 Lawful experimentation

Experiments shall be bounded by purpose, authority, rights, duration, evidence, challenge, and exit. Successful experiments earn consideration, not automatic jurisdiction.

## 2.15 Duty attached to retained jurisdiction

A Meta-Governance process shall not manufacture a response duty. It shall disclose whether a duty exists through law, treaty, charter, compact, contract, internal rule, or voluntary commitment.

Where such a duty exists, the responsible institution should acknowledge a properly routed consequential request and accept, refuse, defer, refer, or identify a capacity limitation within a decision-appropriate period. Reasons, conflicts, expected harm during delay, provisional protections, and escalation routes shall remain visible.

## 2.16 Proportional procedural burden

Record depth shall be proportional to reversibility, rights and service effects, coercion, public-law effect, exposure of non-participants, ecological irreversibility, data sensitivity, duration, and scale.

Existing valid records shall be reused. Low-risk voluntary coordination shall not require dissolution-grade process. High-consequence coordination shall not evade safeguards because its method is technically simple.

The institution proposing coordination bears the primary burden of showing necessity, preparing accessible documentation, and supporting weaker participants’ independent review.

## 2.17 Conformance is not success

Conformance shows that authority, process, rights, and lifecycle requirements were represented. It does not establish legitimacy, causal effectiveness, justice, trust, ecological improvement, or the success of Meta-Governance itself.

## 2.18 Liberatory impermanence

Coordination institutions are provisional concentrations of capacity. Their continuation must remain justified. Dissolution, federation, devolution, and protocol transfer are legitimate outcomes when the originating need changes or ends.

# 3. Coordination actors

## 3.1 Meta-Governance Coordination Councils

A Meta-Governance Coordination Council is a convening and interface body created by the Treaty, a compact, or participating authorities.

Its charter must identify:

- the condition or interface it coordinates;
- participating institutions;
- affected non-participants;
- authority formation: retained, delegated, transferred, concurrent, or jointly constituted;
- delegated powers and prohibited powers;
- causal variables and responsible controllers where material;
- applicable response duties and clocks;
- consequence profile under GMEAIA;
- duration;
- funding;
- decision rules;
- challenge and appeal;
- review, devolution, and closure conditions.

A council does not possess a general power to resolve every issue arising within its field.

## 3.2 Interface Secretariats

An Interface Secretariat may maintain records, schedule deliberation, support translation, publish decisions, track commitments, and administer shared services.

It may not determine jurisdictional disputes, treat record completion as substantive success, or expand its mandate through administrative interpretation. Translation, documentation, and routine interface costs should normally be borne by the coordination arrangement rather than shifted onto lower-capacity participants.

## 3.3 Domain Interface Panels

A Domain Interface Panel brings together authorities from distinct domains when one decision materially affects another.

Examples include:

- health and migration;
- finance and AUBI;
- technology and Indigenous knowledge;
- work and crisis response;
- climate and food systems;
- territorial planning and cross-boundary commons.

Panels prepare options and interface records. They do not replace the institutions that authorize action.

## 3.4 Global foresight function

The Global Council for the Future under Treaty supplies the principal Treaty-level foresight function. Meta-Governance may support a distributed network of foresight labs, knowledge institutions, Indigenous-authorized observers, civil-society analysts, and domain councils.

The network shall not be collapsed into a single intelligence authority with privileged access to every domain.

## 3.5 Citizen and affected-community panels

Sortition panels, citizens’ assemblies, and affected-community bodies may:

- identify lived impacts;
- review contested assumptions;
- participate in mediation;
- recommend action;
- exercise binding power where a valid constitution or compact expressly grants it.

Random selection does not make a body un-capturable, omnicompetent, or entitled to dissolve other institutions.

## 3.6 Office of the Adversary interface

The Office of the Adversary may inspect coordination claims, challenge assumptions, commission red teams, publish dissent, and identify suppressed alternatives.

It does not adjudicate its own objections or direct remedial action.

## 3.7 Indigenous authorities

Meta-Governance recognizes Indigenous authorities identified through Indigenous peoples’ own processes. It shall not create a universal Indigenous council, impose a representation quota, certify traditional authority, or appoint proxies where no authorized Indigenous body has consented.

## 3.8 System Canaries

A System Canary is a person or group whose experience may reveal contradictions, cognitive overload, moral injury, exclusion, or institutional unreality before conventional indicators respond.

Canary testimony is evidence deserving protection and investigation. It is not automatically conclusive, and it must not be medicalized, romanticized, or used to place an unpaid diagnostic burden on vulnerable people.

# 4. Functional polycentrism and interface topology

## 4.1 Horizontal dimensions

A coordination arrangement should identify at least four possible authority dimensions:

- **territorial authority** — place, infrastructure, services, and democratic jurisdiction;
- **commons authority** — ecological systems and resources crossing territorial boundaries;
- **practice authority** — work, professions, standards, and provider governance;
- **Indigenous sovereignty** — inherent authority over peoples, territories, knowledge, and relations as determined by Indigenous institutions.

These dimensions may overlap without forming a fixed universal chamber system.

## 4.2 Vertical dimensions

Coordination may also occur among neighborhood, municipal or BAZ, bioregional, state, regional, Treaty, and intergenerational institutions.

Scale does not itself determine superiority. A global institution may have wider jurisdiction over a shared condition but poorer local knowledge. A local institution may possess detailed knowledge but be unable to contain an externality.

## 4.3 No unilateral cross-domain action

An institution shall not use competence in one domain to determine another domain’s decision without valid authorization.

For example:

- a Treasury may process settlement but not decide the social worth of work;
- a labour body may set professional standards but not control a watershed;
- a Commons Trust may enjoin ecological harm but not redesign a community’s entire economy;
- a Meta-Governance council may identify interface failure but not acquire the affected institutions’ powers.

## 4.4 Polycentric Interface Record

A consequential cross-domain arrangement shall publish a Polycentric Interface Record stating:

1. the condition requiring coordination;
2. participating authorities and their standing;
3. affected non-participants;
4. decision class under Treaty;
5. authority retained by each institution;
6. authority delegated;
7. information exchanged and withheld;
8. disagreement and adjudication procedure;
9. protected rights and minimums;
10. exit, amendment, restoration, and closure conditions.

## 4.5 Fractal adaptation

Small communities need not reproduce the full institutional form of a global system. Functions may be performed through distinct roles within one assembly if conflicts of interest remain visible and consequential decisions receive independent review.

Fractal design preserves constitutional functions, not bureaucracy for its own sake.

## 4.6 Jointly constituted authority and constitutional gaps

Where the governance object exists only through relations among several jurisdictions, a Polycentric Interface Record shall state whether authority is concurrent or jointly constituted and identify the constituting source, parties, shared object, affected non-parties, exit conditions, and continuing duties.

Where no identified authority possesses sufficient lawful jurisdiction, Meta-Governance may publish and route a Constitutional Gap Notice through GMEAIA-08 or GMEAIA-59, as applicable. The notice does not authorize the coordination body to fill the gap. It routes the matter toward lawful compact, legislation, treaty amendment, adjudication, or another constituting process.

# 5. The coordination lifecycle

## 5.1 Trigger and consequence profile

Coordination may be initiated by:

- a participating authority;
- an affected community;
- a Commons Trust;
- an Indigenous authority;
- a Treaty body;
- a tribunal;
- a worker or professional institution;
- an authorized sentinel or audit;
- a valid public petition.

A trigger opens inquiry. It does not establish the diagnosis or the coordinating body’s authority.

The convenor shall select and justify the applicable GMEAIA consequence profile. Low-risk exploratory coordination may use a compact core record. Consequential or protected-domain coordination requires the additional authority, rights, monitoring, and closure functions triggered by its effects.

## 5.2 Standing, jurisdiction, and causal responsibility

Before substantive coordination begins, the convenor shall identify:

- who claims standing;
- who is materially affected;
- which institutions hold legal and practical control over the principal causal variables;
- which institutions hold competence;
- whether any Indigenous nation or protected knowledge is implicated;
- whether the matter can be handled through support or voluntary coordination;
- whether responsibility is being assigned to an actor that can only absorb consequences;
- whether authority is retained, delegated, transferred, concurrent, jointly constituted, or absent;
- who may challenge the classification.

For consequential coordination, the process shall complete or reference the GMEAIA-20 Causal Responsibility Profile. An affected party’s standing creates participation, evidence, consent where applicable, challenge, and remedy rights; it does not itself assign responsibility.

## 5.3 Shared problem formulation

Participants shall document:

- the condition to be addressed;
- competing descriptions;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- causal variables and uncontrolled dependencies;
- boundaries of the inquiry;
- excluded or unseen populations;
- the condition’s relevant timescale;
- what would count as improvement or failure;
- what the proposed coordination cannot establish.

The formulation may remain plural. Coordination does not require one agreed theory of the whole problem.

## 5.4 Response-time and handoff design

Before a consequential referral or handoff, the process shall identify:

- the candidate recipient and basis for competence;
- whether a response duty exists and its source and effect;
- the legal, charter, compact, or GMEAIA-07 basis for acknowledgment and decision clocks;
- expected harm during delay;
- any pre-authorized safe state;
- sender and recipient duties;
- escalation and custody if the recipient refuses, defers, times out, or identifies a capacity limitation.

Exact response states and lifecycle semantics are governed by GMEAIA. Silence does not create acceptance, correctness, or authority transfer.

## 5.5 Option development

Options should distinguish:

- local adaptation;
- capacity support;
- mutual aid;
- shared service;
- voluntary compact;
- jointly constituted authority;
- interface change;
- temporary pilot;
- legal or Treaty action;
- non-action;
- continued disagreement;
- a Constitutional Gap Notice.

## 5.6 Authorization and acceptance

The institution holding the relevant competence authorizes action. Where several competences are involved, each affected authority authorizes the part within its domain unless a valid joint procedure exists.

A recipient’s acceptance of a handoff establishes custody only to the extent recorded. It does not erase sender duties, unresolved dependencies, or the need for separate operational activation.

## 5.7 Implementation

Implementation responsibilities, resources, safety, reporting, action clocks, and termination shall be explicit. Meta-Governance may coordinate implementation only to the extent delegated.

A capacity-limited authority should identify the support, shared service, temporary delegation, or lawful redesign needed to discharge its duty. Incapacity is not a constitutional gap and does not by itself justify permanent centralization.

## 5.8 Evaluation and revision

Evaluation shall include:

- independent outcomes;
- subgroup and rights effects;
- coordination-added latency;
- administrative and translation burden;
- response and performance omissions;
- common-mode dependencies;
- unintended consequences;
- whether the action altered the causal condition;
- whether the same result could be obtained through less coordination;
- evidence that would falsify the intervention’s theory.

## 5.9 Closure, devolution, and unresolved duties

The arrangement closes, narrows, devolves, or changes form when:

- the condition ends;
- the delegated task is complete;
- participants withdraw under the charter;
- the intervention fails its review;
- a permanent lawful institution assumes the function;
- coordination is no longer necessary;
- a simpler or less intrusive mechanism becomes adequate.

Closure shall assign unresolved duties, records, remedies, contracts, workers, assets, data, and continuing service responsibilities. A legal sunset without material capacity to end is not complete closure.

# 6. Decision observability and epistemic governance

## 6.1 Consequential decisions

A decision is consequential where it may materially affect rights, jurisdiction, public funds, Indigenous consent, ecological limits, access to essential services, sanctions, institutional continuity, or cross-boundary obligations.

Consequential decisions require the records in this section.

## 6.2 Observation Contract

An Observation Contract states:

- decision and authority;
- state or condition being estimated;
- observation channels;
- source provenance;
- uncertainty;
- missing populations;
- independence and correlation;
- retention and access;
- challenge and correction;
- review and retirement of the observation method.

## 6.3 State Estimate Record

A State Estimate Record states:

- competing hypotheses;
- supporting and conflicting evidence;
- confidence and unresolved ambiguity;
- distributional and rights implications;
- local and affected-community interpretations;
- proposed action and alternatives;
- evidence that would change the estimate.

## 6.4 Observation Independence and Common-Mode Map

The map discloses shared observers, platforms, funders, incentives, algorithms, governance bodies, data pipelines, professional networks, methodological assumptions, and infrastructure.

Several channels sharing one dependency shall not be counted as independent merely because their outputs are displayed separately.

For high-consequence coordination, the map shall identify:

- common points of failure;
- at least one operationally independent channel where feasible;
- fallback when shared infrastructure fails;
- residual correlation risk;
- the authority accepting that risk where genuine independence is impossible.

## 6.5 Epistemic rights

Affected persons and institutions have rights to:

- notice;
- inspect the evidence used where lawful;
- correct errors;
- provide context and counter-evidence;
- challenge standing and method;
- request human review;
- appeal consequential decisions;
- withhold protected information where no lawful override exists;
- obtain reversal or remediation when the decision fails.

## 6.6 Protected non-transfer

A coordination interface may accept a bounded conclusion, condition, refusal, veto, or attestation without requiring disclosure of the knowledge behind it.

Sacred, Indigenous-restricted, personal, security-sensitive, or intentionally unrecorded knowledge shall not be made open merely to satisfy a generalized transparency norm.

## 6.7 Minimum necessary documentation

Meta-Governance shall collect and retain only the records necessary for authority, coordination, accountability, learning, challenge, remedy, and closure.

Documentation shall be accessible in plain-language, oral, visual, assisted, paper, or offline forms where required. Existing valid records shall be referenced rather than recreated. Records shall expire, archive, return, or delete according to purpose and law.

The absence of a standardized field does not establish absence of value, standing, or relevant knowledge.

## 6.8 Federated institutional memory

Institutional memory should be federated, versioned, and purpose-limited.

A public constitutional record may coexist with local archives, protected repositories, oral custody, and records that expire or are withdrawn. No universal blockchain or central repository is required.

# 7. Learning and Adaptation Profile

## 7.1 From scalar velocity to a profile

v1.4 proposed a Learning Velocity Index comparing growth in observational dimensionality with growth in disturbance dimensionality. That intuition survives only as a research hypothesis.

Institutional learning cannot be reduced to sensor expansion. v1.5 introduced a multidimensional **Learning and Adaptation Profile**; v1.6 retains it and adds coordination-added latency, administrative burden, response ownership, and structural reduction.

## 7.2 Profile dimensions

The profile reports separately:

1. **Detection latency** — how quickly meaningful changes are noticed.
2. **Coverage and missingness** — who and what remain unseen.
3. **Observer independence** — whether distinct channels have distinct dependencies.
4. **Uncertainty calibration** — whether confidence matches later outcomes.
5. **Challenge responsiveness** — whether dissent changes inquiry or decisions.
6. **Correction latency** — how quickly known errors are repaired.
7. **Implementation capacity** — whether authorized changes can be carried out.
8. **Outcome learning** — whether independent human and ecological outcomes improve.
9. **Model revision** — whether failed predictions alter assumptions.
10. **Architectural adaptability** — whether the institution can change roles, interfaces, or structure lawfully.
11. **Rights preservation** — whether learning processes avoid surveillance, retaliation, and coercive legibility.
12. **Knowledge diversity** — whether relevant ways of knowing can participate without forced translation.
13. **Coordination-added latency** — delay introduced by interfaces, serial handoffs, and review architecture.
14. **Administrative burden** — time, expertise, translation, and documentation costs imposed on participants.
15. **Response ownership** — whether acceptance, refusal, delay, incapacity, and unresolved duties remain attributable.
16. **Structural reduction** — whether coordination is avoided, simplified, devolved, federated, or ended when no longer necessary.

No target composite is required.

## 7.3 Learning deficit review

A persistent deficit in one or more dimensions may trigger:

- public notice;
- independent review;
- capacity support;
- an adversarial assessment;
- a causal-responsibility review;
- a response, refusal, and omission record;
- a bounded pilot proposal;
- a constitutional review request to the relevant authority.

It does not automatically shift resources or authority. A coordination body shall not use a deficit it helped define as a self-justifying claim to additional jurisdiction or budget.

## 7.4 Sensor-change governance

Adding, removing, or combining consequential observation channels requires review of:

- purpose;
- marginal information value;
- surveillance and chilling effects;
- source dependence;
- cultural intrusion;
- cost and administrative burden;
- retirement and deletion.

## 7.5 Learning through restraint

A system may learn by deciding not to observe, not to standardize, not to integrate, or not to act where the expected informational or rights cost exceeds the coordination benefit.

# 8. Variety Gap Assessment

## 8.1 Purpose

A Variety Gap Assessment asks whether an institution’s legitimate action and observation capacities are adequate for a defined class of disturbances.

It does not claim to calculate the total dimensionality of society or establish one objective measure of requisite variety.

## 8.2 Decision-specific scope

Each assessment identifies:

- institution and lawful function;
- disturbance class;
- relevant timescale;
- required distinctions;
- available actions;
- observation channels;
- known blind spots;
- principal causal variables and their legal and practical controllers;
- rights and resource constraints;
- burdens assigned to the institution;
- upstream and external duties;
- competing assessments;
- whether an Impossible Mandate or Pilot Notice is warranted.

## 8.3 Plural assessment

At least two independent assessment teams should be used for high-consequence reviews, including affected-community or domain-authority participation where feasible.

Disagreement is published.

## 8.4 Alert status

A widening variety gap is an alert indicating that the institution may need:

- narrower mandate;
- additional local observers;
- improved interfaces;
- capacity support;
- decentralization;
- new response options;
- reduced complexity imposed by other institutions;
- a bounded experiment;
- an Impossible Mandate or Pilot Notice;
- lawful constitution of missing shared authority.

The assessment cannot declare an institution constitutionally invalid or assign responsibility to a body merely because consequences appear within its territory or programme.

## 8.5 The environment is not the only variable

A mismatch may arise because:

- disturbances changed;
- the institution lost capacity;
- its mandate is incoherent;
- another institution created avoidable complexity;
- observation is correlated or distorted;
- the chosen objective is wrong;
- the system is attempting to govern what should remain ungoverned.

# 9. Fractal Governance Bandwidth and response obligations

## 9.1 Bandwidth as diagnostic, not jurisdiction

Different governance layers operate at different characteristic latencies. A local emergency service, regional infrastructure body, global strategic institution, and intergenerational council do not act on the same timescale.

Response speed does not create authority. Retaining authority may nevertheless carry a separately constituted duty to respond within a period appropriate to the condition.

## 9.2 Response-Time and Bandwidth Record

A consequential coordination arrangement shall document or reference:

- disturbance timescale;
- sensing latency;
- validation latency;
- deliberation latency;
- acknowledgment clock;
- decision clock;
- implementation or action clock;
- feedback latency;
- coordination-added latency;
- ecological or social lag;
- risk of acting too slowly;
- risk of acting before diagnosis;
- lawful clock source;
- interim protection or pre-authorized safe state;
- escalation when a clock is missed.

A response clock should cite valid law, charter, compact, contract, voluntary commitment, a GMEAIA-07 assessment, or a combination of these. The record does not create a legal duty where none exists.

## 9.3 Response and performance omission

The lifecycle shall distinguish:

- no response to a valid request;
- lawful refusal;
- justified deferment;
- misrouting;
- practical capacity limitation;
- constitutional absence;
- acceptance followed by missed action or review clocks.

Acceptance does not close the omission question if the recipient then fails to perform the accepted duty.

## 9.4 Non-override rule

A higher-scale body may not override a lower-scale decision merely because it operates on a longer planning horizon or claims a broader model.

Intervention requires:

- valid jurisdiction;
- demonstrated cross-boundary or rights condition;
- minimum necessary authority;
- causal-responsibility analysis;
- local sensemaking;
- protected continuity;
- review and return.

## 9.5 Fast signals and slow variables

Fast operational telemetry shall not automatically control slow constitutional or ecological decisions. Slow-variable changes require the specialized protocols of the relevant domain frameworks.

# 10. Bypass Architecture Protocol

## 10.1 Purpose

A bypass is a bounded institutional experiment created where an existing system cannot presently perform a necessary function or test a credible alternative.

A bypass is not proof that the incumbent has lost legitimacy or that the experiment deserves permanent authority. A bypass shall be small enough for safety and large enough to expose the causal mechanism it claims to test.

## 10.2 Authorization

A Bypass Charter must be authorized by the institution holding the relevant competence or by a valid Treaty procedure.

Meta-Governance may incubate and coordinate the proposal but may not charter coercive jurisdiction on its own.

## 10.3 Required charter terms

The charter states:

1. problem and affected population;
2. authority and jurisdiction;
3. participants and non-participants;
4. rights and protected minimums;
5. resources and funding;
6. observation and evaluation;
7. data boundaries;
8. interaction with incumbent institutions;
9. duration and review;
10. exit and closure;
11. minimum causally adequate scope and conclusions the experiment cannot establish;
12. response duties and clocks for consequential incumbent handoffs;
13. possible future dispositions;
14. continuity obligations;
15. ordinary owner, maintenance capacity, and support-withdrawal test if continuation is proposed.

## 10.4 Possible dispositions

A bypass may:

- dissolve after the experiment;
- be reintegrated into an incumbent;
- become a federated partner;
- continue in stable coexistence;
- transfer a bounded function through lawful agreement;
- contribute to a newly constituted successor.

There is no presumption that reintegration is always correct or that parallel existence is always fragmentation.

## 10.5 Learning transfer

A bypass should publish transferable findings where lawful, including failures and contextual limits. Protected information remains protected.

The incumbent is not compelled to adopt a result merely because the bypass reports success. Where a response duty is validly constituted, non-adoption, deferment, capacity limitation, or referral must receive a reasoned and reviewable response. Timeout does not transfer the function to the bypass.

## 10.6 Anti-enclave safeguards

A bypass must be reviewed for:

- privileged access;
- displacement of public obligations;
- extraction of data or resources;
- exclusion of affected populations;
- regulatory arbitrage;
- dependency on unaccountable funders;
- irreversible infrastructure or rights effects.

# 11. Coexistence, integration, and succession

## 11.1 Three relationship tracks

Meta-Governance may support:

- **reform partnership** within existing institutions;
- **parallel experimentation or service provision**;
- **lawful succession** where a valid authority transfers or reconstitutes a function.

The tracks are options, not a predetermined evolutionary sequence.

## 11.2 Non-predatory transition

New institutions should not manufacture incumbent failure, withhold essential cooperation, or use information advantages to force transfer.

Incumbents should not sabotage lawful alternatives solely to preserve institutional position.

## 11.3 Functional transfer

A transfer requires:

- authority from the body legally able to transfer the function;
- authority-formation classification;
- public reasons;
- affected-party participation;
- a Causal Responsibility Profile;
- capability, dependency, and common-mode risk assessment;
- rights and continuity plan;
- assets, liabilities, records, and staff treatment;
- response and handoff records;
- appeal and review;
- conditions for return or further change.

An Accountable Coordination Audit or Variety Gap Assessment may support the case but cannot execute the transfer.

## 11.4 Stable coexistence

Two institutions may legitimately continue in parallel where they:

- serve different populations;
- use different epistemic or cultural approaches;
- provide redundancy;
- allow meaningful choice;
- do not create destructive externalities;
- maintain interoperable minimums where necessary.

## 11.5 Immune-war de-escalation

Transition actors should distinguish:

- legitimate legal resistance;
- self-protective institutional obstruction;
- rights-based refusal;
- misinformation;
- ordinary political disagreement.

No AI system or coordination council may classify political opposition as an “immune response” and thereby justify exceptional power.

# 12. Institutional sunset and dissolution

## 12.1 Sunset review

Every Meta-Governance institution shall have periodic review and, where appropriate, a sunset date.

Review may consider:

- mission completion;
- continued need;
- rights effects;
- cost;
- duplication;
- capture;
- trust;
- adaptability;
- budget, staffing, prestige, appointment, and survival incentives;
- whether the institution benefits from identifying additional coordination need;
- whether a less intrusive arrangement is available;
- whether the function can be avoided, simplified, devolved, federated, or ended.

## 12.2 Governance Sunset Commission

A Governance Sunset Commission may conduct independent reviews and recommend continuation, reform, devolution, merger, federation, or dissolution.

It shall not unilaterally dissolve institutions unless a constitution or charter expressly grants that narrow power and supplies appeal, continuity, and review.

## 12.3 Appointment and independence

Commission members should have:

- fixed, non-renewable or tightly limited terms;
- conflict restrictions;
- no simultaneous leadership in reviewed institutions;
- transparent methods;
- dissent rights;
- removal only for defined cause.

Appointment by a foresight body alone is insufficient for institutions with constitutional consequences.

## 12.4 Dissolution authorization

Dissolution is authorized by:

- the constituting authority;
- a procedure in the institution’s charter;
- a competent tribunal;
- a Treaty process validly empowered to act.

## 12.5 Continuity plan

Before dissolution, publish:

- rights and service continuity;
- records and protected knowledge;
- staff, pension, contractor, and contractual obligations;
- funding for transition and closure work;
- assets and liabilities;
- active cases, remedies, and appeals;
- transfer or termination of powers;
- data return, deletion, archive, and access revocation;
- public communication;
- post-dissolution accountability.

A sunset is not materially complete while staff, beneficiaries, records, essential services, or unresolved duties lack a lawful destination.

## 12.6 Protocol preservation

Useful protocols and knowledge may survive institutional dissolution, but preservation must respect consent, data retention, Indigenous knowledge, security, and the right to deletion or non-transfer.

# 13. Emergency coordination

## 13.1 Constitutional basis

Meta-Governance emergency coordination operates under Treaty’s Emergency Constitutionalism provisions and relevant domain protocols.

A coordination council may activate a **convening function** within 24 hours. Convening is not command authority.

## 13.2 Emergency Coordination Record

The record states:

- emergency condition and evidence;
- declaring authority;
- participating institutions;
- powers activated;
- powers prohibited;
- affected area and population;
- Indigenous and local interfaces;
- essential services and material minimums;
- data and observation rules;
- duration, review, and termination;
- restoration and after-action obligations.

## 13.3 Provisional action

Where immediate action is lawfully authorized, provisional measures remain narrow, reversible, evidence-preserving, and normally limited to the Treaty’s 72-hour window before fuller authorization.

## 13.4 No emergency competence creep

Emergency coordination shall not be used to:

- create permanent jurisdiction;
- bypass Indigenous consent unrelated to the immediate necessity;
- compel work outside Work in Liberation safeguards;
- alter financial architecture permanently;
- suppress dissent or system-canary testimony;
- retain emergency data without review;
- normalize repeated temporary authority.

## 13.5 Crisis work and finance

Mobilization of people and Community Providers follows Work in Liberation. Financial liquidity and settlement follow Financial Systems. AUBI Layer 1 and protected minimums remain governed by AUBI and the Treaty.

## 13.6 After-action review

Review shall test:

- whether the declaration was justified;
- whose evidence was missing;
- rights and subgroup effects;
- whether provisional measures became de facto policy;
- whether ordinary capacity should be strengthened;
- what data should be deleted;
- what compensation or repair is owed.

# 14. Power, participation, and cognitive equity

## 14.1 Multi-Modal Deliberation Protocol

Official Meta-Governance proceedings shall provide appropriate combinations of:

- real-time verbal deliberation;
- asynchronous written participation;
- oral and narrative testimony;
- visual and systems-mapping methods;
- supported decision-making;
- interpretation and translation;
- sensory-accessible and low-stimulation formats;
- offline participation.

Equal formal availability is not enough; the process shall examine whether one mode still controls agenda, prestige, or decision weight.

## 14.2 Facilitator role

Facilitators shall be trained in:

- power-aware process;
- cognitive and disability inclusion;
- cross-cultural humility;
- conflict of interest;
- distinction between mediation and adjudication;
- protection of dissent;
- documentation of unresolved disagreement.

Facilitators do not certify substantive truth or cultural legitimacy.

## 14.3 Conscious Refuser protections

A participant may refuse a process that:

- lacks authority;
- demands ethical compromise;
- requires disclosure of protected knowledge;
- imposes coercive legibility;
- fails the Accountable Coordination test;
- creates inaccessible or cognitively harmful conditions.

Refusal shall not automatically reduce standing, benefits, future participation, or civic status.

## 14.4 System Sanity and Cognitive Friction Audits

Audits may examine:

- unnecessary cognitive load;
- contradictory rules;
- procedural theatre;
- moral injury;
- sensory exclusion;
- excessive documentation;
- metric gaming;
- demands to affirm institutional narratives contradicted by experience.

An audit is not a mental-health diagnosis of participants.

## 14.5 Narrative–reality alignment

Institutions shall compare public claims with observable outcomes, complaints, distributional effects, and independent evidence.

The goal is not a single official narrative but visibility into where narratives diverge from conditions.

# 15. Knowledge and technology

## 15.1 Human authority supremacy

Humans and legitimate institutions remain responsible for ethical and constitutional judgment. An AI recommendation does not dilute accountability.

## 15.2 Permitted AI support

AI may support:

- translation;
- document comparison;
- scenario generation;
- anomaly detection;
- simulation;
- accessibility;
- retrieval and summarization;
- administrative routing.

## 15.3 Prohibited AI functions

AI may not independently determine:

- jurisdiction;
- Indigenous identity or authority;
- consent or FPIC;
- truth of sacred or cultural claims;
- criminal liability;
- sanctions;
- emergency declaration;
- institutional capture;
- authority transfer;
- dissolution.

## 15.4 Distributed sensemaking

Distributed Sensemaking Councils or networks may connect scientific, local, professional, Indigenous-authorized, experiential, and civil-society knowledge.

They shall document translation loss and preserve the option that knowledge systems remain partially incommensurable.

## 15.5 Cognitive immunity

Information-integrity measures should strengthen source evaluation, public reasoning, media literacy, provenance, and plural inquiry.

They shall not create a centralized authority over legitimate political belief or treat criticism of the GGF as disinformation.

## 15.6 Technology refusal and community veto

Communities may reject intrusive or culturally inappropriate technology within their jurisdiction, subject to valid rights and cross-boundary obligations. Indigenous technology decisions follow Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing.

# 16. Funding and resource sharing

## 16.1 No self-authorized tax

Meta-Governance v1.6 does not create a Coordination Infrastructure Tax or determine a global levy rate.

Funding may come from:

- Treaty-authorized revenue protocols;
- assessed contributions;
- voluntary contributions;
- grants subject to independence safeguards;
- shared-service fees under compacts;
- time-limited pilot funding.

## 16.2 Funding record

Every Meta-Governance institution shall disclose:

- sources;
- restrictions;
- allocation authority;
- conflicts of interest;
- administrative overhead;
- incentives linked to case volume, coordination activity, compliance, renewal, or expansion;
- equity provisions;
- support for devolution, closure, staff transition, and local capacity transfer;
- audit and termination conditions.

## 16.3 Equity participation

Participation support may include compensation, care, travel, accessibility, translation, connectivity, and community-controlled research resources.

Fixed global allocation percentages are proposals requiring lawful budget authorization, not constitutional rules of this framework.

## 16.4 No automatic reallocation

A capture alert may trigger review or a temporary hold where lawfully authorized. It may not automatically redirect funds to the body issuing the alert.

## 16.5 Resource-sharing interfaces

Shared infrastructure and crisis resources shall use explicit compacts defining ownership, access, priority, liability, maintenance, exit, data rights, switching costs, fallback, and the material feasibility of withdrawal. Technical control shall not become political authority through pricing, standards, or access decisions.

# 17. Capture resistance, common-mode risk, and objective reproduction

## 17.1 Structural rather than rhetorical protection

A body is not capture-resistant because it uses rotation, AI detection, sortition, transparency, or the word “polycentric.” Capture resistance requires independent powers, practical challenge, exit, rights, limits on self-expansion, and visibility into the incentives that reproduce its behavior.

## 17.2 Capture and objective-reproduction indicators

Possible indicators include:

- self-justifying mandate expansion;
- asymmetric transparency;
- prohibitive or merely formal exit;
- concentration of agenda control;
- correlated observers and common infrastructure;
- retaliation against dissent;
- funding dependency;
- selective enforcement;
- permanent emergency;
- metrics optimized for institutional survival;
- procedural throughput treated as proof of value;
- case, meeting, audit, or record volume rewarded independently of outcomes;
- staff, funder, vendor, or facilitator dependence on continued coordination;
- routine renewal despite mission completion;
- closure costs borne by workers and communities while continuation benefits accrue to the institution.

No target veto frequency, participation rate, trust score, or decision-share percentage proves health or capture by itself.

## 17.3 Independence and Objective-Reproduction Review

Long-lived, systemically central, or self-renewing Meta-Governance mechanisms shall complete or reference the applicable GMEAIA review. It shall examine:

- shared funders, data, AI, vendors, infrastructure, methods, professional networks, and ontologies;
- common points of failure and operational fallback;
- budget, appointment, promotion, prestige, blame, and survival incentives;
- incentives of auditors, facilitators, tool stewards, and funders;
- behavior likely to regenerate after reform;
- conditions that make the coordination function unnecessary.

Organizational separation does not establish independence where the same substrate shapes all channels.

## 17.4 Integrity response ladder

A response may include:

1. notice and evidence preservation;
2. independent inquiry;
3. public response and correction;
4. capacity support or conflict separation;
5. temporary reversible containment where lawfully authorized;
6. governance or incentive reform;
7. lawful removal, transfer, devolution, or closure;
8. dissolution through authorized process.

The institution issuing the alert should not control the full response chain.

## 17.5 Whistleblowers and dissent

Participants, staff, observers, and affected people shall have protected channels for confidential reporting, external review, public dissent, and remedy.

## 17.6 Integrity of the integrity system

Auditors, Sunset Commissions, Adversary bodies, foresight units, and review panels require their own:

- mandate limits;
- funding and incentive disclosure;
- source-independence and common-mode maps;
- appeal;
- rotation or term rules;
- external audit;
- response duties where validly constituted;
- dissolution pathways.

No exclusive conformance certifier is created by this framework. Conformance may be supported through self-attestation with evidence, peer review, independent challenge, and lawful audit. A body reviewing conformance should be institutionally separate from funding decisions where feasible.

# 18. Evaluation framework

## 18.1 No single Meta-Governance score

The framework does not use one composite index to certify a governance system as healthy, adaptive, legitimate, or future-ready.

## 18.2 Evaluation dimensions

Report separately:

### Coordination effectiveness

- whether the relevant authorities were connected;
- whether causal responsibility and duty-holders were correctly identified;
- commitment completion;
- acceptance, refusal, timeout, capacity-limit, and performance-omission patterns;
- unresolved interface and constitutional gaps;
- coordination-added latency;
- duplication, translation, and administrative load;
- outcome latency and independent effects;
- coordination avoided, simplified, devolved, federated, or terminated.

### Legitimacy and participation

- standing of affected groups;
- accessibility;
- power distribution;
- refusal without retaliation;
- appeal and correction;
- missing participants.

### Epistemic quality

- source and infrastructure independence;
- common-mode risk and fallback;
- uncertainty calibration;
- visibility of disagreement;
- local and Indigenous knowledge boundaries;
- model revision after disconfirmation.

### Adaptability

- correction latency;
- capacity support;
- lawful architectural change;
- pilot learning;
- ability to de-escalate or close coordination.

### Rights and equity

- subgroup effects;
- privacy and protected non-transfer;
- economic and administrative burden;
- disability and cognitive access;
- retaliation or coercion.

### Planetary and intergenerational outcomes

Use domain-authorized ecological and future-generations evidence. Meta-Governance does not create its own universal ecological score.

### Capture resistance

- mandate discipline;
- symmetric accountability;
- exit and challenge;
- independent authorization;
- institutional self-limitation.

### Structural impermanence

- completed and materially supported sunsets;
- successful devolution and local capacity transfer;
- continuity during closure;
- unresolved-duty custody;
- reduction of unnecessary coordination infrastructure;
- proportion of temporary bodies routinely renewed;
- whether success makes the coordination layer less necessary.

## 18.3 Real-time dashboards

Dashboards may show operational status, uncertainty, clocks, handoff states, and alerts. They shall not make constitutional judgments, infer legitimacy from conformance, or expose protected personal or community information.

Throughput indicators—meetings, records, interfaces, referrals, or audits—shall not be reported as success without independent outcomes and administrative-burden context.

## 18.4 Citizen Review Panels

Citizen panels may require a public response, commission review, or exercise binding authority only where the constituting instrument grants it. “Community trust” must not be inferred from platform participation alone.

## 18.5 Framework Falsification Register

Meta-Governance v1.6 advances the following framework-level hypotheses:

1. bounded coordination reduces unresolved cross-domain conflict, duplication, and responsibility gaps without producing residual sovereignty;
2. decision-chain separation reduces concentration without creating unacceptable delay or omission;
3. plural observation and common-mode review improve calibration and correction;
4. bypasses transfer valid learning without becoming privileged enclaves;
5. sunset, devolution, and closure occur materially rather than only legally;
6. causal-responsibility and response records reduce burden dumping and unowned non-action;
7. proportional documentation improves accountability without excluding lower-capacity participants.

Evidence supporting narrowing, suspension, replacement, or non-scaling includes:

- coordination-added latency or administrative burden exceeding the harm reduced;
- persistent constitutional gaps or timeouts despite correct process;
- coordination bodies expanding budget, staff, data, or mandate without independent outcome improvement;
- nominally plural observers sharing one decisive substrate;
- bypasses succeeding only under exceptional support or failing at ordinary institutional transition;
- sunsets producing routine renewal or abandonment of workers, services, records, or remedies;
- simpler direct or domain-specific arrangements performing better;
- low-capacity actors being systematically excluded by the documentation architecture.

No single indicator decides the framework’s validity. Review shall publish evidence, disagreement, and the proposed scope of any narrowing or replacement.

# 19. Implementation pathway

## 19.1 Phase 0 — Constitutional conformance

Before operational expansion:

- align charters with the Treaty and Constitutional Interface Specification;
- adopt compatible GMEAIA authority-effect, consequence-profile, handoff, clock, and closure semantics;
- identify retained, delegated, transferred, concurrent, jointly constituted, and absent authority;
- remove automatic authority-transfer and dissolution triggers;
- establish Indigenous interfaces through the applicable framework and Indigenous authority;
- create Observation Contract, State Estimate, Causal Responsibility, and response-record profiles;
- define proportional documentation, appeal, remedy, and material closure.

## 19.2 Phase 1 — Interface pilots

Pilot low-coercion functions such as:

- cross-domain mapping;
- shared-service coordination;
- multi-modal deliberation;
- plural state estimates;
- federated institutional memory;
- voluntary foresight networks;
- sunset review of temporary working groups;
- response, refusal, and omission tracking;
- minimum-necessary documentation and low-tech record equivalence.

## 19.3 Phase 2 — Bounded adaptive pilots

Test:

- Learning and Adaptation Profiles;
- Variety Gap Assessments;
- Bypass Charters;
- polycentric interface records;
- stable-coexistence and federation options;
- independent integrity and common-mode reviews;
- minimum causally adequate pilots;
- material closure and support-withdrawal tests.

No pilot should begin with compulsory taxation, sanction, emergency command, or authority transfer.

## 19.4 Phase 3 — Treaty activation

Where Treaty institutions lawfully enter into force, Meta-Governance functions may be activated through explicit charters and budgets.

## 19.5 Phase 4 — Review, devolution, and succession

The implementation programme must evaluate not only expansion but:

- which coordination functions can return to domain institutions;
- which shared services can be federated;
- which bodies should close;
- which experiments should remain plural;
- whether the Meta-Governance layer is becoming less necessary;
- whether coordination-added latency and administrative burden justify redesign;
- whether the framework-level falsification findings require narrowing or replacement.

## 19.6 No fixed civilizational timetable

The previous 15-year plan is retained only as an illustrative planning horizon. Institutional readiness depends on legitimacy, capacity, consent, and outcomes rather than calendar progression.

## 20.1 Treaty for Our Only Home

The Treaty supplies:

- constitutional authority;
- institutions;
- decision classes;
- emergency rules;
- finance and enforcement protocols;
- entry into force;
- amendment and withdrawal.

Meta-Governance supplies coordination procedures inside those limits.

## 20.2 Constitutional Interface Specification

The Specification supplies shared rules for:

- plural legitimacy;
- causal subsidiarity and responsibility;
- duties attached to retained jurisdiction;
- omission, incapacity, and constitutional gaps;
- proportional procedural burden;
- observation;
- automatic controls;
- veto and injunction;
- bypasses;
- transfer;
- dissolution;
- continuity;
- framework-level falsification.

GMEAIA supplies the operational lifecycle grammar for:

- authority effects and formation;
- consequence profiles;
- causal-responsibility records;
- acknowledgment, decision, and action clocks;
- accepted, refused, deferred, referred, timed-out, capacity-limited, and constitutional-gap states;
- sender and recipient custody;
- cross-framework handoffs;
- proportional record bundles;
- correction, unresolved duties, and closure.

Meta-Governance does not redefine those controlled states locally.

## 20.4 Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing

That framework governs the GGF’s obligations toward Indigenous sovereignty, consent, knowledge, technology, and community-determined institutions.

Meta-Governance does not define an Indigenous representation percentage or create an Earth Council.

## 20.5 Polycentric Governance Architecture

The Polycentric framework supplies useful horizontal interface concepts among territorial councils, Commons Trusts, practice institutions, and Indigenous authority.

Meta-Governance applies those concepts without assuming universal chambers, target veto frequencies, automatic jury dissolution, generalized trust scores, or GGF-designed Indigenous institutions.

## 20.6 AUBI

AUBI supplies material security that makes refusal more credible and defines population-level observation, claimant rights, and protected unobservability.

Meta-Governance may not use AUBI, LMCI, or Love Ledger data as generalized legitimacy, participation, or institutional-health scores.

## 20.7 Financial Systems

Financial Systems governs Hearts, Leaves, reserves, redemption, settlement, financial crisis controls, ICTL, and NARP.

Meta-Governance may coordinate financial interfaces but does not set exchange rates, redirect funds automatically, or activate Sovereign Debt Transformation.

## 20.8 Nested Economies

Nested Economies governs economic subsidiarity, BAZ-to-BAZ interfaces, voluntary coordination layers, and solidarity protocols.

## 20.9 Work in Liberation

Work in Liberation governs worker rights, Community Providers, professional and guild institutions, work matching, commissioning, and emergency mobilisation.

## 20.10 Domain frameworks

Health, peace, technology, justice, climate, biodiversity, migration, education, and other domain frameworks retain substantive authority within their competence.

# 21. Conformance requirements

A Meta-Governance institution conforms to v1.6 only if it can show:

- lawful constituting authority and authority-formation classification;
- bounded purpose and competence;
- a justified GMEAIA consequence profile;
- a Polycentric Interface Record where relevant;
- decision-chain separation;
- Causal Responsibility Profile for consequential coordination;
- Observation Contracts and plural State Estimate Records where required;
- Observation Independence and Common-Mode Maps;
- accessible participation and independent advice;
- Indigenous recognition, consent, and protected-knowledge safeguards;
- response-duty source, clocks, handoff states, and unresolved-duty custody where applicable;
- distinction among lawful refusal, deferment, timeout, capacity limitation, obstruction, and constitutional absence;
- limits on AI and automation;
- rights-bounded emergency procedures;
- minimum necessary and consequence-proportional documentation;
- appeal, correction, remedy, and restoration;
- funding and objective-reproduction transparency;
- sunset, material closure, and continuity provisions;
- independent outcome evidence;
- no inference that conformance establishes legitimacy or success;
- no automatic authority transfer, funding capture, sanction, or dissolution from its own metrics;
- a framework or mechanism-level falsification profile where long-lived or systemically central.

Conformance may be supported through evidence-backed self-attestation, peer review, independent challenge, and lawful audit. This framework creates no exclusive conformance certifier.

Non-conformance shall be published with a remediation plan or explicit statement that the mechanism remains a non-operative proposal. `P0` constitutional conflicts, `P1` interface gaps, and `P2` operational gaps retain the meanings defined by the Constitutional Interface Specification and must not be confused with GMEAIA consequence profiles `CP0`–`CP3`.

# Layer 4 — The governance horizon

## 1. Governance as a temporary concentration of relationship

Governance is not the opposite of freedom, and freedom is not the absence of every institution. Governance becomes necessary where actions create obligations, where harms cross boundaries, where shared resources require stewardship, and where unequal power makes informal coordination insufficient.

But institutions are not reality’s final form. They are temporary concentrations of attention, authority, memory, and response capacity. Their legitimacy depends on what called them into being and on whether they remain answerable to the people, relations, and conditions they affect.

## 2. Coordination without the fantasy of the whole

No institution can see civilization from nowhere.

A global body sees patterns hidden locally and loses distinctions visible on the ground. A local body knows context and may miss downstream effects. Scientific models reveal measurable dynamics and may omit sacred, experiential, or political realities. Traditional Knowledge can preserve long relational memory and must not be extracted into categories that destroy its meaning.

Meta-Governance is therefore not the construction of a perfect overview. It is the design of relationships among partial observers who can challenge, complement, and sometimes refuse one another.

## 3. The intelligence-honor principle

People who perceive systemic contradiction early are often treated as inconvenient, disordered, disloyal, or insufficiently pragmatic. Institutions need ways to hear such perception without declaring every objection correct and without making dissenters carry the full burden of proof.

An intelligent system preserves channels through which discomfort can become inquiry rather than retaliation.

## 4. Natural coordination and its limits

Communities may develop deep capacities for mutual aid, restorative conflict, shared stewardship, and consensual action. Formal governance may recede where relationship and competence make it unnecessary.

Yet “natural coordination” must not become a spiritualized excuse for invisible domination, suppressed conflict, gendered care burdens, charismatic authority, or the absence of appeal. Informality is not automatically freedom.

## 5. The right to refuse

A system proves little by making participation easy for those who already agree with it. Its integrity is tested by what happens to the person, community, institution, or people who say no.

Refusal can be mistaken, partial, harmful, principled, protective, or necessary. The task is not to romanticize refusal but to preserve enough material security, standing, and due process that consent remains meaningful.

## 6. Learning as the capacity to be changed

An institution has not learned merely because it collected more information, issued another report, or adopted new terminology. Learning is visible when evidence changes its model, when challenge changes its procedure, when harm changes its incentives, when delay becomes attributable, and when success allows it to simplify, devolve, or relinquish power.

## 7. Liberatory impermanence

The highest achievement of a coordination body may be to make itself unnecessary.

This does not mean premature dissolution or neglect of public capacity. It means that the body’s identity is not fused with its mission. It may teach a protocol, federate a service, return authority, archive a lesson, and end.

The protocol may remain. The institution does not need to become immortal.

## 8. Governing the governance of governance

Meta-Governance itself must remain inside the field it studies.

It has blind spots. Its audits can be captured. Its language can become ritual. Its dashboards can privilege what is easy to observe. Its desire for coherence can erase legitimate difference. Its theory of adaptation can become a justification for permanent intervention.

For that reason, the framework’s governing maxims are:

> **Meta-Governance may help institutions perceive their interdependence and coordinate their legitimate powers. It may not turn its own perception of the system into a power over the system.**

> **Recognized interdependence must lead to named responsibility, reviewable response, and a lawful path through refusal, incapacity, delay, or constitutional absence—without creating authority by silence.**

# Conclusion

Integrated Meta-Governance v1.6 retains the ambition of v1.4: governance systems must learn, coordinate, resist capture, experiment, and die gracefully.

It changes the constitutional answer to one decisive question: **who gets to act on that diagnosis?**

The answer is no longer “the Meta-Governance architecture itself.”

Observation remains distinct from judgment. Coordination remains distinct from jurisdiction. Experiment remains distinct from succession. Audit remains distinct from dissolution. Foresight remains distinct from command.

Meta-Governance is strongest not when it becomes the brain of planetary governance, but when it builds reliable interfaces among institutions that remain plural, bounded, contestable, and capable of refusing it.

v1.6 adds the complementary discipline that restraint alone is not enough. A constitutionally careful system can still fail when responsibility is assigned without causal control, legitimate authorities do not respond, handoffs time out, observers share one substrate, or coordination institutions reproduce themselves through process rather than outcomes.

The standard is therefore not more coordination. It is coordination that names responsibility, respects jurisdiction, operates within the timescale of the condition, reduces rather than exports administrative burden, and can demonstrate when it should become less necessary.
