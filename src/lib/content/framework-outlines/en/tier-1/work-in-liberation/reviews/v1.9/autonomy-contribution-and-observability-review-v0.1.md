# Work in Liberation Autonomy, Contribution, and Observability Review v0.1

## Does the framework liberate people from compulsory work, or reorganize contribution into a more humane but still coercive system?

**Status:** Internal bounded architectural review
**Primary object:** Work in Liberation Framework v1.9
**Compatibility targets:** AUBI Framework v2.5; Financial Systems Framework v3.4; Nested Economies Framework v3.2
**Review scope:** Participation autonomy, Community Work Teams, Community Provider governance, flexible time, need broadcasting, matching, work validation, informal care, payment, Community Weaver roles, crisis mobilisation, industrial transition, planetary work, and monitoring
**Out of scope:** External empirical validation of labor-market assumptions, legal feasibility, technical platform performance, projected budgets, case-study claims, or the external treaties and institutions named in the framework

---

# Executive summary

## Overall verdict

**Work in Liberation v1.9 has a strong emancipatory purpose, but its mechanisms do not yet guarantee that liberation survives implementation.**

Its foundational claim is clear: material security should remove the threat of destitution from employment, allowing people to refuse exploitative conditions and contribute through more autonomous, meaningful, and community-governed forms of work. The framework explicitly distinguishes itself from welfare-to-work systems and treats a reduction in labor supplied to exploitative employers as a feature rather than a policy failure.

That constitutional starting point should survive.

The framework also contains several valuable design directions:

* Community Work Teams organized as locally chartered Community Providers;
* worker and community governance rather than external investor ownership;
* flexible and fluid participation across several projects;
* need broadcasting rather than rigid job descriptions;
* support for care, cultural, and ecological work;
* separation of formal Hearts activity from non-monetized informal recognition;
* local cultural adaptation;
* just-transition mechanisms for workers leaving harmful sectors;
* slow-variable protection for ecological work standards;
* accessible and low-tech participation;
* transition pathways for gig workers and conventional businesses.

However, v1.9 has six central architectural problems.

### 1. Freedom from survival coercion is not yet freedom from contribution coercion

The framework protects people from needing a job to survive, but it does not yet protect them from:

* moral pressure to perform “Work With Purpose”;
* reputational consequences of inactivity;
* social visibility through contribution histories;
* algorithmic matching based on past activity;
* Community Renewal ceremonies and public recognition;
* crisis redirection;
* implicit expectations created by a 15-hour contribution norm.

The framework needs an explicit constitutional right to:

* contribute;
* refuse;
* pause;
* remain private;
* engage outside formal systems;
* perform ordinary rather than celebrated work;
* and live without constructing a public contribution identity.

### 2. Need broadcasting is promising, but “need” is not yet governed

v1.9 replaces job postings with problems broadcast by BAZs and Community Providers. People self-select, and fluid teams form around the problem.

The framework does not specify:

* who may declare a need;
* how competing needs are prioritized;
* whether affected people agree with the formulation;
* who supplies resources and compensation;
* how unpopular but essential work is filled;
* how false, inflated, or politically favored needs are challenged;
* or when a broadcast becomes a de facto work directive.

### 3. Matching risks becoming a portable reputation architecture

Skills Resonance Matching is meant to replace self-promotional résumés with the trace of actual contribution, including Love Ledger and Proof of Care records.

This would make previous participation visible to future project organizers and could create a generalized social-work reputation system.

AUBI v2.5 now defines Love Ledger recognition as voluntary, non-monetized, and excluded from LMCI-M, LMCI-A, Hearts, fiat, and entitlement. It also prohibits portable individual LMCI-M scores.
Matching therefore needs to use claimant-controlled, purpose-specific capability credentials—not passive extraction of a person’s relational or contribution history.

### 4. Transaction demand is being used as a proxy for community need

Community Work Teams earn Hearts when community members purchase their services.

That can fund childcare, repair, training, and other directly consumed services. It is less suitable for:

* watershed restoration;
* prevention;
* public health;
* disaster readiness;
* cultural preservation;
* infrastructure maintenance;
* ecosystem monitoring;
* care for people with little spending power;
* services whose beneficiaries are future generations or non-human life.

The framework needs several commissioning channels rather than one market-like demand signal.

### 5. Observation and validation roles are concentrated

Community Weavers train participants, help form providers, facilitate participation, support Love Ledger use, validate services and informal contributions, interpret cultural context, and connect communities to governance.

AUBI v2.5 now explicitly states that Weavers are not presumed neutral or omniscient and may not be the sole validator and adjudicator of the same consequential claim.

Work in Liberation must adopt that separation and add worker-controlled complaint and advocacy channels.

### 6. The framework measures activity more readily than liberation

Its principal composite metric is described as:

* 40% Hearts transactions;
* 30% logged care hours;
* 30% interoperability.

That is neither LMCI-M nor a valid measure of work liberation.

A system could raise all three while increasing:

* social conformity;
* surveillance;
* overwork;
* hidden care burden;
* provider monopoly;
* pressure to log private activity;
* dependence on the platform.

## Central review claim

> **Work in Liberation removes one major source of coercion—the dependence of material survival on employment—but does not yet adequately constrain the social, administrative, algorithmic, moral, and emergency powers that can replace it.**

## Readiness verdict

**Amber-red, suitable for direct revision to v2.0 after this review.**

The framework does not require source reconstruction. The supplied v1.9 file is substantially complete, though it contains encoding corruption, internal reference drift, and a date inconsistency: the status header lists March 10, 2025, while the version history dates v1.9 to March 23, 2026.

The v2.0 revision should be architectural rather than cosmetic.

# 1. Review basis and method

This review distinguishes:

1. **Source statement:** what v1.9 explicitly specifies.
2. **Architectural inference:** a risk or implication arising from those mechanisms.
3. **Compatibility requirement:** a change required by AUBI v2.5, Financial Systems v3.4, or Nested Economies v3.2.

The review uses six tests.

## 1.1 Autonomy test

Can a person refuse, pause, remain private, change direction, or leave without losing:

* AUBI;
* access to necessities;
* civic standing;
* eligibility;
* future opportunity;
* reputation;
* community belonging?

## 1.2 Contribution-boundary test

Does the framework distinguish:

* paid work;
* rewarded contribution;
* voluntary service;
* informal care;
* private life;
* cultural duty;
* civic obligation;
* emergency service?

## 1.3 Demand-and-need test

Can the system distinguish:

* transaction demand;
* unmet material need;
* public goods;
* ecological obligation;
* administratively broadcast priorities;
* socially fashionable projects?

## 1.4 Observation test

Can the framework distinguish:

* visible contribution from actual contribution;
* activity from outcome;
* inactivity from exclusion;
* satisfaction from conformity;
* matching success from meaningful fit;
* participation from freedom?

## 1.5 Authority test

Are commissioning, validation, payment, worker representation, audit, and adjudication controlled by sufficiently separate actors?

## 1.6 Outcome test

Does the framework observe whether people became freer and conditions improved, rather than merely whether more work was recorded and rewarded?

# 2. What should survive

## 2.1 Material security as the precondition of labor freedom

The strongest claim in v1.9 is that employment under a credible survival threat is not fully voluntary. AUBI Layer 1 is intended to make refusal materially possible.

This is compatible with AUBI v2.5, which describes the universal baseline as a material-security foundation and identifies increased practical ability to refuse exploitative conditions as a key long-term outcome.

## 2.2 Work is not the source of human worth

The framework questions whether wage labor should determine material security, social recognition, or life meaning.

v2.0 should sharpen this further:

> **A person’s dignity, rights, baseline income, belonging, and moral standing do not depend on contribution, productivity, employability, ecological usefulness, or willingness to join organized work.**

## 2.3 Community-governed providers

Community Work Teams are defined as locally governed and chartered Community Providers rather than externally owned labor platforms.

Nested Economies v3.2 assigns provider chartering to BAZ Councils while reserving professional and labor standards to Work in Liberation and the FLP.

That boundary is coherent and should be retained.

## 2.4 Fluid participation

v1.9 permits people to participate in several projects, take time away, and return without holding one permanent occupational identity.

This is a real alternative to both fixed employment and atomized gig work.

## 2.5 Slow-variable protection

Green Job Score baselines, eligible ecological categories, and Industrial Sunsetting timelines are subject to the Slow-Variable Decision Protocol and LEKH review.

This should survive, while the financial settlement and evidence details are aligned with the revised frameworks.

## 2.6 Just transition rather than worker abandonment

The framework includes gig-worker transition, cooperative conversion, retraining, fair contracts, and transition funding.

The principle is sound: people should not bear the costs of ending structurally harmful systems merely because they currently work within them.

# 3. The constitutional problem: coercion after survival security

## 3.1 Five sources of work coercion

AUBI can reduce **economic coercion**, but Work in Liberation must also constrain:

1. **social coercion** — praise, shame, belonging, and visible contribution;
2. **administrative coercion** — eligibility, scheduling, documentation, and provider rules;
3. **algorithmic coercion** — ranking, discoverability, matching, and inferred suitability;
4. **moral coercion** — defining some lives as purposeful and others as insufficiently contributory;
5. **emergency coercion** — redirection toward essential work during crisis.

v1.9 contains potential channels for each.

## 3.2 “Work With Purpose” must remain an invitation

The phrase can name a liberatory possibility. It becomes coercive when institutions define purpose on behalf of participants.

A person may legitimately choose:

* private family life;
* contemplation;
* leisure;
* art without audience;
* ordinary paid work;
* episodic participation;
* unrecorded mutual aid;
* recovery from illness;
* no organized contribution at all.

The framework should not imply that a liberated society is one in which every person becomes a visibly regenerative worker.

## 3.3 Constitutional participation clause

v2.0 should state:

> **Participation in Community Work Teams, need broadcasts, Skills Resonance Matching, Love Ledger recognition, Community Renewal ceremonies, public dashboards, ecological work programmes, and crisis mobilisations is voluntary unless a separate constitutional authority lawfully establishes a narrowly bounded public duty.**

And:

> **Refusal, withdrawal, inactivity, low recorded contribution, or a choice to remain undiscoverable may not reduce AUBI Layer 1, ordinary public rights, essential services, provider access, appeal rights, or civic standing.**

# 4. Participation modes

v1.9 moves among several forms of activity without consistently distinguishing their legal and ethical status.

v2.0 should define at least six participation modes.

## 4.1 Private and unrecorded life

Activities outside the system’s legitimate observation:

* household life;
* friendship;
* ordinary family care;
* spiritual practice;
* informal art;
* private study;
* rest;
* intimate mutual aid.

No logging or recognition is required.

## 4.2 Voluntary Love Ledger recognition

A person may choose to record or receive recognition for informal contribution.

The record:

* remains non-monetized;
* is not a work credential by default;
* is not a comprehensive contribution history;
* cannot support adverse inference;
* can be withheld, corrected, made local, or deleted subject to narrow audit needs.

AUBI v2.5 defines this Narrative Feed as voluntary and prohibits mechanical conversion into LMCI-M, LMCI-A, Hearts, fiat, or entitlement.

## 4.3 Formal Community Provider participation

A person joins a chartered provider under explicit:

* role;
* compensation;
* governance;
* safety;
* scheduling;
* data;
* grievance;
* exit rules.

This is organized work, even when intrinsically motivated.

## 4.4 Time-limited project participation

A person joins one bounded need broadcast without becoming an enduring member of the provider or creating a generalized work profile.

## 4.5 Public or collective service

A BAZ or compact commissions work required for public goods.

This needs public authorization, funding, and worker protections rather than relying on individual consumer spending.

## 4.6 Emergency participation

Voluntary emergency work operates under enhanced safety and compensation rules.

Any compulsory civic duty belongs in a separate constitutional protocol and cannot be created merely by labelling a task essential.

# 5. Community Work Teams and provider charters

## 5.1 Current architecture

Teams:

* perform care and ecological services;
* are chartered by BAZ Councils;
* elect leads;
* receive Hearts through service provision;
* fund operations, stipends, and materials;
* redeem through the Hearts Treasury.

The broad structure is coherent, but the professional and worker-rights content remains thin.

## 5.2 Provider charter requirements

v2.0 should require every Community Provider charter to specify:

* mission and permitted services;
* ownership and asset lock;
* worker membership;
* governance and removal of leaders;
* compensation method;
* financial transparency;
* working-time rules;
* safety and safeguarding;
* anti-discrimination;
* disability accommodation;
* data rights;
* intellectual and cultural property;
* conflict of interest;
* external contracting;
* complaint and appeal;
* whistleblower protection;
* exit and dissolution;
* treatment of accumulated assets;
* independent review.

## 5.3 Worker authority

BAZ oversight does not substitute for worker governance.

Workers should have rights to:

* elect and remove operational leadership;
* inspect finances;
* participate in scheduling;
* refuse unsafe tasks;
* challenge valuation;
* organize independently;
* use an advocate;
* report retaliation;
* exit without loss of earned compensation or portable credentials.

## 5.4 Governance-method pluralism

v1.9 specifies quadratic voting for team leadership.

A single voting method should not be universalized. BAZs and providers may use:

* one-person-one-vote;
* consent;
* sociocratic selection;
* sortition;
* delegated roles;
* quadratic methods for bounded preference questions.

Whatever method is used must preserve equal standing, comprehensibility, correction, and removal rights.

## 5.5 Chartering conflicts

A BAZ Council may:

* prioritize services;
* fund providers;
* charter providers;
* receive their reports;
* and respond to complaints.

These functions need partial separation.

A provider should not be judged solely by the body that:

* designed the project;
* supplied its funding;
* depends on its reported success;
* or faces political embarrassment from failure.

# 6. Need broadcasting

## 6.1 Promise

Need broadcasting addresses a real compression problem. Fixed job descriptions can obscure:

* the actual problem;
* diverse capabilities;
* partial availability;
* nonstandard experience;
* cross-disciplinary solutions.

v1.9 correctly shifts attention from “fill this role” to “address this need.”

## 6.2 A broadcast is not proof of need

Possible broadcasters include:

* affected residents;
* BAZ Councils;
* providers;
* public agencies;
* Indigenous authorities;
* ecological observers;
* funders;
* corporations.

Their claims do not have equal legitimacy.

A provider might broadcast a problem that expands its own mandate.
A funder might define a visible project rather than the community’s priority.
A BAZ might broadcast politically attractive work while suppressing inconvenient needs.
A powerful group may formulate another group’s needs without consent.

## 6.3 Need Broadcast Contract

Every funded or institutionally promoted broadcast should state:

1. broadcaster and authority;
2. affected group;
3. affected-group account and consent;
4. problem or need;
5. evidence and uncertainty;
6. competing formulations;
7. urgency;
8. public-good, private-service, ecological, or crisis classification;
9. task scope;
10. resources and funding;
11. compensation;
12. expected time, including preparation and travel;
13. hazards and safeguarding;
14. required capabilities;
15. accessibility;
16. data collected;
17. matching and discoverability rules;
18. outcome criteria;
19. stop and reassessment conditions;
20. complaint and challenge pathway.

## 6.4 Need prioritization

Prioritization should consider separately:

* severity;
* number affected;
* distributional harm;
* urgency;
* irreversibility;
* ecological significance;
* rights;
* neglected populations;
* feasibility;
* existing capacity;
* alternatives.

A single popularity score or transaction count should not decide priority.

## 6.5 Unfilled needs

An unfilled broadcast may indicate:

* insufficient skills;
* inadequate compensation;
* unsafe conditions;
* poor project design;
* inaccessible scheduling;
* lack of trust;
* unclear authority;
* an unwanted intervention;
* participant exhaustion;
* competing caregiving obligations.

It does not establish laziness or lack of community spirit.

# 7. Skills Resonance Matching

## 7.1 Current risk

v1.9 proposes replacing résumés with contribution traces drawn from Love Ledger and Proof of Care.

This solves one compression by creating another.

The résumé compresses a person into self-reported credentials.
An activity trace compresses a person into institutionally visible past behavior.

The second may be more accurate in some respects but more intrusive and path-dependent.

## 7.2 Matching must be claimant-controlled

A participant should choose:

* whether to be discoverable;
* which skills to disclose;
* which projects may contact them;
* geographic range;
* availability;
* accessibility requirements;
* whether historical work may be considered;
* whether a credential may cross BAZ boundaries;
* when the profile expires.

## 7.3 Matching profile versus reputation score

The system may store purpose-specific information such as:

* “trained in first aid”;
* “available five hours this month”;
* “comfortable with remote translation”;
* “holds a locally validated electrical credential.”

It should not infer a general score for:

* reliability;
* helpfulness;
* social value;
* community spirit;
* moral trustworthiness;
* deservingness;
* future eligibility.

## 7.4 No adverse inference from absence

A sparse profile may reflect:

* privacy;
* new arrival;
* disability;
* illness;
* informal experience;
* inaccessible systems;
* caregiving;
* discrimination;
* long absence;
* choice.

It must not automatically rank a person lower.

## 7.5 Explainable matching

The system must reveal:

* what information produced a match;
* which filters excluded someone;
* confidence and uncertainty;
* whether a human modified the result;
* how to correct the profile;
* how to opt out;
* whether the matching method changed.

The “95% match accuracy” target in v1.9 lacks a defined true outcome and should be replaced by several measures, including participant fit, retention, autonomy, correction, and subgroup access.

# 8. The 15-hour rule

## 8.1 Current ambiguity

v1.9 specifies a “15-hour cap” and gives an example of five hours with one provider and ten with another. It does not explicitly state the time period, whether the limit applies to paid work, bonus-eligible work, all organized contribution, or only Community Work Team participation.

## 8.2 The cap should be protection, not quota

v2.0 should state:

> **The default contribution ceiling limits the amount of organized work the system may routinely request or incentivize from a participant. It is not a minimum expectation, measure of citizenship, or limit on private voluntary activity.**

The framework must specify:

* period;
* covered activities;
* whether travel, preparation, administration, on-call time, and emotional debrief count;
* interaction with conventional employment;
* interaction with informal care;
* overtime;
* consent;
* rest;
* emergency exceptions;
* enforcement.

## 8.3 Voluntary additional participation

People may freely choose to contribute more, but the framework should test whether “voluntary” excess hours are driven by:

* inadequate material security;
* social pressure;
* provider understaffing;
* identity attachment;
* ecological emergency;
* lack of replacement workers;
* algorithmic reward.

Repeated excess should trigger provider-capacity review rather than praise.

## 8.4 No minimum

A person contributing zero formal hours remains fully entitled to:

* AUBI Layer 1;
* ordinary public services;
* community membership;
* privacy;
* future participation;
* respect.

# 9. Transaction demand, need, and public goods

## 9.1 Current model

Community members spend Hearts at providers, creating provider revenue.

Financial Systems v3.4 now treats Community Providers as the primary formal redemption pathway but does not define provider charters or the social worth of work.

## 9.2 Five commissioning channels

v2.0 should distinguish:

### A. Individual service purchase

The participant chooses a directly useful service and pays Hearts.

Examples:

* childcare;
* repair;
* tutoring;
* meals;
* transport assistance.

### B. Participatory public commissioning

A BAZ collectively funds a service through a budget.

Examples:

* public-space maintenance;
* preventive health;
* accessibility;
* community mediation;
* emergency preparation.

### C. Commons and ecological commissioning

A BAZ, compact, ecological authority, or GCF commissions work benefiting shared or future interests.

Examples:

* watershed restoration;
* biodiversity monitoring;
* soil recovery;
* cultural-landscape care.

### D. Solidarity or reparative funding

Work is funded because the affected population lacks purchasing power or bears a historical or ecological burden.

### E. Crisis commissioning

Time-limited emergency work with enhanced rights and oversight.

## 9.3 Why this matters

Consumer spending measures:

* ability to pay;
* access;
* awareness;
* individual preference;
* provider visibility.

It does not fully measure:

* need;
* rights;
* prevention;
* ecosystem value;
* future benefit;
* neglected populations.

## 9.4 Provider financial sustainability

“80% team financial sustainability” should not mean that every necessary provider must survive on transaction revenue.

Some providers are intentionally public, redistributive, preventive, seasonal, or crisis-reserve capacities.

The framework should assess whether the **funding model matches the service type**.

# 10. Compensation and valuation

## 10.1 Separate the flows

v1.9 sometimes combines:

* provider Hearts revenue;
* individual stipends;
* AUBI Layer 2 bonuses;
* Leaves;
* ecological outcomes;
* Love Ledger recognition.

v2.0 must separate:

1. provider operating revenue;
2. worker compensation;
3. expenses and materials;
4. AUBI Layer 2 contribution bonus;
5. Leaves ecological recognition or reward;
6. Love Ledger non-monetized recognition;
7. public commissioning;
8. Treasury redemption.

## 10.2 Fair pay is more than redemption

A fair-pay protocol should address:

* compensation floor;
* pay ratios;
* delayed and unpaid time;
* preparation and travel;
* emotional labor;
* risk;
* equipment;
* collective reserves;
* disability accommodation;
* caregiving constraints;
* transparent allocation;
* worker voice;
* portability when leaving.

## 10.3 Do not equate activity and outcome

AUBI v2.5 now requires Proof of Care to distinguish:

* activity;
* recipient or community acceptance;
* outcome;
* additionality;
* uncertainty and adverse effects.

Work in Liberation should inherit this architecture.

A patrol hour is not biodiversity recovery.
A care visit is not necessarily improved wellbeing.
A completed project is not necessarily a solved need.

## 10.4 Ecological incentives

Where ecological outcomes are partly outside worker control, compensation should not depend entirely on observed ecological recovery.

Use:

* stable base compensation for competent work;
* safety and quality standards;
* team-level outcome recognition where attribution is credible;
* uncertainty;
* long-term ecological monitoring;
* no penalty for adverse external shocks outside worker control.

# 11. Informal care and the Love Ledger

## 11.1 Current contradiction

v1.9 describes the Love Ledger as non-monetized while also making it:

* an LMCI data source;
* a contribution history;
* a matching signal;
* a validation system;
* evidence against claims that AUBI recipients are lazy.
  These functions would make participation socially consequential even without monetary convertibility.

## 11.2 Demonstrating contribution is not required

AUBI recipients do not need to rebut accusations of laziness by making private contribution visible.

The correct answer to the accusation is that material rights are not contingent on proving usefulness.

## 11.3 Revised boundary

Love Ledger records may:

* express gratitude;
* preserve voluntary community narratives;
* reveal hypotheses about unmet support needs;
* support community reflection.

They may not, by default:

* rank people;
* match people to work;
* establish competence;
* prove moral worth;
* determine benefits;
* evaluate provider productivity;
* certify LMCI-M;
* support fraud findings.

## 11.4 Informal care burden

AUBI v2.5 creates a voluntary, claimant-controlled Informal Care Burden Sentinel that observes exhaustion, coercion, health effects, lost opportunity, deprivation, and need for respite without ranking the relational act.

Work in Liberation should use that mechanism rather than treating more logged care as success.

# 12. Community Weaver independence

## 12.1 Current role bundle

The Weaver acts as:

* provider-development adviser;
* system trainer;
* participant facilitator;
* Hearts and Love Ledger guide;
* cultural intermediary;
* validator;
* community liaison.

That creates correlated evidence and conflicts of interest.

## 12.2 Permitted roles

Weavers may:

* explain options;
* support access;
* help participants articulate capacity and constraints;
* facilitate team formation;
* originate or attest evidence;
* identify unmet need;
* support complaints;
* translate protocols.

## 12.3 Prohibited role combinations

A Weaver should not alone:

* validate a claim they helped construct;
* adjudicate a complaint against a provider they helped establish;
* determine fraud;
* certify the success of their own programme;
* judge cultural legitimacy without delegated authority;
* assign a generalized participant reputation;
* decide whether refusal was reasonable;
* control both work matching and eligibility.

## 12.4 Independent worker channels

Each BAZ needs access to:

* worker advocate or ombuds;
* confidential complaint channel;
* secondary validator;
* independent provider auditor;
* appeal body outside the original decision chain;
* anti-retaliation protection.

# 13. Governance and decision rights

## 13.1 Current ambiguity

v1.9 assigns the FLP:

* global work principles;
* Green Job Score baselines;
* minimum care standards;
* standard setting;
* dispute resolution;
* AUBI bonus adjustments based on LMCI data.

It assigns the SRC:

* Hearts issuance;
* Treasury operations;
* payment disputes.

The first set is too broad and the second should defer to Financial Systems v3.4.

## 13.2 Recommended matrix

| Decision                                | Primary authority                    | Review or interface                        |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Charter local Community Provider        | BAZ Council                          | Work in Liberation standards; local appeal |
| Set provider labor and safety standards | FLP with worker participation        | BAZ adaptation; rights floor               |
| Set local service priorities            | BAZ community and Council            | Nested Economies subsidiarity rules        |
| Set compensation within provider        | Provider worker governance           | FLP floor; audit and appeal                |
| Validate LMCI-A contribution claim      | AUBI Proof of Care process           | Secondary review and claimant challenge    |
| Process Hearts payment or redemption    | Treasury under Financial Systems     | Financial rights and appeal                |
| Set Green Job Score range               | FLP proposal                         | SVDC, LEKH, ecological governance          |
| Match participant to opportunity        | Participant-controlled system        | Explainability, correction, opt-out        |
| Adjudicate labor dispute                | Independent local process / Tribunal | Not SRC or original provider alone         |
| Commission public-good work             | BAZ or authorized compact            | Participatory process; State Estimate      |
| Activate voluntary crisis mobilisation  | Authorized crisis body               | Work in Liberation safeguards              |
| Impose sanction                         | Tribunal or lawful authority         | Evidence, notice, appeal                   |

Nested Economies v3.2 already assigns labor standards, provider professions, work valuation, occupational governance, provider capacity, and worker rights to Work in Liberation, while reserving cross-BAZ recognition and economic placement to Nested Economies.

# 14. Crisis mobilisation

## 14.1 Current rule

v1.9 permits Meta-Governance to redirect Community Work Teams to essential tasks while the PIS overrides standard protocols.

That language is incompatible with a work-liberation framework unless heavily bounded.

## 14.2 Voluntary mobilisation default

A crisis does not automatically remove:

* refusal;
* informed consent;
* disability accommodation;
* caregiving constraints;
* safety standards;
* compensation;
* rest;
* exit;
* conscientious objection;
* appeal.

## 14.3 Emergency Work Mobilisation Record

A crisis mobilisation should specify:

1. lawful declaration;
2. condition and evidence;
3. authorized body;
4. affected area;
5. essential task;
6. why ordinary capacity is insufficient;
7. voluntary recruitment method;
8. compensation;
9. insurance and safety;
10. training;
11. maximum duration and hours;
12. disability and caregiving accommodation;
13. refusal and withdrawal;
14. conscientious objection;
15. data collected;
16. independent monitoring;
17. reassessment;
18. demobilisation;
19. restoration and after-action review.

## 14.4 Compulsory service

Any mandatory civic service would require a separate constitutional basis defining:

* necessity;
* proportionality;
* democratic authorization;
* protected exemptions;
* duration;
* rights;
* compensation;
* judicial review.

Work in Liberation should not create such authority indirectly.

## 14.5 Financial compatibility

Financial Systems v3.4 permits crisis bodies to accelerate essential-provider liquidity and redirect discretionary project issuance, while protecting Layer 1 absent a separately authorized constitutional emergency.

Work in Liberation should operate inside that boundary.

# 15. Industrial Sunsetting

## 15.1 Current architecture

The framework phases out harmful industries through Treaty Ecocide law, transition funding, retraining, and enforcement.

The principle is sound but the mechanism is compressed.

## 15.2 Workers are not the harmful industry

The framework must separate:

* corporate and ownership responsibility;
* capital allocation;
* political obstruction;
* harmful production process;
* worker livelihood;
* community dependence;
* useful capabilities embedded in the sector.

Workers should not be required to prove ecological virtue to retain material support.

## 15.3 Sunsetting State Estimate

Before a major phase-out decision, publish:

* ecological harm;
* uncertainty;
* affected ecosystems;
* alternative pathways;
* affected workers and communities;
* transferable capabilities;
* regional fiscal dependence;
* critical products currently supplied;
* replacement capacity;
* transition-fund adequacy;
* sequencing;
* distributional burden;
* Indigenous and local evidence;
* outcome and reversal conditions.

## 15.4 Sequencing

A responsible sequence may include:

1. stop public subsidy and expansion;
2. establish worker and community transition bodies;
3. fund material security and retraining;
4. build replacement capacity;
5. transfer useful infrastructure;
6. phase down operations;
7. remediate damage;
8. evaluate worker and ecological outcomes.

## 15.5 Success metrics

“Worker retraining success” is insufficient.

Observe:

* income stability;
* quality and durability of new work;
* autonomy;
* geographic displacement;
* health;
* community fiscal stability;
* skill use;
* worker consent;
* ecosystem recovery;
* unpaid transition burden.

# 16. Blue Stewards and enforcement-adjacent work

## 16.1 Current design

The Marine Stewardship Corps combines:

* ecological monitoring;
* physical presence and deterrence;
* restoration;
* sensor evidence;
* public data streaming;
* financial sanctions against alleged violators.

This is not merely a work programme. It is also an enforcement, evidence, surveillance, and geopolitical architecture.

## 16.2 Automatic sanctions are inadmissible

The source states that evidence triggers automatic Blacklist Protocol sanctions.

Sensor evidence may justify:

* preservation of evidence;
* alert;
* temporary narrowly bounded containment;
* investigation.

It does not by itself establish:

* vessel identity;
* unlawful intent;
* jurisdiction;
* evidentiary completeness;
* sanction proportionality.

Financial Systems v3.4 requires automatic controls to remain narrow, reversible, versioned, and challengeable, with permanent intervention separated from temporary containment.

## 16.3 Evidence pipeline

Enforcement evidence should not be published through the Love Ledger.

It requires a dedicated pipeline with:

* chain of custody;
* source provenance;
* sensor reliability;
* human review;
* uncertainty;
* classified or safety-sensitive handling;
* affected-party notice;
* Tribunal admissibility;
* correction;
* appeal.

## 16.4 Worker protection

Blue Stewards need explicit:

* hazard classification;
* maritime safety;
* no-combat boundary;
* withdrawal rights;
* trauma support;
* insurance;
* rescue capacity;
* protected whistleblowing;
* no compensation incentive to seek confrontation;
* no individual liability for lawful refusal.

## 16.5 Activity and outcome

Patrol hours can justify operating compensation. Biomass recovery cannot automatically be attributed to a crew because it may also reflect:

* migration;
* weather;
* enforcement by others;
* ecological lag;
* measurement change;
* reduced extraction elsewhere.

The Proof of Care distinctions should apply.

## 16.6 Framework placement

The Blue Stewards concept may remain as a flagship case study, but the legal, evidentiary, sanctions, maritime, and ecological-verification architecture should reside in dedicated Treaty, Justice, Ocean, and Technology protocols.

# 18. Indistinguishable-state tests

## Test 1: Low formal participation

Possible states:

* genuine preference for private life;
* illness or recovery;
* strong informal contribution;
* inaccessible opportunities;
* poor matching;
* distrust;
* discrimination;
* caregiving burden;
* sufficient material and relational life;
* social exclusion.

**Verdict:** Low participation is not evidence of apathy, low meaning, or policy failure.

## Test 2: High participation

Possible states:

* joyful contribution;
* inadequate AUBI;
* Hearts usability problems;
* provider understaffing;
* social pressure;
* identity dependence;
* crisis;
* bonus gaming;
* lack of boundaries.

**Verdict:** High participation is not evidence of liberation.

## Test 3: High Love Ledger activity

Possible states:

* rich voluntary recognition;
* pressure to log contribution;
* institutional campaigns;
* competitive gratitude;
* duplicated records;
* surveillance;
* migration from unrecorded care into visible care.

**Verdict:** More records are not proof of stronger relationships.

## Test 4: High matching rate

Possible states:

* effective matching;
* low participant choice;
* broad matching criteria;
* provider desperation;
* repeated low-quality referrals;
* suppressed opt-out;
* homogeneous participant pool.

**Verdict:** Match completion must be separated from fit, autonomy, and outcome.

## Test 5: Unfilled essential work

Possible states:

* skill shortage;
* inadequate compensation;
* hazard;
* poor design;
* lack of equipment;
* inaccessible schedule;
* low trust;
* incorrect need formulation;
* genuine unwillingness.

**Verdict:** Diagnose the work system before pressuring participants.

## Test 6: High provider revenue

Possible states:

* valuable service;
* monopoly;
* essentiality;
* high price;
* poor public alternative;
* provider capture;
* transaction concentration.

**Verdict:** Revenue is not sufficient evidence of social value or good worker conditions.

## Test 7: High satisfaction

Possible states:

* genuine satisfaction;
* fear of retaliation;
* community conformity;
* selection bias;
* exit by dissatisfied people;
* dependence on the evaluator.

**Verdict:** Satisfaction requires anonymous, independent, subgroup-aware interpretation.

## Test 8: Rapid crisis deployment

Possible states:

* strong preparedness;
* permanent understaffing;
* normalized emergency;
* coerced redirection;
* low safety threshold;
* repeated failure to build ordinary capacity.

**Verdict:** Deployment speed is not sufficient evidence of resilience.

## Test 9: Successful industrial transition

Possible states:

* durable autonomous work;
* short training followed by unemployment;
* geographic displacement;
* lower-quality work;
* hidden income loss;
* ecological improvement with community collapse.

**Verdict:** Retraining completion is not transition success.

# 24. Final assessment

Work in Liberation’s deepest contribution is not that it invents a better labor market.

It is that it begins from a more radical premise:

> **A person must be materially free not to work before organized contribution can become meaningfully voluntary.**

That premise survives the review.

But material freedom is necessary, not sufficient.

A person can be free from an employer and still be constrained by:

* community expectation;
* public recognition;
* algorithmic discoverability;
* contribution records;
* provider dependency;
* moral narratives;
* emergency mobilisation;
* the need to demonstrate usefulness.

The governing principle for v2.0 should therefore be:

> **Work is liberated only when participation, refusal, privacy, exit, and return are all materially and socially credible.**

The need-broadcasting principle should be:

> **A need broadcast is an accountable invitation to collective attention—not a command, a diagnosis, or proof that a particular person should respond.**

The matching principle should be:

> **The system may help people find opportunities; it may not convert their lives into a generalized record of employability, reliability, or moral worth.**

The measurement principle should be:

> **More work, more transactions, more logged care, more rapid matching, and more visible contribution do not by themselves demonstrate greater freedom or flourishing.**

And the framework’s strongest constitutional test should be:

> **Could a person decline every formal opportunity, keep their private contributions unrecorded, retain full material security and civic standing, and later return without penalty?**

Where the answer is yes, Work in Liberation is approaching its stated purpose.

Where the answer is no, work has been reorganized—but not yet liberated.
