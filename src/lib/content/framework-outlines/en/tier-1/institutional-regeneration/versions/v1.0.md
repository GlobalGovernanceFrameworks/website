## 1. Purpose

Institutions can outlive the conditions that created them. They can also retain indispensable knowledge, legal duties, public trust, trained staff, operational networks, and services that cannot be replaced casually.

The purpose of this framework is to help institutions ask, with evidence and humility:

- What mandate do we actually hold?
- What purpose are we behaviourally serving?
- Which selection pressures reproduce that revealed purpose?
- Which functions remain necessary?
- Which communities and beneficiaries depend on us?
- Where are authority, causal control, resources, knowledge, and accountability misaligned?
- What can be improved through bounded internal pilots, and what is too structurally constrained for a meaningful pilot?
- What requires charter amendment or member-state action?
- Which programmes should continue, receive capacity, change incentives, redistribute authority, transfer, merge, redesign, or close?
- How can transition protect workers, beneficiaries, records, rights, and unresolved duties?

The framework provides methods, records, and tool sequences. It does not decide the answers.

> **An audit is not an amendment. A score is not a mandate. An Evolution Cell is not a constituent assembly. Funding is not consent. A successful pilot is not authority to scale. A closure proposal is not dissolution. Conformance is not regeneration.**

## 2. Constitutional position

The Institutional Regeneration Framework is not a superior layer above the institution being reformed.

It does not:

- rewrite a treaty, charter, statute, constitution, labour agreement, budget, licence, or judicial order;
- create voting shares, vetoes, public offices, stakeholder councils, or future-generation guardians;
- appoint representatives for affected Indigenous nations;
- direct member states;
- confer eligibility or voting rights in the Global Commons Fund;
- dismiss or redeploy staff;
- terminate pensions, grants, contracts, licences, benefits, or cases;
- dissolve an institution or programme;
- transfer assets, liabilities, data, archives, jurisdiction, or emergency powers;
- authorize surveillance, political pressure, sanctions, or coercive action.

It may support an institution in preparing those matters for the competent authority.

### 2.1 Canonical doctrine

> **Institutional diagnosis may justify reconsideration. It does not create reform authority. Institutional success may justify a new proposal. It does not expand jurisdiction. Institutional failure may justify protection, audit, remedy, or lawful transition. It does not erase duties.**

### 2.2 Methods, authority, and consequence profiles

Every IRF tool must state one of the `GMEAIA/0.2.1` authority-effect labels and select a justified `CP0`–`CP3` consequence profile. The profile determines the minimum semantic functions required; it does not require a separate form for every IRF record.

Most IRF tools are:

- `none`: educational, simulated, reflective, or descriptive;
- `advisory`: analyses and recommendations;
- `record_support`: helps a competent authority prepare a decision;
- `voluntary_internal`: a bounded practice affecting consenting staff or members within existing authority.

The framework uses GMEAIA's core-plus-trigger proportionality rule:

- `CP0` for exploratory and non-consequential activity;
- `CP1` for voluntary bounded practice;
- `CP2` for operational or consequential action;
- `CP3` for protected-domain or constitutional action under the controlling specialized interface.

A low-risk process shall not be burdened with closure-grade documentation. A technically simple action affecting protected rights or essential services shall not escape `CP3` merely because implementation is easy.

No IRF tool independently has:

- `public_law_effect`;
- `charter_amendment_effect`;
- `funding_award_effect`;
- `employment_effect`;
- `dissolution_effect`;
- `emergency_effect`;
- `coercive_effect`.

## 3. Core principles

### Mission before institutional self-preservation

An institution should not exist solely to reproduce itself. But mission review must distinguish institutional inertia from political underfunding, inherited structural constraints, impossible mandates, external shocks, and deliberate obstruction.

Regeneration must examine not only the institution's stated mission but the selection pressures that reproduce its revealed purpose: budget, appointment, promotion, legal exposure, political blame, information control, professional prestige, continuation incentives, and closure costs.

### Charter fidelity and lawful change

A mission may need reinterpretation or amendment. The path must be the institution's valid amendment process—not framework adoption, facilitator consensus, audit recommendation, donor condition, or public popularity.

### Causal subsidiarity with constituted responsibility

Decisions should be taken at the lowest effective level that actually possesses authority, practical capacity, resources, and control over the material causes. Internal decentralization cannot silently transfer member-state, ministerial, board, judicial, fiduciary, or public powers.

Affected-party standing creates participation, consent, challenge, and remedy rights. It does not make the affected party responsible for causes controlled elsewhere. Where causal control is relational, authority may be jointly constituted rather than presumed local or transferred upward.

### Workers and beneficiaries are not implementation variables

Staff, unions, contractors, pensioners, grantees, users, and dependent communities have rights and reliance interests. Efficiency metrics do not erase them.

### Affected Indigenous nations speak for themselves

A global council, generic elder, stakeholder quota, or framework-appointed representative cannot consent, designate sacredness, or waive rights for the specific affected nation.

### Learning without exposure

Institutions should learn from failure, dissent, and reform resistance. Learning systems must protect whistleblowers, staff, beneficiaries, confidential diplomacy, sealed evidence, protected knowledge, and contested allegations.

### Funding without constitutional capture

Financial support may encourage experimentation. It must not purchase charter changes, voting rights, silence, ideology, or the abandonment of essential functions.

### Graceful obsolescence through lawful continuity

Ending a programme can be healthy. Closure is responsible only when authority, services, workers, beneficiaries, assets, liabilities, records, cases, and remedies have a lawful destination. Complexity and unresolved duties justify continuity planning; they do not automatically justify preserving the institutional shell.

### Duty attached to retained jurisdiction

A competent authority that retains jurisdiction should, where law, charter, compact, or voluntary commitment establishes a response duty, acknowledge, accept, refuse, defer, or refer a consequential proposal within a decision-appropriate period. Delay or refusal does not transfer authority automatically, but it must remain visible with reasons, clocks, escalation, and continuing responsibility.

### Proportional process and conformance humility

Record depth shall be proportionate to consequence. Completion of IRF or GMEAIA records establishes traceability and constitutional discipline; it does not establish institutional legitimacy, mission success, causal efficacy, or regeneration.

## 5. Module 1 — Purpose and mandate review

### 5.1 Constituting-instrument baseline

Before assessing relevance, the institution must establish what it is legally and organizationally required to do.

The baseline includes:

- constituting treaty, charter, statute, resolution, or articles;
- delegated powers and limits;
- member-state or governing-body competences;
- financing and budget rules;
- employment and labour instruments;
- beneficiaries and statutory service obligations;
- oversight, audit, judicial, and appeal bodies;
- amendment, suspension, merger, transfer, and dissolution procedures;
- active emergency or exceptional powers;
- pending legal obligations.

The baseline prevents a performance discussion from redefining the mandate by implication.

### 5.2 Purpose, Mandate, and Objective-Reproduction Audit

The audit examines:

1. stated mandate;
2. revealed operational purpose;
3. continuing need;
4. performance and distributional effects;
5. mandate–capacity fit;
6. causal control and external constraints;
7. duplication and complementarity;
8. capture and political constraints;
9. beneficiary dependency;
10. alternatives and lawful reform pathways;
11. selection pressures reproducing current behavior;
12. incentives affecting the audit, reform team, funders, and governing authority;
13. conditions under which the previous behavior would reappear after reform.

The audit must distinguish:

- evidence from opinion;
- institutional performance from environmental conditions;
- mission failure from resource deprivation;
- internal dysfunction from external veto or obstruction;
- observed outcomes from causal attribution;
- public dissatisfaction from legal illegitimacy;
- affected-party standing from responsibility for controlling causes;
- process conformance from durable regeneration.

### 5.3 Reform classes

Every material reform proposal shall identify which class or classes it belongs to:

- **symptom reform:** changes current behavior while the main selection pressures remain;
- **capacity reform:** improves resources, skills, infrastructure, or operational ability under the current mandate;
- **incentive reform:** changes rewards, penalties, funding, promotion, appointment, exposure, or accountability;
- **authority reform:** changes who may decide, block, inspect, fund, or compel;
- **reproductive reform:** changes the conditions that cause the institution's characteristic behavior to return after leadership, policy, or pilot changes.

A cultural, procedural, or communications intervention shall not be described as reproductive reform unless evidence shows how it changes the relevant selection environment.

### 5.4 Causal Constraint and Responsibility Map

For each material performance constraint, the audit shall identify:

- the variable or condition;
- its legal controller;
- its practical controller;
- the institution capable of changing it;
- resources and authority presently available;
- whether that actor benefits from the current condition;
- burdens currently assigned to staff, local offices, beneficiaries, or partners;
- requested upstream action;
- response-duty source and clocks;
- escalation, joint constitution, or constitutional-gap route;
- limits on attribution to the audited institution.

The map may find `retained`, `delegated`, `transferred`, `concurrent`, `jointly_constituted`, or `absent` authority formation through GMEAIA-20. It does not itself alter jurisdiction.

### 5.5 Impossible Mandate Notice

An institution may issue an Impossible Mandate Notice where its valid duties cannot be discharged with the authority, resources, time, information, or external cooperation lawfully available.

The notice shall state:

- duty and expected outcome;
- missing authority, resource, or cooperation;
- contradictory obligations;
- external controller or veto;
- expected harm;
- competent corrective authority;
- support already requested or offered;
- response and action clocks;
- whether the gap is capacity-limited or jurisdictionally constitutional.

The notice does not erase continuing duties. It prevents responsibility from being assigned downward merely because the consequences appear inside the institution.

### 5.6 Mandate Relevance and Reform Option Protocol

The protocol may classify questions as:

- mandate remains relevant;
- mandate relevant but under-resourced;
- mandate relevant but structurally blocked;
- mandate relevant but selection pressures misaligned;
- mandate ambiguous and needs clarification;
- functions overlap and need coordination;
- symptom or capacity reform should be considered;
- incentive or authority reform should be considered;
- transfer, merger, closure, or dissolution should be considered;
- insufficient evidence.

It cannot declare the institution redundant, illegitimate, dissolved, or stripped of authority.

### 5.7 Purpose Clarification Process

A facilitated process may include:

- governing bodies;
- member representatives;
- staff and unions;
- beneficiaries;
- affected communities;
- affected Indigenous nations;
- technical and legal experts;
- youth or future-oriented perspectives;
- civil society and partner institutions.

Participation does not create voting rights or charter standing. The process must preserve dissent, identify who actually decides, and disclose which actors control the conditions under discussion.

### 5.8 Reform Pacing and Response-Time Diagnostic

The diagnostic advises on:

- legal complexity;
- political consent;
- staff capacity;
- budget cycles;
- service dependency;
- data and technology;
- labour relations;
- conflict and retaliation;
- change fatigue;
- reversibility;
- deterioration timescale;
- maximum tolerable audit and decision delay;
- acknowledgment, decision, and post-acceptance action clocks;
- interim protection where lawful process is slower than the condition.

It cannot label an institution “high readiness” and thereby justify faster intervention, funding preference, or bypass of procedure. A clock must cite its legal, charter, compact, voluntary, or GMEAIA-07 basis; urgency does not manufacture jurisdiction.

## 6. Module 2 — Governance renewal

### 6.1 Governance blueprints are options

The framework may provide templates for:

- youth advisory councils;
- citizen assemblies;
- affected-community panels;
- Indigenous liaison or co-governance structures;
- future-generation representation;
- consent-based operational teams;
- stakeholder review boards;
- independent ombuds and audit bodies.

A blueprint is not a constituted body.

Every proposed body must define:

- constituency;
- selection or appointment;
- mandate;
- voting, advisory, review, or veto effect;
- relation to existing authorities;
- fiduciary and confidentiality duties;
- conflicts and recusal;
- budget and staff support;
- removal and succession;
- review and expiry.

Fixed shares such as 30% voting power and universal veto rules are treated as examples requiring local derivation, not defaults.

### 6.2 Charter amendment

A reform requiring changes to:

- membership;
- voting;
- vetoes;
- governing organs;
- mandate;
- jurisdiction;
- budget authority;
- appointment powers;
- legal personality;
- dissolution;

must proceed through `IRF-18` and the institution's lawful amendment route.

The IRF may prepare options and records. `IRF-19` can only import the resulting lawful decision.

### 6.3 Evolution Cells

An Evolution Cell is an internal reform-support team.

It may:

- coordinate the audit;
- gather evidence;
- convene dialogues;
- propose pilots;
- maintain implementation records;
- identify risks;
- support learning and handoffs.

It may not:

- amend the charter;
- bind member states or governing bodies;
- create councils or vetoes;
- reallocate budgets without authority;
- dismiss or discipline staff;
- compel data;
- publish protected allegations;
- close programmes;
- represent beneficiaries or affected nations without mandate.

Its charter must include term, appointment, resources, access, independence, conflicts, complaints, removal, succession, dependency-reduction, and a terminal condition. An Evolution Cell shall not be rewarded solely for continuing reform activity or treated as successful because it produces additional audits, workshops, or records.

### 6.4 Whistleblowers and internal reformers

Protected reporting is separated into:

1. confidential intake;
2. immediate anti-retaliation and safety support;
3. evidence preservation;
4. jurisdictional referral;
5. independent investigation;
6. findings and response;
7. remedy;
8. learning.

A report is not proof. A failure to prove wrongdoing is not proof of bad faith.

### 6.5 Anti-capture

Anti-capture reviews should examine:

- donor and member-state concentration;
- procurement and revolving doors;
- private partnerships;
- agenda control;
- informal vetoes;
- staff retaliation;
- consultant dependence;
- data and vendor capture;
- audit independence;
- representation gaps.

Influence maps must use access controls and cannot become retaliation lists, political intelligence products, or public rankings of alleged blockers.

Anti-capture review shall also test common-mode dependence among auditors, facilitators, funders, consultants, AI systems, vendors, methods, and professional networks. Formal organizational separation does not establish independent observation.

### 6.6 Competent-authority response and omission

A material audit finding, charter proposal, funding referral, or transition recommendation shall identify the competent recipient and use GMEAIA-08 or GMEAIA-59 for exact response states and clocks.

The record shall distinguish:

- no response;
- lawful refusal;
- deferment;
- referral;
- `capacity_limited` acceptance;
- misrouting;
- `constitutional_gap`;
- acceptance followed by performance omission.

No timeout transfers jurisdiction automatically. Where the recipient accepts responsibility but misses the action clock, the omission remains open through implementation, challenge, and remedy records.

### 6.7 Evidence-access status

Evidence shall be classified as:

- available;
- legally protected;
- operationally inaccessible;
- refused by a named authority;
- destroyed or missing;
- disputed;
- outside jurisdiction.

Denied access is relevant to the correction architecture but is not proof of the underlying allegation. Audit findings shall state how each access condition limits inference.

### 6.8 Adoption Integrity Statement

Any public claim of IRF use shall disclose:

- consequence profile;
- modules and records used;
- modules excluded;
- whether power and capture were reviewed;
- whether causal responsibility and selection pressures were examined;
- whether structural or authority change occurred;
- unresolved findings and recipient responses;
- expiry and closure status.

An institution shall not represent workshops, storytelling, dashboard publication, or selective low-risk pilots as full institutional regeneration.

## 7. Module 3 — Culture, leadership, and internal practice

### 7.1 Internal culture experiments

Institutions may pilot:

- cross-silo teams;
- innovation jams;
- reverse mentoring;
- improved meeting methods;
- relational-integrity practices;
- trauma-aware facilitation;
- reflective spaces;
- leadership transitions;
- staff learning.

These are voluntary-internal tools unless separately authorized.

They must not become a substitute for incentive, authority, budget, workload, procurement, or charter reform where those are the material causes.

They must not:

- replace clinical or occupational-health services;
- compel spiritual, therapeutic, or ideological participation;
- create unofficial personnel evaluations;
- expose trauma histories;
- affect promotion, security clearance, or belonging through non-participation.

### 7.2 Recruitment and tours of duty

External experts, liaisons, fellows, and secondees require:

- lawful appointment or contract;
- defined purpose and term;
- access limits;
- security and confidentiality;
- conflicts and funding disclosure;
- compensation and labour protections;
- supervision;
- liability;
- removal;
- knowledge and data exit.

A liaison supports the host institution. It does not acquire command or charter authority.

### 7.3 Resistance analysis

The source archetypes “Bureaucratic Defender” and “Agile Champion” are retained only as workshop prompts.

They cannot be stored as personnel classifications or used for:

- discipline;
- promotion;
- funding;
- surveillance;
- reputational pressure;
- exclusion.

Resistance may reflect self-interest, but also legal duty, professional caution, labour rights, institutional memory, or valid disagreement.

### 7.4 Public communication

Counter-disinformation and legitimacy-renewal tools must distinguish:

- factual correction;
- explanation of reform;
- public deliberation;
- voluntary storytelling;
- advocacy;
- political pressure.

IRF tools cannot authorize behavioural profiling, covert persuasion, public shaming of member states or staff, retaliatory narrative campaigns, or sanctions.

## 8. Module 4 — Funding and budgeting

### 8.1 Funding is separate from reform authority

A Global Commons Fund or other donor may choose to support reform. Funding cannot make a charter amendment valid or an internal pilot active.

The financial chain is:

> application → criteria review → award → agreement → payment authorization → disbursement → procurement → audit → possible suspension or clawback → appeal → closure

### 8.2 Prohibited funding conditions

Funding conditions may not require:

- surrender of statutory independence;
- adoption of MOS or another philosophy;
- fixed voting shares or veto bodies without lawful amendment;
- silence about harm;
- waiver of labour, beneficiary, Indigenous, or human rights;
- transfer of protected data;
- automatic programme closure;
- GGF control of appointments;
- public storytelling;
- ideological conformity.

### 8.3 Funding independence and objective reproduction

Funding review shall examine whether the funder, auditor, consultant, facilitator, vendor, or institution benefits more from continued regeneration activity than from durable resolution. It shall disclose concentration, repeat-provider dependence, exit costs, and whether essential participation support is available without adopting the preferred method.

A funded process should state how local capability, ordinary budget ownership, and support withdrawal will be handled. Continued grant activity is not evidence of institutional regeneration.

### 8.4 Tiered funding

Bronze, Silver, and Gold labels may describe support packages.

They do not automatically:

- determine institutional worth;
- create voting rights in the fund;
- shorten due process;
- trigger public rankings;
- establish eligibility;
- authorize payment;
- pressure incremental adopters through unsafe withdrawal.

Any voting right in a fund must be created through that fund's own lawful constitution.

### 8.5 Essential-function and transition protection

Before reducing or ending support, the funder and institution must assess:

- essential services;
- dependent beneficiaries;
- staff and pensions;
- grants and contracts;
- abrupt safety or humanitarian effects;
- alternative finance;
- transition time;
- appeal and interim support.

### 8.6 Impact-linked budgeting

Outcome-oriented budgeting may improve focus, but metrics must not become automatic payment or closure engines.

A valid model must address:

- budget authority;
- causal uncertainty;
- multiple objectives;
- metric gaming;
- distributional effects;
- long-term and qualitative outcomes;
- worker and beneficiary incentives;
- correction and audit;
- exceptional events;
- right to explain variance.

Blockchain or other ledgers may preserve records. They do not validate causal claims or budget authority.

## 9. Module 5 — Narrative and legitimacy renewal

Narrative work may help an institution explain its role, acknowledge failure, and rebuild trust.

It must not convert:

- favourable stories into proof of effectiveness;
- critical stories into disloyalty;
- low trust into loss of legal mandate;
- communication metrics into funding or discipline;
- dissent into disinformation;
- ceremony into legal closure.

### 9.1 Regeneration Spotlights

A Spotlight requires:

- consent and attribution choice;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- protection of staff and beneficiaries;
- no retaliation;
- correction and withdrawal;
- separation from evaluation and funding.

### 9.2 Public Mirror

A Public Mirror may publish aggregate, privacy-preserving indicators and public explanations.

It must not publish:

- small-team sentiment;
- protected personnel information;
- unverified allegations;
- political orientation;
- member-state “obstruction” scores;
- whistleblower identity;
- sealed or diplomatic records.

### 9.3 Ceremonies

Leadership transitions and programme endings may include secular, spiritual, interfaith, ancestral, or cultural ceremony where participants choose it.

Ceremony does not constitute appointment, amendment, transfer, or dissolution.

## 10. Regeneration Compact and pilot pathway

### Phase 0 — Exploration

The institution may review public materials, join peer learning, and run simulations with no consequential effect.

### Phase 1 — Baseline and audit

Records `IRF-01` through `IRF-11` establish scope, authority, affected interests, evidence, causal responsibility, selection pressures, response times, and options. The selected `CP0`–`CP3` profile determines which semantic functions are required.

### Phase 2 — Internal pilots

A competent authority may authorize bounded pilots using:

- `IRF-12` through `IRF-27`;
- `GMEAIA/0.2.1`;
- EGP v1.1.1 adoption records.

### Phase 3 — Evaluation and new proposals

Audit and learning may support:

- continuation;
- amendment;
- wider pilot;
- charter amendment;
- funding request;
- programme transition.

Success never activates the next phase automatically. A pilot shall be no larger than necessary for safety and no smaller than necessary to expose the causal mechanism it claims to test. Acceptability, feasibility, mechanism, operational, system-effect, and institutionalization pilots shall not be treated as interchangeable.

### Phase 4 — Lawful institutional change

Charter, budget, employment, programme, merger, transfer, or dissolution decisions are made only by the competent authorities.

### Regeneration Compact

The Compact must state whether it is:

- expressive;
- non-binding MOU;
- internal administrative agreement;
- grant agreement;
- contract;
- treaty-level instrument.

Its title cannot obscure its legal effect. Where the Compact creates a response duty, shared function, or jointly constituted authority, it must state the source, formation type, clocks, affected non-parties, exit, and surviving duties.

## 11. Audits, metrics, and AI

### 11.1 Regeneration Audit Council

An audit body must have a lawful appointment or contractual basis and record:

- members and selection;
- competence;
- funding;
- conflicts;
- evidence access;
- confidentiality;
- affected-party participation;
- minority reports;
- correction;
- appeal;
- term and removal.

The term “Council” does not itself create public authority.

Auditor independence shall include common-mode review of funders, appointment authorities, data, methods, AI, vendors, professional networks, and reputational incentives. Minority reports alone do not cure a shared epistemic substrate.

### 11.2 Audit outputs

Audit outputs must distinguish:

- verified facts;
- disputed facts;
- estimates;
- model results;
- causal inferences;
- stakeholder views;
- recommendations;
- unresolved uncertainty.

Audit findings shall also distinguish symptom, capacity, incentive, authority, and reproductive reform; identify denied-access conditions; and name the competent recipient, response clocks, and unresolved omission.

No score may automatically trigger:

- funding;
- withdrawal;
- dismissal;
- charter change;
- programme transfer;
- public shaming;
- dissolution.

### 11.3 Metrics

Adoption counts, module completion, staff sentiment, trust, speed, partnerships, resource efficiency, and public stories are diagnostic inputs.

They must not be treated as a universal Regeneration Index or legitimacy score. Record completion and constitutional conformance are diagnostic facts, not evidence that the institutional objective changed.

### 11.4 AI

AI may support:

- document comparison;
- mandate mapping;
- translation;
- anomaly detection;
- scenario simulation;
- drafting;
- missing-field checks.

It cannot autonomously decide:

- mandate relevance;
- political obstruction;
- employee resistance;
- funding eligibility;
- dismissal;
- charter amendment;
- programme closure;
- institutional dissolution;
- successor custody.

Every system needs a responsible owner, version, data record, prohibited uses, human review, correction, rollback, and expiry.

## 12. Institutional transition and closure

### 12.1 Transition options

A review may propose:

- continuation;
- internal reform;
- mandate clarification;
- charter amendment;
- programme redesign;
- partnership;
- transfer;
- merger;
- temporary pause;
- programme closure;
- institutional dissolution.

Each option requires a distinct authority and transition record.

### 12.2 Essential-function continuity

Before transition, identify:

- statutory and humanitarian functions;
- beneficiaries and access needs;
- dependencies;
- minimum service levels;
- surge or emergency functions;
- funding and staffing;
- successor capacity;
- failure contingencies.

No celebration of “graceful obsolescence” may conceal service abandonment. Conversely, unresolved duties that can be assigned to lawful custodians shall not be used automatically to preserve an obsolete institutional shell.

### 12.3 Workers and pensions

Transition planning must address:

- unions and collective bargaining;
- notice and consultation;
- redeployment;
- severance;
- pensions and accrued benefits;
- contractors and consultants;
- disability and accommodation;
- visas and immigration status;
- safety and psychosocial support;
- claims and appeal.

### 12.4 Assets and liabilities

Inventory:

- land and facilities;
- funds and reserves;
- intellectual property;
- contracts and procurement;
- grants and subgrants;
- debts and guarantees;
- licences and permits;
- pending cases;
- investigations;
- insurance;
- data and systems;
- records and archives.

A framework record does not transfer or extinguish any item.

### 12.5 Dark Archive

The Dark Archive becomes a **Records and Continuity Custody Plan**.

A neutral repository may preserve records only under:

- originating authority;
- access classes;
- retention and deletion;
- sealed-evidence rules;
- diplomatic and security protections;
- protected Indigenous knowledge;
- successor access;
- correction;
- audit.

Hosting in a stable jurisdiction does not give the host ownership or adjudicative authority.

### 12.6 Lawful dissolution

A dissolution decision must come from the competent source:

- treaty or charter process;
- member states;
- governing body;
- legislature;
- court;
- insolvency or other applicable law.

The IRF cannot issue the decision.

### 12.7 Successor custody

A successor record assigns:

- continuing functions;
- assets and liabilities;
- staff and pensions;
- contracts and grants;
- records and data;
- pending cases and investigations;
- remedies;
- public communications;
- residual review.

Temporary or emergency authority ends unless separately and lawfully reconstituted. Successor design shall test whether the same selection pressures, common-mode dependencies, and responsibility gaps would simply reproduce the predecessor's behavior.

### 12.8 Closure

Closure requires:

- authority returned;
- access credentials ended;
- temporary bodies dissolved;
- data disposition completed;
- workers and beneficiaries addressed;
- assets and liabilities transferred lawfully;
- public reasons and corrections;
- unresolved duties assigned;
- reopening route for remedy.

> **Dissolution ends the institution. It does not erase what the institution owes.**

## 13. Crisis and conflict-zone use

Regeneration Rapid Response Packages and Liaisons are support tools, not emergency command.

They may:

- organize information;
- support continuity planning;
- offer facilitation;
- help prepare an EGP signal or CDEE referral;
- support a lawfully accepted mission.

They cannot:

- declare an emergency;
- compel access or data;
- override governing bodies;
- remove officials;
- requisition assets;
- establish checkpoints;
- conduct intelligence;
- search, seize, detain, use force, or impose sanctions.

Emergency and coercive actions remain governed by ECRC, CDEE, substantive frameworks, and SCPA.

## 20. Theory of change and framework-level falsification

### 20.1 Theory of change

The framework assumes that institutional regeneration is more likely when an institution:

1. establishes its lawful mandate and continuing duties;
2. distinguishes revealed purpose from stated purpose;
3. identifies causal constraints and selection pressures;
4. protects dissent, workers, beneficiaries, affected nations, and evidence;
5. tests proportionate and causally adequate reforms;
6. routes structural changes to competent authorities with visible response clocks;
7. changes capacity, incentives, authority, or reproductive conditions where required;
8. evaluates durable behavior and ordinary ownership rather than record completion;
9. closes or transfers obsolete functions without abandoning unresolved duties.

### 20.2 Framework hypotheses

The framework places the following claims at risk:

- purpose and mandate audits identify material objective drift missed by ordinary governance;
- reproductive reforms persist more reliably than symptom reforms where selection pressures caused the failure;
- Evolution Cells improve learning without becoming shadow authorities or permanent reform bureaucracies;
- constitutional separation prevents overreach without making reform impractically slow;
- funding supports capability without purchasing conformity or dependency;
- responsible continuity allows obsolete institutions to close rather than preserving them indefinitely;
- worker and beneficiary protection improves legitimacy and durability;
- the record architecture adds more traceability than administrative burden.

### 20.3 Disconfirming evidence

Evidence against the framework includes:

- audits largely restate known problems or avoid consequential causal findings;
- institutions with completed records show no durable behavior change;
- Evolution Cells become symbolic, agenda-controlling, or self-perpetuating;
- response and authorization chains routinely miss reform windows;
- funded institutions converge on donor-preferred methods or remain dependent on support;
- continuity provisions systematically prevent closure without improving protection;
- low-capacity institutions cannot use the framework without specialist mediation;
- direct lawful reform outperforms the IRF pathway with less burden or delay.

### 20.4 Narrowing, suspension, replacement, and non-scaling

A module or record profile should be narrowed, suspended, replaced, or not scaled where:

- its administrative burden exceeds its decision value;
- it creates common-mode dependence or retaliation risk;
- it does not change decisions or outcomes;
- its causal theory is repeatedly disconfirmed;
- it is usable mainly by already healthy institutions;
- it duplicates a valid ordinary-law or institutional process;
- the institution can preserve the function more simply without the framework.

Conformance to v1.0 does not immunize the framework from these findings.

## 24. Bottom line

Institutional regeneration is not the art of making organizations conform to a preferred template.

It is the disciplined work of helping an institution distinguish:

- its mandate from its habits;
- its mission from its survival;
- its stated purpose from the pressures reproducing its behavior;
- affected standing from responsibility for controlling causes;
- symptom reform from reproductive reform;
- participation from constituted authority;
- advice from amendment;
- incentives from coercion;
- metrics and conformance from success;
- pilot evidence from jurisdiction;
- narrative renewal from propaganda;
- graceful closure from abandonment;
- continuity duties from preservation of an obsolete institutional shell.

A healthy institution can change. A healthy governance ecosystem also knows when the institution cannot change without upstream authority, when reform has become theatre, and when the useful function should survive without the organization that once carried it.

> **Regeneration is demonstrated not by audit completion or pilot activity, but by durable change in the capacities, incentives, authority relations, information structures, and selection pressures that previously reproduced institutional failure.**
