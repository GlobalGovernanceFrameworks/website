# Justice Systems Jurisdiction, Standing, Evidence, and Remedy Audit v0.1

## Can a healing-centred justice architecture adjudicate cross-boundary disputes without becoming a universal government through judicial language?

**Status:** Internal bounded constitutional and architectural audit  
**Primary object:** Justice Systems Implementation Framework for Global Governance v1.3  
**Source integrity note:** The title identifies the document as v1.3, while its closing metadata identifies it as “Framework Version 4.0 REVISED.” This audit treats the uploaded text as the operative source and does not infer which version label is authoritative.  
**Compatibility corpus:** Constitutional Interface Specification v0.1; Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3; Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing v1.1; Integrated Meta-Governance v1.5; Polycentric Governance Architecture v0.6; Genesis Protocol v0.9; AUBI v2.5; Financial Systems v3.4; Nested Economies v3.2; Work in Liberation v2.0

**Recommended successor:** Justice Systems Framework v1.4 — Plural Jurisdiction, Due Process, Restoration, and Remedy

# Executive summary

## Overall verdict

The source contains a serious moral and institutional insight: justice should not be equated with punishment, legal authority should remain accountable and intelligible, and adjudication should be capable of restoration, cultural plurality, learning, accessibility, and humility. Its strongest contributions include:

- universal access and plain-language commitments;
- an attempt to distinguish restoration from retribution;
- explicit attention to trauma, power asymmetry, ecological harm, and historical context;
- recognition that Indigenous legal systems are not merely sources of cultural advice;
- publication of reasoning, dissent, uncertainty, and institutional failures;
- concern about judicial mystification, personal glorification, capture, and permanent emergency authority;
- insistence that AI remain subordinate to human judgment;
- interest in legal aid, multilingual access, community capacity, and non-digital pathways.

Those commitments should survive.

The framework is nevertheless **not compatible with the revised constitutional stack** and is not ready for pilot implementation as a justice system. Its humane aspirations are attached to an architecture that lacks the basic legal rules needed to exercise public power safely.

The central institutional contradiction is severe:

> **The framework rejects concentrated and mystified authority, but creates a Global Justice Oversight Body that develops policy, assigns jurisdiction, resolves disputes, monitors compliance, and imposes sanctions.**

This combines legislative, administrative, prosecutorial, adjudicative, supervisory, and enforcement functions in one body. Calling the body restorative, rotating, transparent, or multi-source does not cure the separation-of-powers defect.

The central jurisprudential contradiction is equally important:

> **The framework treats restoration as the objective of justice rather than one legally bounded and often voluntary justice modality.**

Restoration can be valuable, but it cannot replace factual adjudication, individual rights, protection from ongoing harm, findings of liability, or lawful coercive remedies. Reconciliation, relationship repair, community harmony, gratitude, ceremonial participation, and satisfaction cannot become conditions of justice. A person must be able to seek protection, judgment, compensation, or acquittal without consenting to reconciliation.

The framework also assumes that GGF frameworks themselves create enforceable rights and offences. It proposes to enforce the Moral Operating System, AUBI, Financial Systems, Indigenous frameworks, and Treaty provisions without distinguishing:

- ethical guidance from law;
- framework specifications from valid legal instruments;
- ratified Treaty obligations from unactivated proposals;
- contractual rules from criminal offences;
- Indigenous jurisdiction from state or Treaty jurisdiction;
- professional standards from public regulation.

The source therefore risks turning “Justice Systems” into the universal enforcement layer for the entire GGF ecosystem. That would recreate a general government through judicial language.

## Central audit claim

> **Justice Systems should not be the enforcement layer of all GGF frameworks. It should specify how plural legal orders establish jurisdiction, standing, due process, evidence, remedy, appeal, recognition, and lawful enforcement—while keeping voluntary restoration separate from compulsory adjudication and protective action.**

## Readiness verdict

**Amber-red: P0 constitutional rebuild required before pilots involving adjudication, sanctions, legal status, compulsory process, or enforcement.**

Some non-coercive components may be tested earlier under existing lawful authority:

- legal-access support;
- plain-language decision summaries;
- voluntary restorative services;
- multilingual and disability access;
- trauma-informed participation support;
- judicial-learning and retrospective methods;
- community legal education;
- non-binding institutional-health review.

They must not be represented as a global justice system or as evidence that jurisdiction has been created.

## Recommended structural shift

The successor should move from a vertically integrated “global justice system” to a **plural justice interface architecture** with five distinct functions:

1. **Adjudication:** authoritative determination of rights, obligations, facts, liability, and lawful remedies within valid jurisdiction.
2. **Restorative process:** voluntary or carefully authorized processes addressing harm, responsibility, repair, and relationship where safe and appropriate.
3. **Protective process:** urgent, proportionate, reviewable measures preventing ongoing or imminent harm.
4. **Enforcement:** execution of lawful orders by separately authorized bodies subject to judicial review.
5. **Justice administration and access:** legal aid, translation, accessibility, case administration, recognition of judgments, and public information without adjudicative power.

No institution should control the complete chain.

# 1. Audit basis and method

## 1.1 Source characterization

The source describes itself as the enforcement and adjudication layer for the GGF ecosystem. It proposes a Global Justice Oversight Body, Regional Justice Hubs, National Implementation Units, international arbitration, climate and ecological tribunals, a Digital Justice Tribunal, community mediation, security handoffs, global monitoring, technology infrastructure, funding, and phased global scaling.

It is simultaneously:

- a philosophical essay on the evolution of justice;
- a restorative-justice programme;
- a global institutional blueprint;
- a legal harmonization proposal;
- a court-design outline;
- an enforcement coordination plan;
- a technology and funding plan;
- an implementation roadmap.

Those layers should be separated in v1.4.

## 1.2 Compatibility standard

The revised constitutional stack establishes rules that the source predates or does not yet implement:

- GGF frameworks do not create public authority merely by being written;
- Treaty powers activate only through valid ratification and separately authorized protocols;
- jurisdiction is bounded, decision-specific, and traceable to a constituting instrument;
- Indigenous sovereignty is recognized rather than integrated into a GGF hierarchy;
- Meta-Governance coordinates but does not supervise or adjudicate;
- guardians, monitors, and auditors may alert or seek review but do not become judges or executives;
- observation, diagnosis, provisional protection, adjudication, remedy, enforcement, and evaluation must remain distinct;
- indicators are alerts, not legal findings;
- protected non-transfer and protected continuity constrain open-data and sanction systems;
- coercive powers require legality, defence, evidence, independent adjudication, proportional remedy, and non-weaponization review;
- personal material security, access to justice, and essential services cannot be withdrawn as ordinary sanctions.

## 1.3 Audit tests

The review applies thirteen tests.

### A. Source-of-law test

What instrument turns a principle, framework rule, moral claim, compact, or technical standard into enforceable law?

### B. Jurisdiction test

Which court may hear which matter, over which person, territory, act, institution, or commons, and why?

### C. Standing test

Who may initiate, defend, intervene, appeal, or represent another person, people, ecosystem, future interest, or digital entity?

### D. Role-separation test

Are lawmaking, administration, investigation, prosecution, adjudication, monitoring, enforcement, and evaluation institutionally separated?

### E. Due-process test

Are notice, counsel, impartiality, legality, presumption, burden, proof, disclosure, hearing, defence, reasons, appeal, and remedy protected?

### F. Evidence test

How are provenance, admissibility, reliability, expert disagreement, protected knowledge, digital evidence, and uncertainty handled?

### G. Restorative-consent and safety test

Is restorative participation genuinely voluntary, informed, revocable, safe, and unsuitable where coercion or ongoing danger makes it inappropriate?

### H. Indigenous-jurisdiction test

Does the framework recognize Indigenous law and affected-nation authority without subordinating, aggregating, extracting, or treating one body as a global proxy?

### I. Remedy and continuity test

Are remedies targeted, proportionate, reviewable, and protective of innocent parties, material rights, essential services, and access to justice?

### J. Enforcement-handoff test

Can investigators or security bodies search, seize, arrest, freeze assets, or deploy only under valid law and review?

### K. Data and automation test

Can technology support justice without determining credibility, guilt, risk, standing, identity, sentience, sentence, or remedy?

### L. Learning-without-control test

Can institutions learn from outcomes without allowing satisfaction metrics, dashboards, or health monitors to determine legal validity?

### M. Entry-and-scaling test

Do pilots and coverage targets remain subordinate to ratification, local law, jurisdiction, and participant rights?

# 2. What the source is trying to preserve

The framework should be read sympathetically before correction. It is responding to real failures.

## 2.1 Law as protection from arbitrary power

The historical discussion correctly recognizes codified law as a genuine achievement. Predictability, public rules, and constraints on arbitrary authority are not obsolete remnants. They remain necessary even when justice becomes more participatory and restorative.

## 2.2 Restoration and repair

Many justice systems inadequately address victims’ needs, collective harm, structural causes, relationship repair, reintegration, and recurrence prevention. A justice architecture should offer more than punishment.

## 2.3 Accessibility and intelligibility

The source is right that legal language, architecture, cost, delay, and professional monopoly can make justice inaccessible. Plain-language summaries, legal education, interpretation, accommodations, and legal aid are constitutional goods.

## 2.4 Institutional fallibility

Publishing dissent, uncertainty, corrections, and institutional failures can improve accountability. Courts should not perform infallibility.

## 2.5 Cultural and legal plurality

A global constitutional order cannot assume that one state-derived legal tradition is universally legitimate. Indigenous law, customary law, compact law, domestic law, Treaty law, commons charters, and professional self-governance may coexist.

## 2.6 Ecological and intergenerational harm

Existing law often struggles with diffuse, cumulative, long-latency, cross-border, and non-human harms. The source correctly identifies a need for standing and remedies adapted to those problems.

## 2.7 Technology limits

The source’s statement that AI assists rather than replaces humans is directionally correct, although insufficient by itself.

These are valuable design commitments. The audit objects to the authority architecture and missing safeguards, not to the aspiration to make justice more healing, accessible, plural, and adaptive.

# 3. Document identity and readiness claims

## 3.1 Version conflict

The title identifies the framework as v1.3. The closing metadata calls it “Framework Version 4.0 REVISED,” provides a 2025 integration date, and declares it ready for stakeholder consultation and pilot implementation.

This creates uncertainty about:

- which version is being audited;
- whether earlier sections were fully reconciled with later revisions;
- whether “v1.3” is a framework version or filename lineage;
- whether the consultation and readiness claims apply to the whole document.

v1.4 should use one version, one date, one status, and a complete version history.

## 3.2 Outline versus legal instrument

The source is not yet a legal code, treaty protocol, procedural rulebook, or institutional charter. It lacks definitions, operative jurisdiction clauses, rights of parties, evidence rules, remedies, appeal structures, conflict-of-law rules, and entry-into-force provisions.

The statement that it is ready for pilot implementation must therefore be bounded. Voluntary service pilots may be possible. Exercises of adjudicative or coercive authority are not justified by this outline.

## 3.3 Claims of empirical readiness

The framework provides percentages for satisfaction, restoration success, coverage, compliance, and repeat-harm reduction without defining validated measures, baselines, sampling, attribution, or failure thresholds. These are aspirations, not readiness evidence.

# 4. GGF frameworks are not self-executing law

## 4.1 Source architecture

The source proposes to enforce:

- Moral Operating System rights across several tiers;
- Treaty obligations;
- Indigenous framework principles;
- AUBI and Hearts/Leaves rules;
- Financial Systems transparency rules;
- Work in Liberation cooperative obligations;
- rights of animals, ecosystems, AI, emergent entities, and planetary or alien entities.

## 4.2 Legal-authority problem

A framework may be:

- ethical guidance;
- model policy;
- technical specification;
- voluntary compact;
- professional standard;
- contract term;
- organizational charter;
- implementing legislation;
- ratified Treaty law.

These have different legal effects. A court cannot “enforce a framework” without identifying the instrument that gives the relevant rule legal force.

The Moral Operating System may inform ethical interpretation. It does not become criminal, civil, constitutional, or administrative law merely because Justice Systems cites it.

AUBI, Financial Systems, Nested Economies, and Work in Liberation define programme and institutional rules. Some may become contractual, statutory, charter-based, or Treaty obligations. Others remain design proposals. The court must identify the operative source.

## 4.3 Required Source-of-Law Record

Every claim should state:

1. the legal rule invoked;
2. the instrument containing it;
3. the authority that adopted the instrument;
4. entry into force;
5. territorial and personal scope;
6. whether it is civil, criminal, administrative, constitutional, contractual, customary, Indigenous, or advisory;
7. hierarchy relative to other law;
8. reservation, derogation, or withdrawal rules;
9. available remedy;
10. review and appeal.

No record, no compulsory adjudication.

## 4.4 Recommended legal hierarchy

v1.4 should not establish one universal hierarchy for every jurisdiction. It should define an interface method distinguishing at least:

- non-derogable rights and peremptory norms where legally recognized;
- Treaty law validly in force for relevant Parties;
- Indigenous law within Indigenous jurisdiction;
- domestic constitutional and statutory law;
- valid compacts, covenants, and commons charters;
- contracts and programme terms;
- professional and labour self-governance rules;
- persuasive ethical and technical guidance.

Conflicts require a jurisdiction-specific analysis rather than an assumption that a GGF document is supreme.

# 5. The Global Justice Oversight Body recreates concentrated authority

## 5.1 Combined powers

The proposed Global Justice Oversight Body performs:

- policy development;
- monitoring;
- dispute resolution;
- jurisdiction assignment;
- compliance enforcement;
- sanctions and incentives;
- leadership and institutional-health decisions;
- coordination with security and other frameworks.

This is not merely oversight. It is a combined legislature, regulator, court administrator, adjudicator, prosecutor, sanctioning authority, and systemic supervisor.

## 5.2 Rotation does not cure concentration

Four-year limits, rotating chairs, regional representation, capped state voting, and public votes can reduce some forms of entrenchment. They do not resolve the institutional conflict of interest created when one body:

- writes or adapts policy;
- interprets that policy;
- decides who has jurisdiction;
- judges compliance;
- imposes consequences;
- evaluates its own success.

A rotating concentration of power remains concentrated power.

## 5.3 Multi-source validation does not create jurisdiction

Validation by an Earth Council, citizens’ representatives, and Regional Justice Hubs is presented as distributed legitimacy. But:

- no universal Earth Council may be assumed;
- citizens’ representatives cannot validate jurisdiction beyond their constituting instrument;
- Regional Hubs are themselves created by the framework;
- mutual validation among GGF-created bodies can be circular.

## 5.4 Access-to-justice sanction

The body may restrict access to arbitration courts as a compliance sanction. Access to an independent remedy cannot be withheld as punishment by the institution whose decisions may need challenge.

The prohibition should be categorical:

> **No person, community, institution, or Party may lose access to an independent tribunal, defence, appeal, or remedy as an ordinary sanction for non-compliance.**

## 5.5 Replacement architecture

v1.4 should replace the Oversight Body with separated institutions:

### A. Justice Coordination and Access Secretariat

Administrative only. It may support translation, legal aid, scheduling, recognition records, training, accessibility, and cross-court communication. It cannot decide cases, assign contested jurisdiction, prosecute, sanction, or write binding law.

### B. Treaty Constitutional and Interjurisdictional Tribunal

Created only under Treaty or a separately ratified Justice Protocol. It decides defined Treaty, jurisdictional, and rights matters within valid competence.

### C. Judicial Appointments and Conduct Commission

Independent of courts, prosecutors, enforcement bodies, Meta-Governance, and political executives. Its powers, evidence, appeal, and removal standards must be defined.

### D. Office of Public Counsel and Justice Access

Supports legal aid, representation, translation, disability access, public-interest cases, and procedural assistance.

### E. Restorative Justice Service Network

Provides voluntary or lawfully referred processes under safety and consent rules. It does not determine criminal guilt, compel reconciliation, or enforce settlements without valid consent and review.

### F. Enforcement bodies

Remain separate and act only under valid orders and review.

# 6. Vertical implementation risks becoming a judicial command hierarchy

## 6.1 Source architecture

The source places:

1. a Global Justice Oversight Body;
2. Regional Justice Hubs;
3. National Implementation Units;
4. community and Indigenous processes

in a layered structure. National units enforce global justice policies and report upward. Regional Hubs adapt standards and resolve disputes with national units.

## 6.2 Constitutional problem

This architecture treats legal plurality as implementation variation beneath a global policy centre. It does not begin with a map of independently valid jurisdictions.

Domestic courts, Indigenous courts, Treaty tribunals, compact arbitrators, commons adjudicators, labour bodies, and restorative services do not form one administrative chain.

## 6.3 Polycentric replacement

v1.4 should use a jurisdiction network:

- **territorial courts** exercise authority under domestic or local law;
- **Indigenous legal institutions** exercise inherent or recognized jurisdiction under their own law and agreements;
- **Treaty tribunals** exercise only ratified Treaty jurisdiction;
- **commons and compact tribunals** exercise authority established by valid charters or agreements;
- **professional and labour bodies** exercise bounded self-governance;
- **restorative services** operate by consent or lawful referral;
- **coordination offices** support interfaces without superiority.

Regional bodies may provide capacity, interpretation, and mutual-recognition support. They should not become appellate courts or supervisors unless a constituting instrument expressly grants that jurisdiction.

# 7. Jurisdiction is asserted but not constituted

## 7.1 Missing jurisdictional bases

The source says the framework applies internationally, regionally, nationally, and in BAZs. It does not establish the legal basis by which a court obtains jurisdiction over:

- states;
- non-member states;
- corporations;
- individuals;
- Indigenous peoples;
- BAZs;
- digital entities;
- ecosystems;
- conduct outside territory;
- cross-border effects;
- Treaty crimes;
- contractual disputes;
- internal cooperative disputes.

## 7.2 Required jurisdiction categories

v1.4 should distinguish at least:

### A. Territorial jurisdiction

Authority based on conduct, persons, property, or harm within a lawful territory.

### B. Personal jurisdiction

Authority over persons or entities through nationality, domicile, presence, incorporation, consent, or another valid basis.

### C. Treaty jurisdiction

Authority accepted by Parties for defined Treaty obligations and procedures.

### D. Compact or contractual jurisdiction

Authority accepted in a lawful agreement, including arbitration or programme disputes.

### E. Indigenous jurisdiction

Authority deriving from affected Indigenous peoples’ law, sovereignty, treaty relationships, or valid intergovernmental agreements.

### F. Commons jurisdiction

Authority established by a valid commons charter, Treaty, covenant, or law for a defined ecological system.

### G. Universal or international-crime jurisdiction

Available only where valid law defines the offence, jurisdiction, procedural rights, and non-weaponization safeguards.

### H. Effects-based cross-boundary jurisdiction

Targeted jurisdiction over a material externality where valid law authorizes it and lesser remedies are inadequate.

## 7.3 Jurisdiction may be absent

The framework must permit a finding that no proposed GGF institution has jurisdiction. In that case, the response may be:

- referral to a competent existing court;
- negotiation of a compact;
- voluntary mediation;
- support for domestic capacity;
- preservation of evidence;
- a political or legislative proposal;
- an unresolved jurisdiction record.

A gap does not authorize the Global Justice Oversight Body to fill itself with jurisdiction.

# 8. Standing and representation are under-specified

## 8.1 Persons and groups

The source does not clearly establish standing for:

- directly harmed individuals;
- accused or regulated persons;
- communities;
- workers;
- consumers;
- refugees and stateless persons;
- future generations;
- Indigenous nations;
- ecosystems;
- animals;
- public-interest organizations;
- whistleblowers;
- corporations and public institutions;
- AI or claimed digital persons.

## 8.2 Representation problems

Representation requires rules for:

- authorization;
- conflict of interest;
- fiduciary duty;
- internal dissent;
- class or collective actions;
- guardianship;
- replacement;
- settlement authority;
- appeal;
- costs.

An advocate cannot simply claim to speak for “the community,” “future generations,” “the ecosystem,” or “Indigenous wisdom.”

## 8.3 Standing matrix

Every case class should identify:

1. who may initiate;
2. who must receive notice;
3. who may intervene;
4. who has defence rights;
5. who may appeal;
6. who may represent absent or non-human interests;
7. what instrument creates that representation;
8. how conflicts among representatives are handled.

## 8.4 Ecosystem and future-generations standing

A rights-of-nature or future-generations representative should derive authority from a law, Treaty, compact, trust, or charter. Appointment by the challenged institution is a conflict. Multiple representatives and adversarial ecological evidence may be necessary.

## 8.5 Digital-entity standing

No AI, emergent system, or extraterrestrial category should receive legal standing through an “Exceptional Cases Committee” using an unvalidated consciousness test. The relevant law must first define provisional standing, representation, evidentiary standards, and reversible protections under uncertainty.

# 9. Applicable law and conflicts of jurisdiction are missing

## 9.1 Multiple legal orders

A case may implicate:

- Treaty law;
- domestic constitutional law;
- Indigenous law;
- human-rights law;
- commons charters;
- labour or professional rules;
- private contracts;
- criminal law;
- administrative law;
- restorative agreements.

The source does not specify which controls.

## 9.2 Required conflict-of-law questions

A tribunal should record:

1. the legal orders implicated;
2. each order’s jurisdictional claim;
3. the parties’ consent or objection;
4. mandatory and non-derogable rules;
5. Indigenous and territorial authority;
6. choice-of-law clauses;
7. public-policy limits;
8. parallel proceedings;
9. exhaustion requirements;
10. recognition and enforcement consequences.

## 9.3 Forum conflict

v1.4 needs rules for:

- forum shopping;
- lis pendens and parallel cases;
- double jeopardy or equivalent protections;
- issue and claim preclusion;
- transfer and referral;
- abstention;
- exhaustion of local remedies;
- urgent exceptions;
- appellate route;
- recognition of judgments.

## 9.4 Jurisdiction assignment

A political-administrative Oversight Body should not assign disputed jurisdiction. Jurisdiction should be determined by the receiving court under published law, subject to interlocutory review or a designated interjurisdictional tribunal.

# 10. The due-process architecture is incomplete

## 10.1 Missing minimum rights

The framework does not provide a full guarantee of:

- legality and non-retroactivity;
- notice of claims and charges;
- an independent and impartial tribunal;
- presumption of innocence in criminal matters;
- burden and standard of proof;
- access to evidence;
- privilege against self-incrimination where applicable;
- counsel and legal aid;
- interpretation and communication support;
- disability and cognitive accessibility;
- opportunity to respond and call evidence;
- cross-examination or safe functional equivalent;
- reasoned decisions;
- public hearing subject to lawful exceptions;
- timely but not rushed proceedings;
- appeal or review;
- protection against double jeopardy;
- compensation for wrongful action;
- judicial recusal;
- equality of arms;
- protection from retaliation.

## 10.2 Context does not replace proof

Poverty, oppression, trauma, colonial history, and power asymmetry may be legally relevant. They do not eliminate the need to determine facts and apply defined standards.

“Contextual rather than binary” cannot mean that guilt, innocence, liability, validity, or jurisdiction remain indeterminate where rights depend on a finding.

## 10.3 Decision time

A universal requirement to publish reasoning within seven days may be appropriate for simple interim matters and dangerous for complex litigation. Timeliness standards should reflect case type, urgency, record size, translation, accessibility, and the parties’ ability to participate.

## 10.4 Public comment on judgments

Public comment can inform rulemaking and institutional review. It should not influence the adjudication of an individual pending case or substitute for appeal.

# 11. Evidence architecture is largely absent

## 11.1 Missing evidence rules

The source does not define:

- relevance;
- admissibility;
- reliability;
- authentication;
- hearsay and exceptions;
- expert evidence;
- burden and standard;
- privilege;
- confidential evidence;
- protected Indigenous knowledge;
- digital chain of custody;
- algorithmic evidence;
- adverse inference;
- disclosure;
- witness protection;
- correction of records;
- treatment of uncertainty.

## 11.2 Epistemic plurality without evidentiary collapse

Multiple ontologies and evidence traditions may be relevant. The court should preserve disagreement and protected non-transfer while still deciding the legal question before it.

It may distinguish:

- factual claim;
- normative claim;
- legal rule;
- expert inference;
- bounded attestation;
- sacred or protected knowledge;
- community authorization;
- uncertainty;
- precautionary reason.

## 11.3 Protected non-transfer

A party or Indigenous authority may sometimes communicate:

- authorization;
- refusal;
- threshold exceeded;
- risk classification;
- procedural compliance;
- bounded attestation

without disclosing sacred or restricted knowledge. Courts need reviewers, confidentiality arrangements, and limits on adverse inference.

## 11.4 Digital evidence

Security-body transfers must preserve:

- lawful collection;
- warrant or authorization;
- chain of custody;
- integrity;
- disclosure obligations;
- challenge;
- minimization;
- deletion or return;
- protected data.

“Blockchain transfer” does not establish legality or truth.

# 12. Restoration is a modality, not the universal legal objective

## 12.1 Source overreach

The source repeatedly defines success as healing relationships, restoring community harmony, and reducing the need for formal intervention. It contrasts restoration with factual or binary judgment.

This risks making justice contingent on outcomes that may be impossible, unsafe, unwanted, or normatively inappropriate.

## 12.2 Rights not to reconcile

A harmed person may legitimately seek:

- protection;
- factual acknowledgment;
- declaration of rights;
- restitution;
- compensation;
- injunction;
- removal of an official;
- criminal accountability;
- institutional reform;
- separation from the person who caused harm.

They need not forgive, reconcile, repair a relationship, participate in a circle, express gratitude, or restore “community harmony.”

## 12.3 Restorative consent standard

Restorative participation should require:

- informed and specific consent;
- ability to withdraw;
- independent advice;
- absence of retaliation for refusal;
- no loss of adjudicative rights;
- safety assessment;
- power-asymmetry assessment;
- confidentiality rules;
- enforceability and review of any agreement;
- protection against compelled apology or forgiveness;
- separate support for affected persons.

## 12.4 Suitability exclusions and heightened safeguards

Restorative processes require special caution or may be unsuitable where there is:

- ongoing coercive control;
- domestic or sexual violence;
- trafficking;
- severe child abuse;
- organized criminal intimidation;
- credible retaliation risk;
- inability to consent;
- major power imbalance without effective safeguards;
- denial of basic facts combined with pressure on the harmed party;
- crimes involving broad public interests that private settlement cannot resolve.

## 12.5 Restorative and adjudicative processes may coexist

A restorative process may address repair after or alongside a lawful factual finding. It should not erase public accountability, conceal patterns, waive non-waivable rights, or prevent other affected parties from seeking remedy.

# 13. “Community harmony” and satisfaction are unsafe legal metrics

## 13.1 Harmony can conceal domination

A community may report harmony because dissenters were silenced, displaced, pressured to forgive, or excluded from measurement. A rights-protecting judgment may reduce short-term satisfaction because it disrupts a dominant local norm.

## 13.2 Satisfaction cannot validate a judgment

Community satisfaction below sixty percent is proposed as an automatic review trigger. Satisfaction can be useful service feedback. It is not evidence that a judgment is legally wrong or that a court lacks legitimacy.

## 13.3 Restored relationships are not universally measurable

Some cases concern strangers, institutions, ecosystems, future generations, or public law. No relationship exists to restore. A remedy may be successful because it stops harm, compensates loss, clarifies law, or preserves rights.

## 13.4 Multidimensional evaluation

Evaluation should separate:

- procedural fairness;
- access and accessibility;
- timeliness;
- legal accuracy and appellate stability;
- safety;
- remedy delivery;
- recurrence;
- restoration where chosen;
- participant experience;
- distributional effects;
- institutional independence;
- rights violations;
- missing populations.

No composite score should determine validity, funding, jurisdiction, or personnel removal automatically.

# 14. Protective authority, criminal justice, and incapacitation are underdeveloped

## 14.1 False binary between restoration and punishment

Not every coercive measure is retribution. Some measures protect people, preserve evidence, prevent flight, stop pollution, remove an abusive official, freeze disputed assets, or incapacitate a continuing threat.

The relevant distinction is not simply restorative versus punitive. It is:

- protective versus retaliatory;
- necessary versus excessive;
- lawful versus arbitrary;
- targeted versus collective;
- reviewable versus unaccountable;
- reparative versus degrading.

## 14.2 Criminal-law requirements

If the framework addresses ecocide, cybercrime, organized crime, fraud, or violence, it needs:

- defined offences;
- legality and non-retroactivity;
- mental elements;
- defences;
- prosecutorial independence;
- burden beyond the applicable criminal standard;
- counsel;
- public and fair trial;
- sentencing principles;
- detention safeguards;
- appeal;
- wrongful-conviction remedy;
- victim participation without control of guilt determination.

## 14.3 Detention and restrictive measures

The source does not state whether detention, travel restriction, asset freezing, exclusion, or removal are permitted. Silence is unsafe. v1.4 should either exclude these powers or establish the separate legal protocol required to authorize them.

## 14.4 No sanctions against access or essentials

Sanctions must not withdraw:

- access to court;
- legal aid;
- AUBI Layer 1;
- essential health, water, food, shelter, or communication;
- personal balances unrelated to wrongdoing;
- whistleblower protection;
- appeal;
- protected Indigenous or community rights.

# 15. Indigenous legal systems are recognized and simultaneously subordinated

## 15.1 Source strengths

The framework says Indigenous justice is not something Western systems should improve, recognizes Indigenous tribunals, and requires FPIC for processes affecting Indigenous communities.

## 15.2 “Integration into national units” contradiction

It also proposes integrating Indigenous tribunals into national implementation units. This can transform inherent jurisdiction into a delegated subunit of the state or GGF system.

v1.4 should use **recognition and interface agreements**, not integration by default.

## 15.3 “Full authority within recognized territories” ambiguity

The phrase leaves unresolved:

- who recognizes the territory;
- overlapping territorial claims;
- internal community plurality;
- non-member residents;
- individual rights;
- criminal jurisdiction;
- cross-boundary harms;
- concurrent state jurisdiction;
- appeal or review;
- treaty relationships.

The framework cannot answer these globally. Each interface must identify the affected people, law, territory, subject matter, persons, procedure, consent, conflict rules, and remedy.

## 15.4 No universal Indigenous validator

The Global Justice Oversight Body cannot rely on an Indigenous Data Sovereignty Council or Earth Council as universal validators. Indigenous-created bodies may participate within the authority granted by the peoples who constituted them.

## 15.5 Knowledge and training extraction

Training non-Indigenous judges may be valuable when Indigenous authorities invite it. Indigenous facilitators are not obligated to teach, disclose, exchange, or participate reciprocally. Protected non-transfer applies.

## 15.6 Tribunal limits

A Treaty tribunal may adjudicate whether a recognized authority followed an applicable procedure or whether a Treaty obligation was breached. It should not decide:

- who is authentically Indigenous;
- spiritual truth;
- sacred meaning;
- validity of Traditional Knowledge;
- internal leadership outside its jurisdiction;
- the content of Indigenous law reserved to the affected people.

# 16. Ecological justice needs constituted guardianship and bounded precaution

## 16.1 Legal personhood is jurisdiction-dependent

Rivers, forests, species, animals, and ecosystems may receive rights or legal personality under particular laws. The Justice framework cannot assume one universal rights ontology.

## 16.2 Guardian authority

Every guardian must identify:

- constituting instrument;
- ecological object represented;
- duties;
- powers;
- conflicts;
- funding;
- evidence access;
- replacement;
- accountability;
- relationship to affected Indigenous and territorial authorities.

## 16.3 Precaution

“When uncertain, protect ecosystems” is a valuable orientation but not a complete legal test. Precautionary action should consider:

- severity and reversibility of threatened harm;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- urgency;
- distributional effects;
- Indigenous rights;
- alternative measures;
- duration;
- review;
- restoration if the estimate proves wrong.

## 16.4 Interim ecological measures

Courts may need temporary injunctions, monitoring, evidence preservation, or activity limits. These should be narrow, time-bounded, and reviewable rather than automatic permanent prohibitions.

## 16.5 Systemic responsibility

Climate and ecological harms may involve states, corporations, financiers, supply chains, and historical contributions. v1.4 needs rules for causation, attribution, joint liability, contribution, remedy allocation, and innocent-party continuity.

# 17. Adaptive law cannot mean unstable rights or judicial legislation

## 17.1 Living jurisprudence

Courts inevitably interpret law and learn from precedent. But an annual Regional Hub review should not rewrite legal standards without the authority and process required for legislation or Treaty amendment.

## 17.2 Five-year sunset problem

The source subjects all legal standards to five-year sunset review. Review is sensible; automatic expiration is not. Fundamental rights, offences, jurisdiction, contracts, final judgments, and procedural protections require stability.

## 17.3 Separation of change mechanisms

v1.4 should distinguish:

- judicial interpretation;
- correction of precedent;
- procedural rule amendment;
- administrative guidance;
- legislative amendment;
- Treaty amendment;
- pilot revision;
- emergency temporary measures.

Each has a different authority and retroactivity rule.

## 17.4 No original-intent infallibility

Institutional-health review should preserve founding purposes and later learning. Neither founders nor current judges should control legal evolution by unilateral interpretation.

# 18. Jurisdictional compatibility ratings can become ideological ranking

## 18.1 Source mechanism

The source proposes High, Medium, and Low compatibility ratings based partly on punishment, mystification, authority concentration, and MOS alignment.

## 18.2 Risk

A rating may become:

- a condition for funding;
- a presumption against recognition of judgments;
- a justification for intervention;
- a geopolitical legitimacy score;
- pressure to adopt GGF cultural preferences.

## 18.3 Replacement

Use a **Jurisdictional Interface Assessment** identifying concrete interoperability facts:

- court independence;
- due process;
- rights protections;
- applicable law;
- recognition procedure;
- data and privacy;
- Indigenous jurisdiction;
- enforcement capability;
- corruption and capture risks;
- remedy availability;
- unresolved incompatibilities.

It should not rank whole societies as developmentally or morally compatible.

# 19. Developmental-stage classification is inappropriate for legal authority

## 19.1 Source framing

The source maps justice approaches to developmental centres associated with Blue, Orange, Green, and Yellow and describes some contexts as needing more structure before fuller restoration.

## 19.2 Legal danger

A court or global institution could use such categories to:

- assign fewer participatory rights;
- justify paternalism;
- treat a community as less capable of autonomy;
- privilege external experts;
- vary legal protections;
- dismiss dissent as developmental limitation.

## 19.3 Context without ranking

Justice design should respond to concrete conditions:

- violence and security;
- institutional capacity;
- trust;
- legal tradition;
- language;
- displacement;
- trauma;
- inequality;
- local authority;
- access.

It should not classify peoples or communities by developmental stage. The entire stage-based legal-operational section should be removed or relocated to a non-authoritative design reflection with strong anti-discrimination limits.

# 20. Tribunal culture cannot be constitutionally mandatory

## 20.1 Circles and physical design

Circle seating and less intimidating spaces may help some processes. They cannot replace the authority, security, accessibility, evidentiary, and role clarity required in every proceeding.

## 20.2 Mandatory ceremonies

The source requires every proceeding to begin with prescribed humility statements, land acknowledgment, gratitude to Indigenous wisdom, and ceremonial framing.

A compulsory ceremony can:

- violate conscience or religious neutrality;
- appropriate Indigenous forms;
- imply endorsement of a metaphysical or historical claim;
- pressure participants to perform agreement;
- obscure the court’s legal function.

Ceremony should be optional, locally authorized, party-appropriate, and never a condition of standing or respectability.

## 20.3 Humility as conduct, not ritual compliance

Judicial humility should be demonstrated through:

- reasoned decisions;
- uncertainty disclosure;
- dissent;
- correction;
- appeal;
- recusal;
- limits on jurisdiction;
- respectful treatment.

It should not be assessed through mandated phrases, affect, personal modesty, or aversion to institutional formality.

## 20.4 Plain language

Every decision should include an accessible summary. The complete legal reasons must remain sufficiently precise for appeal, precedent, enforcement, and future interpretation. “Explainable to a twelve-year-old in five minutes” is a useful editorial aspiration, not a validity test.

# 21. Transparency is overgeneralized

## 21.1 Open justice

Public hearings and published reasons are important. “Everything visible except where privacy truly protects vulnerable people” is not an adequate legal standard.

## 21.2 Legitimate confidentiality

Restrictions may protect:

- children;
- sexual-violence survivors;
- witnesses;
- personal health;
- trade secrets subject to public-interest limits;
- national or community security;
- ongoing investigations;
- privileged communication;
- Indigenous protected knowledge;
- jury or judicial deliberation;
- settlement negotiation;
- personal addresses and identity.

## 21.3 Live streaming

Universal live streaming can expose participants to harassment, retaliation, misinformation, facial recognition, and permanent searchability. Courts should decide access under published rules and less intrusive alternatives.

## 21.4 Public comment

Public accountability belongs in rulemaking, institutional evaluation, and public-interest dialogue. Comments on an active individual case can prejudice proceedings and threaten parties.

## 21.5 Correction and deletion

Immutable records conflict with correction, expungement, rehabilitation, witness protection, data minimization, and protected non-transfer. A federated record system with integrity proofs, access controls, correction history, and lawful deletion is preferable to mandatory blockchain permanence.

# 22. Technology safeguards are incomplete

## 22.1 Human final decision is not enough

AI may materially shape outcomes through:

- legal research ranking;
- translation;
- case triage;
- evidence extraction;
- risk scoring;
- credibility cues;
- precedent selection;
- settlement suggestions;
- resource allocation.

A human clicking “approve” does not neutralize upstream bias.

## 22.2 Prohibited AI uses

Absent separately authorized and validated rules, AI should not determine or recommend:

- guilt or liability;
- credibility;
- detention or release;
- sentence;
- jurisdiction;
- standing;
- Indigenous identity or authority;
- cultural authenticity;
- consent;
- sentience or personhood;
- emergency declaration;
- sanction;
- remedy amount without human-auditable legal basis.

## 22.3 Required AI record

Any AI-assisted function should disclose:

- purpose;
- model and version;
- provider;
- training and evaluation limits where available;
- inputs and prohibited inputs;
- output and uncertainty;
- human use;
- contestability;
- bias and accessibility tests;
- retention;
- security;
- alternatives.

## 22.4 Exceptional Cases Committee

The proposed use of an AI Consciousness Assessment Framework and Moon Wish Test to decide rights of AI, emergent, planetary, or alien entities is not a valid evidentiary basis. At most, such tools may inform research or precautionary state estimates. Legal status requires separately authorized law and a robust evidentiary process.

# 23. Security and Shield handoffs require warrants, review, and return rules

## 23.1 Source architecture

Justice matters revealing criminal activity are transferred to the Shield Protocol for investigation, arrests, and asset seizures, then returned for reparations or restoration.

## 23.2 Missing safeguards

The framework does not specify:

- who authorizes the referral;
- thresholds;
- warrant requirements;
- prosecutorial control;
- detention rights;
- search and seizure limits;
- privileged material;
- disclosure;
- chain of custody;
- classified evidence;
- asset-freeze review;
- return or deletion;
- remedies for unlawful action.

## 23.3 Required separation

- courts authorize or review compulsory investigative measures;
- investigators collect evidence;
- prosecutors or authorized applicants bring cases;
- courts adjudicate;
- enforcement bodies execute orders;
- independent bodies investigate misconduct;
- restorative services address repair where appropriate.

The Council of Guardians should not provide operational ethical oversight of individual security handoffs.

## 23.4 Immediate protection

A security concern may justify urgent action under law. “Security never overrides restoration” is too absolute; immediate safety may require temporary separation or restraint. The correct rule is that necessity does not erase legality, proportionality, review, or restoration obligations.

# 24. Peace and mediation handoffs are arbitrary

## 24.1 Three sessions or ninety days

Mediation failure cannot be universally defined by three sessions or ninety days. Some cases need urgent adjudication before mediation. Others may benefit from longer voluntary dialogue.

## 24.2 No compulsory mediation for rights protection

Mediation should not delay:

- emergency injunctions;
- protection from violence;
- limitation periods;
- preservation of evidence;
- criminal investigation;
- non-waivable public-law duties.

## 24.3 Joint panel authority

A joint framework panel should not assign judicial pathways unless law or party agreement gives it that authority. It may provide intake and non-binding referral support.

## 24.4 Mediation privilege and data separation

Information disclosed in mediation should not automatically flow into adjudication, security, or global knowledge systems. Consent, privilege, safety exceptions, and data boundaries are essential.

# 25. Funding architecture is unconstituted and metric-heavy

## 25.1 Revenue authority

The source relies on member-state contributions, global carbon and financial-transaction taxes, and philanthropy. Treaty v1.3 does not self-activate compulsory global taxation. Funding requires lawful instruments.

## 25.2 Judicial independence

Justice funding should be protected from:

- donor influence;
- executive retaliation;
- case-linked funding;
- performance pay based on satisfaction or restoration;
- technology-vendor dependence;
- political control of legal aid.

## 25.3 Exact allocation ratios

Forty percent to regions, thirty percent to technology, twenty percent to coordination, and ten percent to contingencies are planning assumptions. They should not become constitutional rules without needs assessment.

Thirty percent for blockchain and AI infrastructure may itself produce vendor capture and divert resources from counsel, access, investigation, interpretation, witness protection, and local courts.

## 25.4 “Restoration per dollar”

This metric can pressure facilitators to select easy cases, underfund complex rights claims, or define justice through quantifiable reconciliation. Cost information belongs in administration, not as a validity standard.

## 25.5 Funding plan

v1.4 should require:

- legal source;
- budget independence;
- protected legal aid;
- transparent procurement;
- vendor neutrality;
- conflict disclosure;
- regional equity;
- insolvency and continuity;
- no funder role in cases or appointments;
- audit without case interference.

# 26. Monitoring and institutional-health review risk ideological enforcement

## 26.1 Valuable aim

Courts should be monitored for delay, discrimination, abuse, corruption, inaccessible procedure, excessive punishment, and retaliation.

## 26.2 Vague indicators

Terms such as:

- self-glorification;
- lack of humility;
- authority-centred behaviour;
- mystification;
- ego-driven decision-making

are too subjective to support removal, early rotation, or formal discipline.

## 26.3 Judicial independence

A judge may issue an unpopular, firm, or highly technical decision without being authoritarian. Conduct review should be based on defined obligations and evidence, not personality style or ideological conformity.

## 26.4 Monitor powers

Monitors may:

- collect data;
- inspect administration;
- receive complaints;
- publish findings;
- refer alleged misconduct;
- recommend reform.

They should not automatically remove personnel, invalidate judgments, or alter jurisdiction.

## 26.5 Personnel rotation

A universal four-year maximum in the same role may weaken competence and independence, particularly for judges. Terms should reflect function. Long, non-renewable judicial terms may better reduce political dependence, while administrators and leadership roles may rotate more frequently.

# 27. Implementation sequence activates institutions before authority

## 27.1 Treaty and MOS approval

The source begins Phase 0 after approval by thirty or more nations, without using Treaty v1.3’s actual entry-into-force and limited-activation rules. It also treats MOS approval as a constitutional prerequisite even though MOS is not established as law.

## 27.2 Oversight body first

The framework proposes establishing the Global Justice Oversight Body before defining the legal authority, jurisdiction, judicial appointment process, or separately ratified coercive protocol.

## 27.3 Coverage is not legitimacy

Targets such as seventy percent global coverage, eighty percent compliance, and ninety percent digital access can turn expansion into institutional purpose. Jurisdictions may lawfully remain outside, participate partially, or use equivalent local systems.

## 27.4 Pilot boundaries

Before a ratified Justice Protocol, pilots may test:

- voluntary mediation and restoration;
- legal-aid delivery;
- translation and accessibility;
- case-management tools;
- public legal education;
- institutional learning;
- interoperable non-binding referral.

They may not test compulsory jurisdiction on non-consenting parties, criminal adjudication, sanctions, asset seizure, or Treaty enforcement without existing legal authority.

## 27.5 Genesis v0.9 alignment

First seating of any Tribunal, compulsory sanctions system, criminal jurisdiction, forensic body with compulsory power, or deployment service must remain inactive until the relevant ratified protocol is in force.

# 31. Recommended v1.4 architecture

## Proposed title

**Justice Systems Framework v1.4 — Plural Jurisdiction, Due Process, Restoration, and Remedy**

The title should avoid describing one integrated global justice system where several legal orders actually coexist.

## Proposed structure

### 1. Purpose, status, and non-authority

State that the framework does not itself create offences, jurisdiction, courts, sanctions, taxes, or legal status.

### 2. Justice modalities

Define adjudication, mediation, restorative process, protective action, enforcement, administration, investigation, and public legal education.

### 3. Sources of law

Create the Source-of-Law Record and hierarchy/interface method.

### 4. Decision and case classes

Distinguish private, administrative, constitutional, Treaty, criminal, labour, financial, ecological, Indigenous-interface, compact, and public-interest matters.

### 5. Jurisdiction

Define bases, objections, transfer, abstention, externalities, and absence of jurisdiction.

### 6. Standing and representation

Persons, groups, Indigenous nations, workers, ecosystems, future generations, public-interest applicants, and provisional digital-entity questions.

### 7. Applicable law and conflict of laws

Parallel proceedings, choice of law, exhaustion, recognition, non-derogable rules, and Indigenous interfaces.

### 8. Institutional separation

Replace the Oversight Body with separated court, administration, appointment/conduct, public counsel, restorative-service, and enforcement functions.

### 9. Judicial appointment and independence

Qualification, nomination, confirmation, term, recusal, discipline, removal, budget, and accessibility.

### 10. Due-process bill

Notice, counsel, equality, burden, proof, disclosure, hearing, reasons, accessibility, and appeal.

### 11. Evidence and epistemic plurality

Provenance, experts, protected non-transfer, digital evidence, uncertainty, and AI assistance.

### 12. Restorative justice protocol

Consent, safety, suitability, confidentiality, withdrawal, legal effect, and relationship to adjudication.

### 13. Indigenous law and affected-nation interfaces

Recognition without integration, nation-specific agreements, non-engagement, internal plurality, and tribunal limits.

### 14. Ecological, collective, and future-generations justice

Guardianship, standing, causation, precaution, interim protection, and restoration.

### 15. Digital systems, AI, and claimed emergent entities

Prohibited uses, contestability, provisional protections, and no automated status determination.

### 16. Interim measures

Urgency, threshold, scope, duration, review, compensation, and restoration.

### 17. Remedies

Declaration, restitution, compensation, injunction, structural relief, restoration, sanctions, costs, and continuity.

### 18. Appeals, review, reopening, and correction

Appellate routes, standards, stays, new evidence, wrongful action, and precedent correction.

### 19. Investigation, prosecution, and enforcement handoffs

Warrants, chain of custody, asset actions, detention, classified information, and misconduct review.

### 20. Recognition and enforcement of judgments

Cross-border effect, refusal grounds, non-party rights, immunity, and assistance.

### 21. Access, counsel, language, disability, trauma, and child safeguards

Material access rather than merely digital access.

### 22. Data, records, transparency, and protected knowledge

Open justice, privacy, correction, deletion, security, and institutional memory.

### 23. Funding and administrative independence

Lawful revenue, legal aid, procurement, continuity, and no performance pressure on judgments.

### 24. Learning, evaluation, and institutional conduct

Separate service metrics from legal validity and define conduct standards precisely.

### 25. Transition, pilots, first seating, and non-activation

Align with Genesis v0.9 and Treaty v1.3. Keep coercive functions inactive until validly constituted.

### 26. Amendment, withdrawal, dissolution, and continuity

Defer Treaty amendment to Treaty law; provide administrative change and institutional-wind-down rules.

### 27. Operational records and appendices

Provide reusable templates rather than universal cultural or technological requirements.

# 33. Falsification gates

The successor should not be treated as constitutionally ready unless it passes these tests.

## Gate 1 — No framework-to-law conversion

No MOS, AUBI, financial, labour, Indigenous, ecological, or technical framework rule is enforced without a valid legal source.

## Gate 2 — No self-assigned jurisdiction

A coordination or oversight body cannot create jurisdiction by identifying a gap or cross-framework conflict.

## Gate 3 — No combined justice sovereign

No body develops policy, prosecutes, adjudicates, sanctions, monitors, and evaluates the same matter.

## Gate 4 — No denial of remedy as sanction

Access to an independent tribunal, defence, appeal, or legal aid is not withdrawn to compel compliance.

## Gate 5 — No forced restoration

No harmed or accused person is pressured to reconcile, forgive, participate ceremonially, or accept community harmony as a condition of justice.

## Gate 6 — No rights-by-satisfaction

Community satisfaction or restoration metrics cannot validate or invalidate a judgment automatically.

## Gate 7 — No Indigenous integration by default

Indigenous legal institutions are not made subordinate implementation units, universal proxies, or compulsory knowledge providers.

## Gate 8 — No universal cultural ritual

No ceremony, land acknowledgment, gratitude statement, circle format, or humility performance is mandatory across all proceedings.

## Gate 9 — No AI determination of legal status

AI does not determine credibility, guilt, detention, sentence, standing, Indigenous identity, consent, sentience, jurisdiction, sanction, or remedy.

## Gate 10 — No immutable case record by default

Record integrity does not eliminate correction, privacy, expungement, protected knowledge, rehabilitation, or lawful deletion.

## Gate 11 — No security action without lawful process

Search, seizure, arrest, detention, asset freezing, or deployment requires valid authority, review, evidence handling, and remedy.

## Gate 12 — No mediation delay of urgent rights

Mediation timelines cannot postpone protection, evidence preservation, criminal process, or limitation deadlines.

## Gate 13 — No automatic legal sunset

Rights, offences, jurisdiction, final judgments, and procedural guarantees do not expire merely because a five-year review date arrives.

## Gate 14 — No ideological compatibility ranking

Jurisdictions are assessed for concrete interoperability and rights facts, not ranked by MOS alignment or developmental stage.

## Gate 15 — No unratified global revenue or coercion

Taxes, criminal jurisdiction, sanctions, and enforcement remain inactive until lawfully constituted.

## Gate 16 — No coverage-as-legitimacy

Global reach, compliance percentages, or digital uptake do not substitute for accession, jurisdiction, or rights.

## Gate 17 — Failure can stop the justice project

A pilot or review may conclude that the institution should not receive jurisdiction, should remain voluntary, needs redesign, or should dissolve.

# 35. Final assessment

The Justice Systems framework has the right moral anxiety: a new planetary legal order could easily reproduce the punishment, mystification, hierarchy, cultural domination, and institutional self-preservation it claims to transcend.

Its current answer relies too heavily on institutional culture:

- rotating chairs;
- circles;
- humility rituals;
- restorative language;
- public dashboards;
- satisfaction scores;
- anti-glorification norms;
- multi-source representation.

These may influence culture. They cannot substitute for constitutional structure.

A court is not made safe because its officials speak humbly. It is made safer because:

- its jurisdiction is valid and bounded;
- the law is knowable;
- the parties have standing and defence;
- evidence is tested;
- rights do not depend on reconciliation;
- decisions are reasoned and appealable;
- interim action is narrow and reviewable;
- remedies are proportionate;
- enforcement is separate;
- Indigenous sovereignty and protected knowledge are not absorbed;
- data and AI are contestable;
- funding cannot control outcomes;
- the institution can admit error, reverse harm, and remain without jurisdiction.

The governing principle for v1.4 should be:

> **Justice authority arises from valid law and bounded jurisdiction—not from moral aspiration, restorative intent, global need, technical capability, or GGF framework coherence.**

The restorative principle should be:

> **Restoration is offered where it is safe, lawful, and freely chosen. No person must reconcile, forgive, disclose, perform harmony, or surrender adjudicative rights in order to receive justice.**

The plural-jurisdiction principle should be:

> **Treaty, domestic, Indigenous, commons, compact, labour, and professional legal orders may interact without being collapsed into one global chain of command. A jurisdictional gap does not authorize the coordinator to become the court.**

The evidence principle should be:

> **Context, lived experience, Indigenous knowledge, scientific models, and technical evidence may all matter, but no form of knowledge becomes an unchallengeable shortcut from observation to legal consequence. Protected knowledge may remain protected.**

The enforcement principle should be:

> **Investigation, adjudication, remedy, and enforcement remain separate. No claim of security, restoration, ecology, or emergency erases legality, defence, review, proportionality, or continuity.**

The final readiness test should be:

> **Could the framework protect an unpopular person, reject a morally attractive but unlawful claim, recognize that no GGF court has jurisdiction, preserve Indigenous non-engagement, refuse an automated recommendation, permit a victim to decline reconciliation, and reverse a wrongful interim measure?**

Where the answer is yes, the framework is becoming a justice architecture.

Where the answer is no, “healing-centred justice” remains a benevolent vocabulary for unbounded power.
