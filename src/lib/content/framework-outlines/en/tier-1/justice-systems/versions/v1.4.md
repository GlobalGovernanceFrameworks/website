# Justice Systems Framework v1.4
## Plural Jurisdiction, Due Process, Restoration, and Remedy

**Status:** Constitutional and operational framework draft  
**Revision:** 1.4  
**Date:** 2026-08-01  
**Primary compatibility instruments:** Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3; Constitutional Interface Specification v0.1; Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing v1.1; Integrated Meta-Governance v1.5; Polycentric Governance Architecture v0.6; Genesis Protocol v0.9  
**Related implementation frameworks:** AUBI v2.5; Financial Systems v3.4; Nested Economies v3.2; Work in Liberation v2.0; Aurora Accord; Peace and Conflict Resolution; Shield Protocol; Ecological Intelligence and Rights

> **Justice is the disciplined protection of rightful difference, equal dignity, lawful responsibility, and repair across plural legal orders.**

---

# Preamble

Justice is not exhausted by punishment, nor can it be reduced to reconciliation. It must be capable of determining facts, protecting rights, restraining power, addressing harm, restoring what can be restored, and acknowledging what cannot be repaired. It must remain intelligible to the people subject to it while preserving the independence required to decide unpopular cases fairly.

This framework retains the earlier commitment to accessible, transparent, culturally responsive, trauma-aware, ecologically literate, and restorative justice. It rejects, however, the idea that one global justice institution should develop policy, assign jurisdiction, investigate, adjudicate, sanction, monitor, and evaluate the same matter. Justice cannot credibly constrain concentrated authority while concentrating the full justice chain in itself.

The framework therefore establishes a **plural justice interface architecture**. Domestic courts, Indigenous legal orders, Treaty institutions, compact tribunals, labour and professional bodies, commons institutions, restorative services, and other lawful forums may coexist. Their powers do not arise from the Global Governance Frameworks initiative or from this document. They arise from constitutions, treaties, legislation, Indigenous authority, lawful compacts, contracts, charters, and other valid sources of law.

The framework’s governing rules are:

1. **No framework-to-law conversion.** A principle, policy framework, technical standard, ethical claim, dashboard, or model becomes enforceable only through an independently valid legal act.
2. **No self-assigned jurisdiction.** A court, coordinator, monitor, or justice administrator cannot create jurisdiction by identifying a gap, urgency, or cross-framework conflict.
3. **No combined justice sovereign.** Lawmaking, investigation, prosecution, adjudication, enforcement, administration, and evaluation must remain institutionally distinguishable.
4. **No forced restoration.** Reconciliation, forgiveness, apology, ceremony, disclosure, community harmony, or continued relationship cannot be imposed as conditions of justice.
5. **No denial of remedy as sanction.** Access to an independent tribunal, legal aid, defence, appeal, or emergency protection cannot be withdrawn to compel compliance.
6. **No automated legal status.** AI and metrics may support analysis but cannot determine guilt, credibility, detention, sentence, standing, Indigenous identity, consent, personhood, jurisdiction, or remedy.
7. **No authority without provenance.** Every consequential decision must identify the source, scope, subject, duration, review, and limits of the authority exercised.

# 1. Purpose, status, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

This framework specifies constitutional and operational interfaces for justice across plural legal orders. It defines minimum requirements for:

- identifying valid sources of law;
- determining jurisdiction and applicable law;
- establishing standing and representation;
- separating adjudication, restoration, protection, investigation, enforcement, administration, and evaluation;
- protecting due process and equal access;
- handling evidence, uncertainty, expert disagreement, and protected knowledge;
- designing remedies, appeals, recognition, enforcement, and institutional continuity;
- recognizing Indigenous law and affected-nation authority without integration into a GGF hierarchy;
- governing technology and data use in justice systems;
- first seating, piloting, transfer, amendment, and dissolution.

## 1.2 Non-authority clause

This framework does not itself:

- enact offences or civil liabilities;
- establish jurisdiction over any person, people, territory, institution, commons, or state;
- create a court, prosecutor, police service, enforcement body, tax, sanction, detention power, or compulsory process;
- convert the Moral Operating System or another GGF framework into law;
- constitute or recognize Indigenous authorities on behalf of Indigenous peoples;
- determine the legal status or rights of AI, digital, emergent, planetary, or extraterrestrial entities;
- authorize armed deployment, criminal jurisdiction, asset seizure, or compulsory sanctions;
- override domestic constitutions, Indigenous law, or valid treaty obligations.

A body using this framework must identify an independently valid source of authority for every consequential act.

## 1.3 Applicability

The framework may be adopted, adapted, or referenced by:

- Treaty institutions established under Treaty v1.3;
- states and other public parties;
- Indigenous nations and legal orders on their own terms;
- cities, BAZs, commons institutions, and regional compacts;
- labour, professional, cooperative, and sectoral self-governance bodies;
- arbitral, administrative, restorative, and community justice institutions;
- public-interest and legal-aid organizations.

Adoption does not merge these authorities or place them under a single hierarchy.

## 1.4 Interpretation

Where provisions conflict, the institution must determine the applicable legal hierarchy through a **Source-of-Law and Authority Record**. Ethical commitments may guide interpretation where law permits, but they may not create jurisdiction, offences, sanctions, or compulsory remedies.

# 2. Justice modalities

Different justice functions answer different questions and require different authority.

## 2.1 Adjudication

Adjudication authoritatively determines facts, rights, obligations, liability, legality, jurisdiction, or remedies within valid legal authority. Adjudicators must be independent from investigators, parties, prosecutors, enforcers, and political supervisors in the matter before them.

## 2.2 Mediation

Mediation assists parties in negotiating an agreement. Unless a lawful instrument provides otherwise, mediators cannot impose outcomes. Participation may be voluntary or procedurally required only where urgent protection, limitation periods, evidence preservation, and access to adjudication are not delayed.

## 2.3 Restorative process

A restorative process addresses harm, responsibility, repair, reintegration, recurrence prevention, and relationship where safe and desired. It may accompany or follow adjudication, or operate independently for appropriate matters. It does not replace findings, protection, compensation, acquittal, or criminal process where those are required.

## 2.4 Protective action

Protective action consists of urgent, temporary, proportionate measures to prevent imminent or continuing harm, preserve evidence, secure assets, maintain essential services, or protect the effectiveness of later proceedings. It requires defined legal authority and prompt review.

## 2.5 Investigation

Investigation gathers and tests evidence. Investigators do not determine guilt or final liability. Intrusive investigative acts require lawful authorization and challenge mechanisms.

## 2.6 Prosecution or public enforcement

A prosecutor or public enforcement authority brings cases on behalf of a lawful public order. It must disclose exculpatory material, remain distinct from the adjudicator, and be subject to legality, equality, and non-discrimination constraints.

## 2.7 Enforcement

Enforcement executes lawful orders. Enforcers may not expand, reinterpret, or add penalties to a judgment. Arrest, detention, search, seizure, asset restraint, physical force, and cross-border deployment require separately constituted authority.

## 2.8 Justice administration and access

Administrative bodies may provide registry services, scheduling, interpretation, accessibility, legal aid, case information, public reporting, and recognition assistance. They may not decide cases or condition access on political alignment, contribution records, reputation, or acceptance of restoration.

## 2.9 Public legal education

Legal education may explain rights, procedures, and institutions. It must distinguish binding law from proposals, ethical principles, framework recommendations, and unsettled interpretation.

# 3. Sources of law and authority

## 3.1 Valid sources

A justice institution may act only under one or more identified sources, including:

- constitutional law;
- legislation or valid regulation;
- a ratified treaty and activated protocol;
- Indigenous law or authority identified by the relevant people;
- a lawful intergovernmental, intermunicipal, BAZ, or commons compact;
- a valid charter for a public institution;
- a contract or arbitration agreement;
- labour, cooperative, or professional rules within lawful scope;
- customary law recognized by the relevant legal order;
- emergency law that satisfies constitutional limits.

## 3.2 Frameworks are not law by default

The Treaty, MOS, AUBI, Financial Systems, Work in Liberation, ecological, technological, or other GGF frameworks are not independently enforceable merely because a justice framework references them. A provision becomes legally operative only when a competent authority adopts it through a valid instrument.

A court must not describe a framework recommendation as a legal duty unless the Source-of-Law and Authority Record identifies the adopting instrument and the provision’s legal effect.

## 3.3 Authority provenance

Before accepting a case or issuing a consequential order, the forum must record:

- constituting instrument;
- provision granting authority;
- legal subject and territory;
- decision class;
- temporal scope;
- applicable rights and non-derogable limits;
- review and appeal body;
- enforcement authority;
- unresolved authority objections.

## 3.4 Hierarchy and interface

No universal hierarchy is presumed among domestic, Indigenous, Treaty, compact, and contractual law. Their relationship depends on applicable constitutions, treaties, consent, territorial authority, subject matter, and rights constraints.

Where two valid legal orders overlap, the institution must use the conflict-of-laws process in Section 7 rather than assuming that “global” law automatically prevails.

## 3.5 Ultra vires acts

An act beyond lawful authority is not cured by good intentions, restoration outcomes, public approval, urgency, or technical competence. A competent reviewer may suspend or invalidate the act and order restoration, compensation, record correction, or institutional reform.

# 4. Decision and case classes

Every matter must be assigned a case class before jurisdiction and procedure are determined.

## 4.1 Private and contractual matters

Disputes between persons or entities concerning agreements, property, obligations, or private harms. Arbitration and mediation depend on valid consent and cannot waive non-waivable rights.

## 4.2 Administrative matters

Review of public or delegated administrative decisions, benefits, licensing, regulation, procurement, data processing, and service access.

## 4.3 Constitutional and public-law matters

Challenges concerning institutional competence, rights, separation of powers, emergency authority, electoral process, public finance, or legality of government action.

## 4.4 Treaty matters

Disputes arising under Treaty v1.3 or separately ratified protocols. The base Treaty does not activate coercive functions reserved to additional protocols.

## 4.5 Criminal and quasi-criminal matters

Alleged offences carrying punishment, detention, severe stigma, asset forfeiture, or comparable coercive consequences. Full legality, presumption, defence, proof, and appeal protections apply.

## 4.6 Labour, cooperative, and professional matters

Worker rights, collective agreements, cooperative governance, professional discipline, and practice standards. Professional bodies may regulate membership and practice only within lawful delegated scope and may not replace public courts for non-waivable rights.

## 4.7 Financial and economic matters

Settlement, currency, fraud, fiduciary duty, market conduct, fund governance, and financial regulation. Hearts, Leaves, AUBI, or other instruments are governed by their valid legal and contractual structures, not by obsolete Love Ledger assumptions.

## 4.8 Ecological and commons matters

Harm to ecosystems, shared resources, future conditions, non-human interests, and cross-boundary externalities. Standing and remedies require constituted guardianship and bounded precaution.

## 4.9 Indigenous-jurisdiction and interface matters

Matters concerning Indigenous law, territory, rights, knowledge, culture, persons, or institutions. No GGF body determines Indigenous authority or treats one Indigenous institution as a universal proxy.

## 4.10 Compact and inter-institutional matters

Disputes concerning BAZ, municipal, commons, regional, or functional compacts. Meta-Governance may convene competent authorities but cannot decide legal jurisdiction.

## 4.11 Public-interest and structural matters

Cases alleging widespread, systemic, recurring, or institutionally produced harm. Structural remedies require clear legal authority, affected-party participation, feasibility review, proportionality, and continuing judicial supervision that does not become permanent administration.

## 4.12 Provisional status questions

Claims concerning unrecognized digital, emergent, or non-human entities may justify narrow precautionary protections without determining full legal personhood. Permanent status requires a competent lawmaking or constitutional process, not an ad hoc committee or AI consciousness test.

# 5. Jurisdiction

## 5.1 Bases of jurisdiction

A forum may exercise jurisdiction only where a valid legal source establishes one or more of the following bases:

- territorial jurisdiction;
- personal jurisdiction through nationality, residence, presence, or valid service;
- consent through treaty, compact, contract, or arbitration agreement;
- subject-matter jurisdiction;
- effects jurisdiction for material cross-boundary harm, where law permits;
- protective or universal jurisdiction for narrowly defined offences under ratified law;
- Indigenous jurisdiction grounded in the relevant people’s law and authority;
- appellate or review jurisdiction over a specified lower body.

## 5.2 Jurisdiction is decision-specific

A forum competent to mediate, administer funds, or decide a contract is not thereby competent to determine criminal guilt, constitutional validity, Indigenous authority, or ecological guardianship.

## 5.3 Jurisdictional objections

Parties and affected authorities may challenge jurisdiction at the earliest reasonable stage and later where new facts arise. The decision must state:

- asserted legal basis;
- objections and counterarguments;
- relevant territorial and personal connections;
- applicable consent;
- parallel proceedings;
- rights implications;
- reasons for accepting, narrowing, transferring, or declining the case.

## 5.4 Abstention and no-jurisdiction outcomes

A forum may decline or stay a matter where:

- another competent forum is clearly better placed;
- local or Indigenous remedies should be respected, subject to urgency and rights constraints;
- the dispute is non-justiciable under the constituting instrument;
- essential facts or parties are absent;
- consent is invalid;
- the claimed law has not been activated;
- the forum would need to manufacture jurisdiction to proceed.

A no-jurisdiction finding is a legitimate outcome. It must not trigger institutional expansion.

## 5.5 Transfer and referral

Transfer requires a receiving forum with valid authority, notice to parties, preservation of limitation periods and evidence, data-minimization rules, and an opportunity to contest transfer. Referral does not predetermine liability.

## 5.6 Parallel proceedings

Forums must identify related proceedings, risk of inconsistent judgments, double jeopardy or duplicate sanction, and possibilities for coordination. Coordination does not permit evidence sharing beyond lawful purpose or merge independent legal orders.

## 5.7 Emergency jurisdiction

Urgency does not create jurisdiction. A forum may issue only pre-authorized provisional measures within its lawful scope and must submit them to prompt independent review.

# 6. Standing and representation

## 6.1 Individual standing

A person has standing where they allege a legally cognizable injury, risk, deprivation, obligation, or status under the applicable law. Formal documentation barriers must be adapted for displaced, stateless, low-documentation, disabled, detained, or otherwise marginalized persons.

## 6.2 Collective and associational standing

Groups, unions, cooperatives, communities, civil-society bodies, or public-interest organizations may act where law permits and where representation is accountable to affected persons. A representative must disclose mandate, conflicts, funding, and limits.

## 6.3 Indigenous standing

Indigenous peoples, nations, communities, authorities, and persons determine representation through their own laws and processes. State recognition, GGF validation, ethnicity databases, or universal councils are not prerequisites.

The forum must distinguish:

- collective standing of a people;
- authority of a nation or legal institution;
- rights of individual Indigenous persons;
- internal dissent and plurality;
- overlapping territorial claims;
- diaspora or displaced members;
- non-engagement.

## 6.4 Ecosystem and commons standing

An ecosystem or commons may be represented only through a valid legal instrument identifying:

- the protected entity or system;
- appointing authority;
- guardian duties and independence;
- affected communities and Indigenous standing;
- scientific and Traditional Knowledge interfaces;
- conflicts and removal;
- accountability and remedy.

## 6.5 Future-generations standing

Future interests may be represented by a lawfully constituted guardian, public counsel, or designated institution. Representation does not authorize speculative control over present persons without proportionality, evidence, and rights review.

## 6.6 Digital and emergent entities

Claimed digital or emergent entities may receive provisional evidence-preservation or non-destruction orders where serious irreversible harm is plausible. Standing, personhood, or rights may not be determined by AI-generated consciousness scores, the Moon Wish Test, popularity, or a GGF framework alone.

## 6.7 Accused and respondent rights

Respondents have standing to contest jurisdiction, evidence, interim measures, representation, remedies, and enforcement. Restorative participation cannot be treated as admission, and refusal cannot aggravate liability or sentence.

## 6.8 Amicus and expert participation

Friends of the court and experts may assist without becoming parties or acquiring authority. Funding, affiliations, uncertainty, data access, and conflicts must be disclosed.

# 7. Applicable law and conflict of laws

## 7.1 Applicable-law determination

The forum must identify:

- each potentially applicable legal order;
- the connecting facts;
- hierarchy or interface rules;
- party agreements;
- non-waivable rights;
- public-policy limits;
- Indigenous law and authority;
- Treaty obligations;
- consequences for non-parties.

## 7.2 No automatic global supremacy

Treaty law prevails only to the extent validly ratified, activated, and applicable. A global label does not establish priority over domestic, Indigenous, compact, or local law.

## 7.3 Indigenous law interfaces

Where Indigenous law applies or Indigenous rights are affected, the forum must seek an interface agreement or use an existing one. It must not translate sacred, restricted, oral, or relational law into open legal databases without authority.

## 7.4 Non-derogable standards

No choice-of-law clause, arbitration agreement, compact, restorative agreement, or local custom may waive non-derogable rights established by the applicable constitutional order.

## 7.5 Exhaustion and subsidiarity

Local remedies may be required where effective, accessible, independent, timely, and safe. Exhaustion is not required where remedies are unavailable, futile, discriminatory, dangerously delayed, or would require surrender of protected knowledge or rights.

## 7.6 Recognition of plural outcomes

Different legal orders may reach different outcomes. The framework does not require forced synthesis. Recognition depends on jurisdiction, due process, public policy, rights, and the enforcement rules in Section 20.

# 8. Institutional separation

## 8.1 No Global Justice Oversight Body

The v1.3 Global Justice Oversight Body is retired. No successor body may simultaneously make policy, assign jurisdiction, adjudicate disputes, impose sanctions, monitor compliance, and evaluate its own performance.

## 8.2 Treaty judicial bodies

A Treaty tribunal or court may be established only by Treaty v1.3 or a separately ratified Justice Protocol. Its jurisdiction must be enumerated. It may not become a general court for every GGF framework dispute.

## 8.3 Justice Access and Administration Secretariat

A non-adjudicative secretariat may provide:

- registry and filing support;
- interpretation and accessibility;
- legal-aid administration;
- public information;
- recognition and enforcement assistance;
- statistical reporting;
- secure records services.

It cannot assign substantive jurisdiction, decide merits, impose sanctions, direct prosecutors, or evaluate judges in individual cases.

## 8.4 Appointments and Conduct Commission

An independent commission may administer nominations, qualification review, conflict checks, complaints, and discipline under law. It does not direct judgments or evaluate ideology, personality, humility performance, or popularity.

## 8.5 Public Counsel and Legal Aid Office

Public counsel supports persons, groups, ecosystems, future interests, and public-interest cases where law provides. Funding decisions must be independent from political approval and adverse-case outcomes.

## 8.6 Restorative Justice Services

Restorative services remain separate from courts and prosecutors. They may receive referrals but cannot pressure participation, report confidential discussions as admissions, or deny return to adjudication.

## 8.7 Investigation and prosecution bodies

Investigators and prosecutors are constituted separately under applicable law. They cannot sit as judges, control court administration, or determine final remedies.

## 8.8 Enforcement bodies

Enforcement bodies execute lawful orders and remain subject to judicial review, use-of-force law, rights protections, and misconduct investigation.

## 8.9 Independent evaluation

Researchers and institutional evaluators may assess access, delay, implementation, and outcomes. They cannot invalidate judgments, remove officials, pause proceedings, or determine legal status through metrics.

# 9. Judicial appointment and independence

## 9.1 Constituting law

Each judicial institution must have a lawful charter specifying jurisdiction, composition, qualifications, appointment, term, remuneration, recusal, discipline, removal, budget, administration, appeal, and dissolution.

## 9.2 Qualification

Criteria may include legal competence, adjudicative experience, rights literacy, intercultural capacity, ecological or technical expertise where relevant, integrity, and ability to provide reasoned decisions. Criteria must be published and accessible.

No candidate may be rejected through a vague “authenticity,” “wisdom,” developmental-stage, or humility test.

## 9.3 Nomination and confirmation

Nomination channels may be plural, but confirmation authority must be legally defined. Indigenous authorities participate only where they choose and where Indigenous jurisdiction or the constituting agreement gives them a role; no universal Indigenous body is presumed.

## 9.4 Terms and continuity

Terms should balance independence, renewal, and institutional memory. Universal four-year maximums are not required. Short rotation can make judges vulnerable to political pressure and destroy expertise. Non-renewable or staggered terms may be appropriate depending on the institution.

## 9.5 Recusal

Judges must disclose relevant financial, familial, professional, political, and institutional conflicts. Parties may seek recusal through an independent process. Disagreement with a judge’s legal reasoning is not itself misconduct.

## 9.6 Discipline and removal

Discipline requires defined misconduct, evidence, notice, response, impartial decision, reasons, and review. “Authority-centred behaviour,” low satisfaction, insufficient humility, or resistance to popular feedback are not standalone grounds.

## 9.7 Budgetary independence

Judicial budgets must be protected from retaliation for unpopular decisions while remaining auditable. Funding cannot depend on conviction rates, settlement rates, restoration scores, case volume, revenue collected, or political compliance.

## 9.8 Administrative independence

Case assignment must use transparent, contestable rules. Executives, funders, prosecutors, Meta-Governance bodies, Guardians, and framework administrators may not assign judges to obtain preferred outcomes.

# 10. Due-process bill

The following minimum protections apply according to the seriousness of the proceeding.

## 10.1 Legality and notice

No person may be held liable or punished under a rule that was not validly applicable and sufficiently knowable at the relevant time. Parties receive timely notice of claims, authority, facts, evidence, possible consequences, and procedural rights.

## 10.2 Independent and impartial tribunal

The decision-maker must be legally constituted, independent, unbiased, and free from prior involvement in investigation, prosecution, mediation, or enforcement of the same matter.

## 10.3 Equality and access

Parties receive reasonable equality of arms, including interpretation, disability accommodation, communication support, time, access to evidence, and legal assistance where needed.

## 10.4 Counsel and assistance

Criminal, detention, severe-sanction, complex public-law, and other high-stakes cases require access to qualified counsel. Community advocates, support persons, and cultural or disability liaisons may participate without replacing legal representation where counsel is necessary.

## 10.5 Presumption, burden, and proof

Applicable law must identify:

- who bears each burden;
- the standard of proof;
- presumptions and rebuttal;
- treatment of silence;
- adverse inferences;
- evidentiary exclusions.

Criminal guilt requires the highest applicable standard and cannot be inferred from refusal to participate in restoration.

## 10.6 Disclosure

Parties receive relevant evidence, including exculpatory or materially adverse information, subject to narrowly tailored protection for safety, privacy, security, and protected knowledge. Secret evidence requires exceptional safeguards and cannot be the sole basis for severe consequences without meaningful challenge.

## 10.7 Hearing and participation

Parties may present evidence, question adverse evidence, make legal arguments, and respond to proposed remedies. Oral, written, remote, accessible, culturally appropriate, and low-technology formats may be used without sacrificing fairness.

## 10.8 Reasons and uncertainty

Consequential decisions must state:

- jurisdiction and applicable law;
- findings of fact;
- evidentiary assessment;
- legal reasoning;
- remedies;
- uncertainty and unresolved issues where material;
- dissent where applicable;
- appeal and compliance routes.

Plain-language summaries accompany, but do not replace, the legally operative decision.

## 10.9 Timeliness

Delay can deny justice. Institutions must publish time standards while preserving sufficient time for preparation. Missed administrative targets do not automatically decide a case or lower proof standards.

## 10.10 Appeal and review

Parties must receive the review rights established in Section 18. A final judgment cannot be made unreviewable merely because the forum uses restorative, community, digital, or emergency procedures.

# 11. Evidence and epistemic plurality

## 11.1 Evidence provenance

Every material item should record, where applicable:

- source and creator;
- collection authority;
- time and location;
- chain of custody;
- transformations or translations;
- known gaps;
- access restrictions;
- authenticity disputes;
- model or software involvement;
- correction history.

## 11.2 Admissibility and weight

Applicable law determines admissibility. Reliability, relevance, prejudice, legality of acquisition, privacy, protected knowledge, and ability to challenge must be considered separately. Admission does not establish truth; exclusion does not imply the underlying event did not occur.

## 11.3 Expert evidence

Experts must disclose qualifications, methods, assumptions, data, uncertainty, conflicts, and institutional affiliations. The tribunal should permit competing expertise and explain why it accepts, limits, or rejects an opinion.

No discipline, model, institution, or AI system has a monopoly on interpretation.

## 11.4 Scientific and ecological uncertainty

Scientific uncertainty must be stated rather than converted into false precision. Precaution may support temporary protection where potential harm is serious and irreversible, but permanent liability or punishment still requires the proof standard established by law.

## 11.5 Traditional Knowledge and protected non-transfer

Traditional, sacred, restricted, oral, relational, or community-controlled knowledge may be presented through:

- bounded testimony;
- authorized knowledge holders;
- closed or partially closed proceedings;
- community-defined summaries;
- independent attestation;
- proof of authority without disclosure of substance;
- sealed records under Indigenous control.

No forum may compel public recording merely because open evidence is administratively convenient. Refusal to disclose protected knowledge does not establish that the knowledge is invalid.

## 11.6 Digital evidence

Digital evidence requires authentication, integrity review, context, and contestability. Metadata, automated logs, biometric matches, platform records, and blockchain entries may be incomplete or misleading. An “immutable” entry is not self-proving.

## 11.7 Translation and interpretation

Machine or human translation must be reviewable. Material ambiguity, untranslatable concepts, cultural context, and interpreter disagreement must be documented. A translated legal concept must not silently replace a distinct Indigenous or local legal meaning.

## 11.8 AI assistance

AI may support research, document organization, translation drafts, accessibility, anomaly detection, and non-binding scenario analysis. The AI-Assisted Justice Function Record must disclose:

- purpose;
- system and version;
- inputs and prohibited data;
- operator;
- error and bias evidence;
- human review;
- contestability;
- retention;
- effect on the decision.

AI may not determine credibility, guilt, risk of reoffending, detention, sentence, standing, Indigenous identity, FPIC, sentience, jurisdiction, sanction, or remedy.

## 11.9 Illegally obtained evidence

The forum must apply the governing exclusion, proportionality, and remedy rules. Evidence gained through torture, coercion, unlawful surveillance, prohibited extraction of protected knowledge, or serious rights violation requires especially strong exclusion and accountability safeguards.

# 12. Restorative justice protocol

## 12.1 Status

Restorative justice is an important justice modality, not the universal definition of justice. It may seek acknowledgment, repair, restitution, safety, reintegration, institutional change, and recurrence prevention.

## 12.2 Consent

Participation must be informed, specific, revocable, and free from improper pressure. Refusal must not:

- imply guilt;
- reduce access to court;
- aggravate sentence or remedy;
- reduce AUBI, services, legal aid, or rights;
- affect immigration, employment, housing, or reputation outside lawful rules.

## 12.3 Suitability and safety

Before referral, an independent suitability assessment should consider:

- continuing violence or coercive control;
- power and resource imbalance;
- ability to participate freely;
- trauma and psychological safety;
- child or dependent-party interests;
- risk of retaliation;
- evidentiary and limitation deadlines;
- public-interest need for authoritative findings;
- seriousness and recurrence;
- community pressure;
- accessibility and cultural appropriateness.

Restorative processes are presumptively unsuitable where a party cannot safely refuse or where the process would conceal ongoing serious harm.

## 12.4 Facilitator independence

Facilitators must be trained, conflict-free, culturally competent for the particular process, and accountable under defined standards. They do not determine legal guilt or provide legal advice unless separately qualified and authorized.

## 12.5 Confidentiality and evidentiary use

The law or participation agreement must specify confidentiality, mandatory reporting, threats, new offences, admissions, records, and exceptions. Statements made in a restorative process should not be used in adjudication without valid consent or a clearly applicable legal exception.

## 12.6 Relationship to adjudication

A case may:

- remain in adjudication while limited restorative work proceeds;
- be stayed without losing urgent protection or limitation rights;
- return to adjudication after withdrawal or failure;
- use an agreement as a proposed remedy subject to judicial review;
- continue to judgment where public findings or protection are needed.

## 12.7 Agreements

A restorative agreement must be voluntary, lawful, feasible, proportionate, understandable, time-bounded where appropriate, and protective of third parties. It cannot require forced labour, sacred disclosure, surrender of non-waivable rights, exile, degrading treatment, ideological confession, or reconciliation.

## 12.8 Success and failure

Success is not measured solely by reconciliation or satisfaction. Valid outcomes include:

- repair without reconciliation;
- safe separation;
- acknowledgment without forgiveness;
- partial restitution;
- institutional reform;
- return to court;
- a decision that restoration is inappropriate.

Failure of restoration is not failure of justice.

# 13. Indigenous law and affected-nation interfaces

## 13.1 Recognition without creation

Indigenous jurisdiction, law, and authority arise from the relevant peoples, not from this framework, a state integration scheme, a BAZ designation, or a GGF council.

## 13.2 No default integration

Indigenous tribunals are not “national implementation units,” regional branches, cultural advisers, or sources of legitimacy for unrelated Treaty institutions. Relationships require nation-specific treaties, compacts, recognition rules, or interface agreements.

## 13.3 Interface record

For a matter involving Indigenous law, territory, people, knowledge, culture, or sacred relations, the Indigenous Jurisdiction and Affected-Nation Interface Record should identify:

- affected people or peoples;
- authorities identified by them;
- applicable laws and treaties;
- territorial and personal scope;
- individual and collective rights;
- internal plurality or contested authority;
- consent, refusal, or non-engagement;
- knowledge restrictions;
- procedural forms;
- mediator or forum selection;
- enforcement and appeal;
- unresolved boundaries.

## 13.4 Affected-nation consent

Consent is decision-specific. Participation by one Indigenous nation or transnational network does not authorize decisions for another. State accession to the Treaty does not constitute Indigenous consent.

## 13.5 Individual rights and internal plurality

Recognition of collective authority must coexist with the rights and safety of individuals. Forums must not romanticize community harmony, presume unanimity, or dismiss women, youth, LGBTQ+ persons, disabled persons, dissenters, displaced members, or other internal constituencies.

## 13.6 Knowledge and evidence

Indigenous authorities determine whether and how protected knowledge may enter proceedings. Courts may use bounded attestation and other protected non-transfer methods. Knowledge participation is not a duty owed to the GGF.

## 13.7 Cross-jurisdiction disputes

Where domestic, Treaty, or compact law overlaps with Indigenous jurisdiction, the parties should establish a joint or coordinated process only with lawful authority and consent. Possible outcomes include:

- one forum proceeding;
- parallel proceedings;
- issue-specific referral;
- jointly appointed panel;
- mutual recognition;
- refusal of recognition;
- unresolved jurisdiction with temporary protective arrangements.

No forced synthesis is required.

# 14. Ecological, collective, and future-generations justice

## 14.1 Source of rights and duties

Rights of nature, ecosystem personhood, public trust duties, planetary-boundary obligations, ecocide offences, and future-generation duties must be grounded in valid law. The Moral Operating System’s rights tiers are not independently enforceable.

## 14.2 Guardianship

A guardian must act for the defined ecological or future interest rather than for the appointing institution. The constituting instrument must specify selection, expertise, Indigenous and affected-community interfaces, conflicts, funding, reporting, removal, and accountability.

## 14.3 Causation and cumulative harm

Law may recognize cumulative, probabilistic, systemic, and long-latency causation. The applicable standard must be explicit. Courts should distinguish:

- causal contribution;
- material risk creation;
- regulatory breach;
- strict liability;
- criminal intent or recklessness;
- collective and historical responsibility.

## 14.4 Precaution

Precaution can justify narrow interim protection where:

- potential harm is grave or irreversible;
- evidence is credible though incomplete;
- delay materially increases risk;
- measures are proportionate and reviewable;
- less restrictive alternatives are considered;
- innocent-party and livelihood impacts are addressed.

Precaution does not automatically establish final liability or authorize unlimited prohibition.

## 14.5 Remedies

Ecological remedies may include cessation, restoration, monitoring, habitat repair, restitution, compensation, community transition support, structural reform, and long-term stewardship. Courts must avoid remedies that protect ecosystems by transferring disproportionate costs to workers, subsistence communities, or Indigenous peoples who did not cause the harm.

## 14.6 Intergenerational representation

Future-generation claims must state the time horizon, affected system, evidence, uncertainty, present rights impacts, and review mechanism. “Future generations” cannot become a blank mandate for present technocratic rule.

---

# 15. Digital systems, AI, and claimed emergent entities

## 15.1 Technology neutrality

No blockchain, central global database, biometric identity, universal digital wallet, or single platform is required. Institutions may use federated, paper, local, encrypted, or other lawful systems appropriate to risk and access.

## 15.2 Prohibited automated uses

No automated system may independently:

- accept or reject jurisdiction;
- determine standing or legal identity;
- assess credibility or deception;
- predict guilt or liability;
- recommend detention or sentence;
- classify Indigenous identity or authority;
- determine consent;
- establish AI consciousness or personhood;
- impose sanctions or remedies;
- rank judges or parties by ideology, humility, or reputation.

## 15.3 Contestability

A person materially affected by an automated aid must be informed where lawful, receive an understandable account of its role, challenge inputs and outputs, and obtain meaningful human reconsideration.

## 15.4 Digital identity

Participation and access cannot depend on Love Ledger identity, contribution scores, generalized reputation, or permanent global identifiers. Identity systems must support privacy, pseudonymity where lawful, stateless and displaced persons, low documentation, correction, and appeal.

## 15.5 Claimed digital sentience

An Exceptional Cases Committee with proprietary consciousness tests is not created. A competent court may address a concrete dispute under existing law and issue reversible protective measures. Broader legal status belongs to a legitimate legislative, treaty, or constitutional process informed by plural evidence.

## 15.6 Cybersecurity

Security controls must protect confidentiality, integrity, availability, correction, and continuity. Cybersecurity cannot justify indefinite secrecy, unchallengeable evidence, or exclusion of offline participants.

---

# 16. Interim measures

## 16.1 Purpose

Interim measures preserve safety, evidence, assets, rights, ecosystems, essential services, or the effectiveness of final judgment. They are not preliminary punishment.

## 16.2 Threshold

The applicant must show the standard required by law, including as relevant:

- plausible or prima facie right;
- urgency;
- serious or irreparable harm;
- connection between measure and harm;
- proportionality;
- balance of impacts;
- jurisdiction or provisional jurisdiction.

## 16.3 Ex parte action

Orders without prior hearing are exceptional. They require strict necessity, narrow scope, short duration, prompt notice, rapid adversarial review, and compensation where wrongfully imposed.

## 16.4 Protected continuity

Interim measures must protect, as applicable:

- life and physical safety;
- AUBI Layer 1 and equivalent material minima;
- personal balances and lawful wages;
- essential health, water, food, housing, energy, and communication services;
- legal representation and access to court;
- worker and dependent-party interests;
- protected knowledge and data;
- ecological thresholds.

## 16.5 Asset restraint

Asset freezing must identify legal authority, property, beneficial ownership evidence, exceptions for ordinary living and essential operations, duration, challenge, review, and remedy for wrongful restraint.

## 16.6 Review and expiration

Every interim order must state its expiration, review date, evidence requirements, and pathway to modification or dissolution. Emergency continuation cannot become indefinite through repeated administrative renewal.

---

# 17. Remedies

## 17.1 General principles

Remedies must be lawful, targeted, proportionate, reasoned, reviewable, feasible, and attentive to third parties and continuity. The objective may include protection, declaration, cessation, repair, compensation, restoration, deterrence, accountability, and recurrence prevention.

## 17.2 Available remedies

Depending on law, remedies may include:

- declaration of rights or illegality;
- injunction or cease-and-desist order;
- restitution and return of property;
- compensation and damages;
- correction, deletion, expungement, or restoration of records;
- specific performance;
- reinstatement;
- public-law remand or reconsideration;
- institutional or structural relief;
- ecological restoration;
- restorative agreement;
- civil penalty or criminal sentence under activated law;
- costs and legal aid recovery;
- guarantees of non-repetition;
- monitoring for a defined period.

## 17.3 No access restriction as punishment

A remedy cannot remove access to independent adjudication, appeal, legal aid, emergency protection, or basic defence. Restricted access to optional commercial arbitration may occur only under ordinary contractual and regulatory law, never as retaliation for noncompliance with the GGF.

## 17.4 Sanctions and innocent parties

Sanctions must identify the responsible legal subject and avoid generalized harm. Essential services, workers, children, dependants, refugees, non-party communities, and individual material security require protection.

## 17.5 Structural remedies

Structural relief must define:

- legal violation;
- objectives;
- responsible institution;
- milestones without outcome manipulation;
- affected-party participation;
- budget and feasibility;
- independent monitoring;
- duration and exit;
- review and appeal;
- consequences of unintended harm.

The court should not permanently administer an institution.

## 17.6 Wrongful judicial or enforcement action

Law must provide correction, compensation, release, record repair, property return, apology where appropriate, institutional investigation, and guarantees of non-repetition for wrongful detention, seizure, judgment, exclusion, or data use.

## 17.7 Remedy and continuity record

Every consequential remedy should specify affected rights, beneficiaries, burdened parties, third-party effects, essential-service protections, implementation authority, monitoring, review, restoration, and end condition.

---

# 18. Appeals, review, reopening, and correction

## 18.1 Appeal rights

The constituting law must define who may appeal, which decisions are appealable, deadlines, standard of review, record, new evidence, stay, and available outcomes.

## 18.2 Standards of review

Possible standards include correctness, legality, reasonableness, abuse of discretion, procedural fairness, and clear factual error. The appellate body must state the applicable standard rather than substituting its preferences without authority.

## 18.3 Interlocutory review

Urgent review should be available for jurisdiction, detention, severe interim measures, privilege, protected knowledge, and other decisions that could cause irreparable harm before final judgment.

## 18.4 Reopening

A final matter may be reopened under defined grounds such as new decisive evidence, fraud, serious procedural injustice, wrongful conviction, concealed conflict, invalid jurisdiction, or authoritative change in law where retroactivity permits.

## 18.5 Correction and precedent

Courts may correct clerical errors and publish amended reasons with a visible history. Precedent can evolve through lawful appellate or interpretive processes. Annual performance review cannot silently rewrite final judgments or vested rights.

## 18.6 Restorative agreements

Review may address consent, legality, safety, feasibility, third-party harm, and non-waivable rights. Courts should respect valid agreements without treating settlement as evidence that no legal violation occurred.

---

# 19. Investigation, prosecution, and enforcement handoffs

## 19.1 Handoff principle

A handoff transfers a defined function and evidence; it does not transfer guilt, jurisdiction, or unlimited data access.

## 19.2 Investigation authorization

Search, surveillance, interception, compelled disclosure, forensic access, and covert operations require valid law, necessity, proportionality, minimization, authorization, logging, and review.

## 19.3 Warrants and judicial orders

Warrants must identify object, place, person, offence or legal basis, evidence threshold, duration, execution rules, data limits, protected material, return, and challenge.

## 19.4 Chain of custody

Investigators document collection, handling, duplication, analysis, transfer, access, and alteration. Cross-border evidence must satisfy the receiving forum’s legality and reliability requirements.

## 19.5 Prosecutorial independence

Prosecutors must not receive case-specific commands from the Justice Access Secretariat, Meta-Governance, Guardians, funders, political bodies, or enforcement services.

## 19.6 Shield Protocol interface

The Shield Protocol may investigate or enforce only under separately valid authority. Referral requires an Investigation and Enforcement Handoff Record. Arrest, seizure, detention, asset action, and force remain subject to warrants, rights, review, disclosure, and remedy.

The Council of Guardians does not supervise operations or replace judicial review.

## 19.7 Peace and mediation interface

Mediation failure after a fixed number of sessions does not itself create court jurisdiction. A party may seek adjudication whenever applicable law permits. Mediation cannot delay urgent protection, criminal reporting duties, evidence preservation, or limitation periods.

## 19.8 Classified and sensitive information

Restrictions require legal authority, necessity, independent review, and maximum possible disclosure. Severe consequences cannot rest solely on evidence the affected party cannot meaningfully challenge.

## 19.9 Return pathway

After investigation or enforcement, the matter returns to the competent forum for adjudication or remedy. Enforcement bodies cannot settle legal liability through operational conclusions.

---

# 20. Recognition and enforcement of judgments

## 20.1 Recognition

A judgment may receive cross-border effect under treaty, legislation, compact, comity, or reciprocal arrangement. Recognition is distinct from agreement with the merits.

## 20.2 Recognition record

The receiving forum should identify:

- issuing authority and jurisdiction;
- finality;
- notice and participation;
- due process;
- applicable recognition instrument;
- public-policy and rights objections;
- Indigenous-jurisdiction implications;
- non-party effects;
- available appeal or review.

## 20.3 Refusal grounds

Recognition may be refused or narrowed for:

- lack of jurisdiction;
- fraud;
- denial of due process;
- manifest conflict with non-derogable rights;
- incompatible prior judgment;
- invalid or coerced consent;
- punishment for protected identity or conduct;
- violation of Indigenous jurisdiction or protected knowledge;
- remedy beyond the issuing body’s authority.

## 20.4 Enforcement assistance

Enforcement must use lawful domestic or Treaty channels. A judgment does not authorize foreign enforcement personnel to act directly without agreement.

## 20.5 Immunity and public assets

State, diplomatic, Indigenous, public, ecological-trust, and essential-service assets require applicable immunity and public-interest analysis. Immunity rules must not become impunity, but neither may courts ignore legal limits because a claim is morally compelling.

---

# 21. Access, counsel, language, disability, trauma, and child safeguards

## 21.1 Universal access objective

Access means the practical ability to understand, enter, participate in, challenge, and obtain a remedy from a lawful process. Digital availability alone is insufficient.

## 21.2 Legal aid

Legal aid should prioritize severity, vulnerability, complexity, equality of arms, public interest, and inability to pay. It must not depend on political alignment, reputation, cooperation with investigators, or willingness to reconcile.

## 21.3 Language

Parties have access to competent interpretation and translation appropriate to the proceeding. Plain-language summaries should be available, but the operative legal record must remain precise.

## 21.4 Disability access

Institutions provide reasonable accommodations, accessible formats, communication supports, sensory and cognitive adaptations, physical access, remote or in-person alternatives, and sufficient time. Disability cannot be treated as unreliability or incapacity without individualized evidence and safeguards.

## 21.5 Trauma-informed process

Trauma-informed practice includes predictability, choice, safety, support, pacing, privacy, and avoidance of unnecessary repetition. It does not mean abandoning cross-examination, factual testing, or the respondent’s rights; procedures should be adapted proportionately.

## 21.6 Children and young persons

The best interests, evolving capacities, privacy, participation, counsel, guardianship conflicts, education, family safety, and long-term consequences require specific protection. Children are not instruments of intergenerational symbolism.

## 21.7 Stateless, displaced, detained, and low-documentation persons

Access cannot depend solely on citizenship, permanent address, national digital identity, or conventional records. Alternative verification and representative pathways must be available.

## 21.8 Material support

Travel, communication, childcare, personal assistance, safe lodging, subsistence, and time away from work may determine whether formal rights are usable. Justice-access budgets should address these barriers.

---

# 22. Data, records, transparency, and protected knowledge

## 22.1 Open justice and its limits

Public proceedings and reasoned decisions support accountability, but universal livestreaming is not required. Privacy, safety, children, sexual violence, medical information, trade secrets, national security under law, protected Indigenous knowledge, restorative confidentiality, and rehabilitation may justify limits.

## 22.2 Data minimization

Collect only data necessary for lawful purposes. Case administration, adjudication, research, enforcement, and public reporting should use separated access and retention rules.

## 22.3 Record integrity and correction

Integrity means a verifiable history, not permanent public exposure. Systems must support:

- correction;
- annotation;
- sealed amendments;
- expungement;
- lawful deletion;
- rehabilitation;
- data return;
- protected archival retention;
- audit of access and changes.

## 22.4 No mandatory blockchain

Blockchain may be used only where its characteristics fit the legal purpose. Immutability, global replication, and public traceability may conflict with justice rights. Technology-neutral standards govern authenticity and audit.

## 22.5 Public reporting

Published statistics should protect privacy, distinguish legal validity from service performance, disclose missing data and uncertainty, and avoid rankings that pressure judges or stigmatize communities.

## 22.6 Protected non-transfer

Sacred, restricted, personal, security-sensitive, confidential, sealed, or otherwise protected information may remain outside shared systems. Institutional memory does not override lawful non-transfer.

## 22.7 Data sovereignty

Indigenous data remains governed by the relevant people’s authority. Parties and communities should receive clear control, access, correction, retention, and exit terms for data generated through pilots and proceedings.

## 22.8 Research access

Research use requires lawful basis, ethics, minimization, community and participant protections, and separation from live adjudication. Researchers do not receive privileged access because the framework values learning.

---

# 23. Funding and administrative independence

## 23.1 Lawful revenue

Justice institutions may be funded through lawful public appropriations, assessed contributions, grants, fees, or other authorized sources. This framework does not activate global taxes, carbon levies, financial transaction taxes, or a fixed annual budget.

## 23.2 Judicial independence

Funds must be sufficient, stable, transparent, and insulated from case-specific retaliation. Executives, donors, litigants, and corporations cannot condition support on outcomes, doctrine, appointments, or procurement.

## 23.3 Fees

Fees must not prevent access. Criminal defence, urgent rights protection, constitutional review, and essential legal aid cannot depend on ability to pay. Progressive or waived fees may apply under law.

## 23.4 Legal aid and public counsel

Legal aid requires a protected budget and independent administration. It should not be funded primarily through fines whose collection creates incentives for punishment.

## 23.5 Procurement

Technology and service procurement must be competitive, transparent, interoperable, secure, and free from vendor lock-in. Vendors receive no data rights, standard-setting authority, or future constitutional role.

## 23.6 No universal allocation ratios

The earlier 40/30/20/10 allocation is retired. Budgets should follow legal mandates, access needs, risk, local capacity, and independent review. Technology spending is not presumed to deserve thirty percent.

## 23.7 No restoration-per-dollar metric

Financial stewardship may evaluate cost, access, delay, and outcomes, but “restoration achieved per dollar” cannot direct judgments or penalize hard cases, unpopular rights claims, acquittals, or safe separation.

## 23.8 Continuity

Funding plans must protect active cases, legal aid, records, staff obligations, and essential access during transfer, budget crisis, withdrawal, or dissolution.

---

# 24. Learning, evaluation, and institutional conduct

## 24.1 Learning without control

Justice institutions should learn from error, delay, exclusion, reversal, implementation failure, and unintended harm. Learning processes do not alter legal validity automatically.

## 24.2 Evaluation dimensions

Evaluate separately:

- access and affordability;
- timeliness;
- procedural fairness;
- reversal and correction;
- remedy implementation;
- recurrence and safety;
- restorative participation and refusal;
- language and disability access;
- legal-aid coverage;
- data protection;
- judicial independence;
- user understanding;
- third-party and continuity effects.

No composite justice-health score controls jurisdiction or funding automatically.

## 24.3 Satisfaction

Satisfaction may reveal accessibility, dignity, communication, or implementation problems. It cannot establish whether a judgment is legally correct. Courts must sometimes protect rights against majority dissatisfaction.

## 24.4 Institutional conduct

Conduct standards should address corruption, conflict, discrimination, abuse, harassment, dishonesty, improper communication, undue delay, breach of confidentiality, retaliation, and interference with independence.

Vague traits such as self-glorification, ego, insufficient humility, mystification, or attachment to office may inform organizational reflection but cannot by themselves support discipline.

## 24.5 Public failure reporting

Institutions may publish anonymized learning reports on reversals, access failures, wrongful action, and implementation problems. Publication must respect privacy, finality, pending cases, and protected knowledge.

## 24.6 Community input

Communities may propose reforms, describe harm, evaluate access, and participate in rulemaking where authorized. They do not vote on individual guilt, legal rights, or removal of judges outside valid procedures.

## 24.7 Independent review

Evaluators publish methods, funding, limitations, data access, uncertainty, and dissent. Recommendations require adoption by competent authorities; evaluators do not pause courts or rewrite mandates.

---

# 25. Transition, pilots, first seating, and non-activation

## 25.1 Genesis alignment

Justice institutions arise only after valid authority under Genesis Protocol v0.9, Treaty v1.3, domestic law, Indigenous law, compact, or contract. A successful restorative or digital-access pilot does not create jurisdiction.

## 25.2 Permissible pre-constitutional pilots

Under existing lawful authority, pilots may test:

- legal-aid delivery;
- plain-language summaries;
- interpretation and disability access;
- voluntary restorative services;
- secure records and correction;
- bounded ecological evidence processes;
- judicial learning and retrospectives;
- community legal education.

## 25.3 Pilot prohibitions

A pilot may not without valid law:

- impose sanctions;
- compel participation;
- determine criminal guilt;
- detain, search, seize, or freeze assets;
- claim global or Treaty jurisdiction;
- integrate Indigenous tribunals;
- determine AI personhood;
- use AUBI or services as leverage;
- convert participation into Treaty consent.

## 25.4 First seating

Each new institution requires a Justice Institution First-Seating Record identifying:

- constituting instrument;
- activated jurisdiction;
- appointing and confirming authorities;
- nomination and qualification process;
- independence and conflicts;
- budget and administration;
- access and legal aid;
- records and technology;
- appeal and enforcement interfaces;
- start date and transitional cases.

## 25.5 Coercive-power non-activation

The register must show whether the following are inactive, partially active, or active under a specified instrument:

- criminal jurisdiction;
- compulsory sanctions;
- detention;
- search and seizure;
- asset restraint or forfeiture;
- armed or police deployment;
- compulsory Treaty revenue;
- cross-border enforcement;
- permanent legal-status determination.

Silence means inactive.

## 25.6 Scaling

Coverage targets, numbers of participating states, digital uptake, satisfaction, or pilot success do not establish legitimacy. Scaling follows lawful accession, demonstrated capacity, rights performance, and local adaptation.

## 25.7 Failure can stop implementation

A pilot or review may conclude that:

- the proposed forum lacks authority;
- restoration should remain voluntary;
- technology should not scale;
- a jurisdictional interface is unresolved;
- coercive powers should remain inactive;
- an institution should be redesigned or dissolved.

# 26. Amendment, withdrawal, dissolution, and continuity

## 26.1 Amendment

Treaty judicial powers and institutions are amended only under Treaty v1.3 and any ratified Justice Protocol. Domestic, Indigenous, compact, contractual, and administrative bodies follow their own valid amendment rules.

Evaluation reports, annual reviews, community satisfaction, or Regional Justice Hubs cannot amend law automatically.

## 26.2 No automatic legal sunset

Review dates do not automatically terminate rights, offences, jurisdiction, final judgments, or procedural guarantees. Temporary institutions and pilot rules may sunset where their constituting instrument says so.

## 26.3 Withdrawal

Withdrawal from a compact, court, arbitration arrangement, or Treaty relationship follows the governing law. It does not erase accrued rights, valid judgments, compensation, confidentiality, evidence preservation, or other surviving duties.

Withdrawal cannot be punished by removing basic legal protection, AUBI, personal balances, essential services, or access to independent remedy.

## 26.4 Transfer

Transfer of jurisdiction, cases, assets, data, staff, or records requires lawful authority, notice, continuity, conflict review, access protection, and an appeal or objection route.

## 26.5 Dissolution

A justice institution may dissolve when its mandate ends, authority is withdrawn, replacement is lawfully constituted, or failure warrants closure. Dissolution requires:

- final case disposition or transfer;
- legal-aid continuity;
- record custody and protected deletion;
- asset and liability reconciliation;
- staff and contractor obligations;
- enforcement and monitoring handoff;
- public report and unresolved issues;
- no automatic successor centre.

## 26.6 Continuity plan

Every institution must maintain a Justice Institution Transfer or Dissolution Continuity Plan. Courts cannot threaten loss of rights or evidence to preserve their own existence.

---

# 27. Conformance requirements

An institution claiming conformance with v1.4 must demonstrate:

## 27.1 P0 constitutional conformance

- explicit source of law and authority;
- bounded jurisdiction;
- standing and applicable-law rules;
- separation of adjudication, investigation, enforcement, administration, and evaluation;
- due-process bill;
- evidence and protected non-transfer rules;
- appeal and remedy;
- voluntary and safe restorative process;
- Indigenous recognition without integration;
- AI prohibitions;
- lawful first seating and non-activation of undelegated coercion.

Failure of a P0 requirement means the institution must not exercise the affected compulsory power.

## 27.2 P1 operational conformance

- access and legal aid;
- language and disability accommodation;
- interim-measure safeguards;
- recognition and enforcement procedures;
- funding independence;
- conduct and complaint systems;
- data correction and deletion;
- continuity and dissolution planning.

## 27.3 P2 learning conformance

- independent evaluation;
- transparent limitations;
- outcome review separated from legal validity;
- pilot withdrawal and non-scaling pathways;
- publication of unresolved questions and dissent.

## 27.4 Falsification gates

The architecture is not ready where any of the following remain possible:

1. a framework rule is enforced without valid law;
2. a body assigns itself jurisdiction;
3. one body makes policy, prosecutes, adjudicates, sanctions, and evaluates;
4. access to remedy is withdrawn as a sanction;
5. restoration is coerced;
6. satisfaction validates a judgment;
7. Indigenous legal orders are integrated by default;
8. ceremony is compulsory;
9. AI determines legal status or liability;
10. records cannot be corrected or lawfully deleted;
11. security action lacks valid process;
12. mediation delays urgent protection;
13. rights or judgments expire through routine review;
14. jurisdictions are ranked by ideology or developmental stage;
15. revenue or coercion activates without ratification;
16. global coverage substitutes for authority;
17. failure cannot stop the project.

# Closing commitment

A justice system does not become legitimate because it speaks the language of healing, wisdom, plurality, technology, or planetary responsibility. It becomes legitimate when its authority is valid, its jurisdiction bounded, its procedures fair, its evidence contestable, its remedies proportionate, its coercion reviewable, and its institutions capable of admitting error without abandoning the rule of law.

Restoration remains central where restoration is possible and freely chosen. Protection remains available where relationship repair is unsafe or unwanted. Adjudication remains disciplined where facts and rights must be determined. Enforcement remains separate and legally constrained. Indigenous law remains Indigenous rather than becoming a cultural layer inside a global system. Ecological and future interests receive constituted representation rather than rhetorical invocation. Technology remains a tool rather than an oracle.

> **Justice serves human and ecological flourishing not by dissolving authority into goodwill, but by making every exercise of authority visible, bounded, challengeable, and answerable to the rights it exists to protect.**
