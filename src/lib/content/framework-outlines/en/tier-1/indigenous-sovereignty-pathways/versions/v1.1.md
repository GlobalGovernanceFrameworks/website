# Preamble: an invitation that binds the GGF before it proposes anything to others

This framework begins from a limit.

The Global Governance Frameworks initiative can describe obligations that it places upon itself. It can offer resources, legal interfaces, technical options, and provisional institutional designs. It can invite dialogue. It cannot determine what Indigenous sovereignty means for every people, identify who legitimately speaks for a nation, convert Indigenous law into a GGF protocol, or declare that a non-Indigenous synthesis has become Indigenous governance.

The framework therefore contains three different kinds of statement.

1. **GGF obligations** are commitments that the GGF and conforming institutions place upon themselves. These include non-interference, consent, knowledge protection, material continuity, restitution, and refusal to manufacture Indigenous authority.
2. **Co-development proposals** are provisional mechanisms that may be explored only with the affected Indigenous peoples and may be modified, replaced, or rejected without penalty.
3. **Community-determined possibilities** name broad spaces of possibility without prescribing institutions, metrics, ceremonies, territorial forms, or technologies.

The original v1.0 correctly insisted that Indigenous peoples are not merely stakeholders to be consulted. It also acknowledged that the document was produced by a non-Indigenous person with AI assistance and offered its mechanisms as possibilities rather than prescriptions. Version 1.1 makes that limitation operative throughout the architecture.

The framework’s purpose is not to integrate Indigenous peoples into a predesigned planetary system. Its purpose is to ensure that any GGF system approaching Indigenous peoples, territories, knowledge, ecological relations, or futures must first become capable of restraint, consent, accountability, and withdrawal.

# 1. Status, purpose, and limits

## 1.1 Purpose

This framework defines:

- the obligations of GGF institutions toward Indigenous peoples and nations;
- the constitutional boundaries around Indigenous territory, law, knowledge, data, culture, and sacred relations;
- the conditions under which a GGF institution may seek consent, enter a compact, provide support, or recognize an Indigenous determination;
- the limits of BAZs, Treaty institutions, Meta-Governance bodies, financial systems, and technological systems where Indigenous sovereignty is implicated;
- provisional proposals for Indigenous–GGF interfaces that require Indigenous-led co-development;
- pathways through which Indigenous communities may engage, decline, withdraw, or create entirely different arrangements.

## 1.2 Non-certification clause

This framework does not certify:

- any person as an Elder, knowledge keeper, custodian, representative, or authority;
- any council as the legitimate voice of an Indigenous people;
- any teaching, quotation, ceremony, law, or interpretation as authentic;
- any territory, boundary, or membership claim;
- any technological representation of Traditional Knowledge;
- any metric as an adequate representation of Indigenous flourishing.

Those questions belong to Indigenous-determined processes. A GGF institution may recognize an authority identified through such a process. It may not create the authority whose consent it seeks.

## 1.3 No universal Indigenous framework

Indigenous peoples are plural. They have different laws, political orders, languages, territories, histories, spiritual traditions, relationships to states, and views of technology, markets, development, and global governance.

This document is therefore not a universal Indigenous constitution. It is a constitutional constraint on GGF conduct and an invitation to co-develop interfaces where communities choose to engage.

## 1.4 Scope boundaries

This framework does not define:

- the internal law or governance of any Indigenous people;
- a universal Earth Council or Indigenous Wisdom Council;
- mandatory BAZ organization;
- a global Traditional Knowledge database;
- a universal Gross Planetary Health score;
- an Indigenous identity registry;
- a global standard for ceremony, spirituality, or ecological relationship;
- the substantive validity of Traditional Knowledge;
- automatic rights to access Indigenous land, data, or knowledge.

## 1.5 Normative language

Within this document:

- **must** and **shall** bind GGF institutions and conforming proposals;
- **should** states a strong design presumption;
- **may** identifies an option requiring appropriate authority and consent;
- **provisional proposal** means a mechanism with no authority until adopted through Indigenous-determined and legally valid processes.

# 2. The Red Lines Clause

## 2.1 Core commitment

The Red Lines Clause is the constitutional centre of this framework:

> **No GGF institution, framework, currency, technology, treaty, BAZ, council, tribunal, or emergency process may claim legitimacy from Indigenous peoples while denying their authority to refuse, condition, alter, or withdraw from the relevant relationship.**

## 2.2 Red Lines

A conforming GGF institution shall not:

1. treat Indigenous peoples as one stakeholder category among many where inherent sovereignty or territorial rights are implicated;
2. substitute representation quotas for affected-nation consent;
3. appoint a universal Indigenous authority;
4. determine who legitimately speaks for an Indigenous people;
5. require BAZ membership, Hearts adoption, Love Ledger participation, data integration, or GGF institutional participation as a condition of recognition;
6. compel disclosure of Traditional Knowledge, sacred knowledge, community data, or internal deliberation;
7. train AI systems on protected knowledge without community-governed authorization;
8. convert knowledge, ceremony, care, land relationship, or cultural vitality into a financial asset merely because a technical conversion is possible;
9. use crisis powers to permanently bypass Indigenous consent or territorial authority;
10. make an Indigenous veto automatically destroy unrelated personal balances, AUBI Layer 1, essential services, or innocent third-party rights;
11. infer consent from silence, partial participation, historic data sharing, funding acceptance, or the participation of individual Indigenous persons;
12. declare a community insufficiently modern, legible, representative, or administratively capable and then acquire its authority;
13. require a community to prove cultural authenticity to a GGF body;
14. use a GGF metric to rank Indigenous peoples, cultures, or governance systems;
15. present this framework as Indigenous endorsement.

## 2.3 Right to modify, disregard, or withdraw

An Indigenous people or authorized community body may:

- reject any co-development proposal;
- replace GGF terminology with its own;
- restrict participation to selected domains;
- withdraw knowledge or data where legally and technically possible;
- suspend an interface while harm is investigated;
- terminate a compact under its agreed terms;
- maintain relations with some GGF institutions while rejecting others;
- develop an alternative architecture outside the GGF.

Withdrawal from a relationship does not erase pre-existing rights, restitution claims, earned balances, valid compensation, or obligations to repair harm.

## 2.4 GGF self-obligation

The GGF may make binding commitments upon itself even where no Indigenous people chooses to participate. These include:

- not appropriating protected knowledge;
- not speaking in the name of Indigenous peoples;
- not using non-participation as evidence of backwardness or hostility;
- funding Indigenous-led work without demanding control over its meaning;
- respecting protected non-transfer;
- preserving evidence of GGF misconduct;
- making correction, restitution, and withdrawal materially possible.

# 3. Sources of authority and standing

## 3.1 Inherent Indigenous sovereignty

Indigenous sovereignty is not delegated by the GGF, a state, a BAZ, Meta-Governance, or a Treaty institution. The GGF recognizes that Indigenous peoples may possess their own laws, authorities, territorial relations, and procedures for consent, representation, and dispute resolution.

Recognition does not require the GGF to understand or reproduce every internal basis of authority.

## 3.2 Affected-nation standing

Where a decision affects Indigenous territory, rights, knowledge, sacred relations, cultural continuity, or community data, standing belongs first to the affected Indigenous people or peoples through authorities they identify.

Global Indigenous participation, pan-Indigenous networks, or advisory bodies may provide important perspectives. They do not automatically substitute for the affected nation.

## 3.3 Individual and collective standing

The framework distinguishes:

- the rights of Indigenous individuals;
- collective rights of peoples and nations;
- the authority of community institutions;
- the participation of Indigenous professionals, advocates, or knowledge holders;
- the standing of displaced, urban, mobile, or diaspora members.

An individual’s Indigenous identity does not by itself authorize them to bind a people. Collective authority does not erase individual rights, safety, dissent, privacy, or access to remedy.

## 3.4 Internal plurality

A community may contain disagreement among:

- nations, clans, families, generations, genders, locations, and governance traditions;
- hereditary, elected, ceremonial, professional, and movement-based authorities;
- residents and diaspora members;
- supporters and opponents of a proposed GGF relationship.

GGF bodies shall not resolve such disagreement by selecting the most convenient speaker. When standing is contested, the responsible GGF body shall:

1. disclose the contest;
2. pause irreversible action where possible;
3. ask the relevant communities to identify an acceptable process;
4. provide resources for participation without controlling the result;
5. preserve minority and individual rights;
6. record unresolved plurality rather than manufacturing consensus.

## 3.5 No proxy authority

Where no Indigenous people claims authority over a territory or chooses to participate, the GGF shall not create a “Traditional Knowledge proxy,” appoint allied ethicists as substitutes, or request certification from a neighbouring people.

Ecological and ethical governance may proceed through other legitimate commons, scientific, democratic, and rights-based institutions. It shall not be falsely labelled Indigenous authority.

# 4. Participation, consent, refusal, and withdrawal

## 4.1 Participation is modular

Participation may be limited to one or more domains, including:

- legal recognition;
- land or water governance;
- ecological monitoring;
- cultural or language work;
- financial settlement;
- research;
- technology;
- emergency coordination;
- migration and sanctuary;
- education;
- Treaty development.

Consent in one domain does not imply consent in another.

## 4.2 Consent record

A consequential Indigenous–GGF interface shall maintain a consent record stating:

- affected people or peoples;
- authority or process identifying consent;
- subject and territory;
- purpose;
- permitted actions;
- prohibited actions;
- data and knowledge boundaries;
- benefit, compensation, or restitution terms;
- duration;
- review and amendment;
- withdrawal process;
- continuity obligations;
- unresolved disagreement.

## 4.3 Consent is ongoing and specific

Consent shall not be treated as a one-time signature authorizing indefinite expansion. Material changes in purpose, scale, technology, data use, financial consequences, environmental effects, or institutional authority require renewed review and, where the original consent terms require it, renewed consent.

## 4.4 Refusal is not failure

A refusal may reflect:

- an unacceptable proposal;
- unresolved internal process;
- insufficient information;
- historic distrust;
- a sacred or legal prohibition;
- a preference for another partner or system;
- a decision to remain autonomous;
- lack of administrative capacity;
- a wish not to disclose the reason.

The GGF shall not interpret refusal as ignorance, anti-science sentiment, insufficient development, or evidence that another actor may speak for the community.

## 4.5 Emergency limits

A genuine emergency may justify temporary action to protect life or stop immediate cross-boundary harm. It does not automatically authorize:

- permanent occupation or authority transfer;
- compulsory knowledge disclosure;
- permanent technology deployment;
- suspension of individual rights;
- cancellation of land, treaty, or restitution claims;
- permanent waiver of consent.

Any provisional action affecting Indigenous peoples requires the Emergency Constitutionalism Protocol in the Constitutional Interface Specification, an affected-authority interface where feasible, a narrow scope, and post-emergency restoration and review.

# 5. Territory, bioregions, and BAZs

## 5.1 Bioregional possibility

The v1.0 framework proposed Bioregional Autonomous Zones organized through watersheds, ecosystems, traditional territories, and seasonal relationships. This remains a potentially valuable form where communities choose it.

A BAZ is not presumed to be the natural or required form of Indigenous governance.

## 5.2 Territorial priority

A BAZ, municipal authority, commons institution, or Treaty body operating on or affecting Indigenous territory shall not treat bioregional mapping as a means of erasing:

- existing Indigenous jurisdiction;
- treaty boundaries;
- seasonal territories;
- overlapping territorial relationships;
- mobility routes;
- sacred places;
- land restitution claims.

Ecological boundaries and Indigenous territories may overlap without coinciding. The interface must preserve both rather than forcing one map to replace the other.

## 5.3 BAZ formation conditions

Where a BAZ is proposed on Indigenous territory or materially affects Indigenous rights, the proposal shall specify:

- the affected Indigenous people or peoples;
- existing and contested authorities;
- territorial overlap;
- the legal and political basis of the BAZ;
- what authority remains Indigenous;
- what authority is delegated to the BAZ;
- what authority is shared;
- what cannot be delegated;
- refusal and withdrawal;
- land, water, knowledge, and data boundaries;
- dispute and continuity processes.

## 5.4 Non-participating communities

A BAZ has no authority over a non-participating Indigenous people merely because the people or territory lies within a map used by the BAZ.

Cross-boundary harms and valid law may still require coordination or adjudication. Those powers must arise from an appropriate legal or constitutional source, not from cartographic inclusion.

## 5.5 Polycentric interface

Where communities choose a polycentric arrangement, authority may be distributed among:

- territorial public bodies;
- cross-boundary Commons Trusts or equivalent institutions;
- labour, guild, provider, or professional bodies;
- Indigenous authorities;
- rights-bearing ecosystem guardians;
- Treaty or adjudicative bodies within valid jurisdiction.

Indigenous authority is not a fourth GGF chamber. It enters the interface through Indigenous law, consent, covenant, territorial authority, or another community-determined form.

# 6. Indigenous coordination bodies and the Earth Council concept

## 6.1 Recognition, not creation

The v1.0 framework proposed an Earth Council—also named Kawsay Pacha or Terra Sapiens—as a possible wisdom and coordination body. Version 1.1 retains the possibility of Indigenous-led translocal coordination but removes any implication that the GGF can create, name, compose, or certify such a council.

Indigenous peoples may choose to form:

- one council;
- several regional or issue-specific councils;
- a confederation;
- temporary delegations;
- a network without centralized representation;
- no shared body.

## 6.2 Provisional Earth Council interface

Where an Indigenous-created body chooses to engage as an Earth Council or equivalent, the GGF may offer an interface supporting:

- communication among Indigenous nations and GGF institutions;
- Treaty and planetary-commons dialogue;
- notification of transboundary risks;
- submission of conditions, objections, or vetoes within valid standing;
- protection of sacredness and knowledge boundaries;
- conflict mediation requested by participating peoples;
- support for future generations and rights of nature.

The body’s name, composition, selection, decision procedures, accountability, and territorial standing remain Indigenous-determined.

## 6.3 No universal mandate

An Indigenous coordination body shall not be presumed to:

- represent all Indigenous peoples;
- speak for non-participants;
- authorize access to knowledge held by another people;
- waive the rights of affected nations;
- replace direct nation-to-nation engagement;
- exercise a global ecological veto outside the authority delegated to it.

## 6.4 GGF obligations toward coordination bodies

GGF institutions shall:

- support multilingual and multimodal participation;
- cover reasonable participation costs without conditioning the outcome;
- respect community-controlled records;
- disclose conflicts of interest;
- provide enough time for Indigenous processes;
- avoid artificial deadlines except for genuine immediate harm;
- preserve dissent and separate opinions;
- not demand public disclosure of internal deliberation.

# 7. The Treaty of the Circle: revised provisional covenant

## 7.1 Surviving principle

The original Treaty of the Circle expressed a strong constitutional insight:

> A BAZ or economic system operating on Indigenous territory should not gain legitimacy merely by consulting Indigenous peoples while retaining unilateral power over land, water, ecological limits, and covenant obligations.

Version 1.1 preserves the possibility of a binding Indigenous–BAZ covenant through which an Indigenous authority may grant, condition, suspend, or withdraw specific authorization.

## 7.2 Provisional status

The Treaty of the Circle is a **co-development proposal**, not a universal requirement or an operational legal instrument.

It becomes valid only where:

- the affected Indigenous people chooses to develop or adopt it;
- the authority to enter the covenant is determined through Indigenous processes;
- all other parties possess lawful authority;
- the terms are compatible with individual rights and valid law;
- financial and material continuity are specified;
- dispute and restoration processes exist.

## 7.3 Possible covenant objects

A covenant may govern:

- land use;
- water and ecosystem limits;
- extraction;
- sacred places;
- cultural and knowledge access;
- benefit sharing;
- restitution and land return;
- BAZ authority;
- Commons Trust participation;
- data and monitoring;
- economic activities linked to the territory;
- emergency coordination.

## 7.4 Veto and injunction

An authorized Indigenous body may hold power to:

- deny consent;
- impose conditions;
- require additional evidence;
- suspend a harmful activity;
- seek an injunction;
- withdraw covenant authorization;
- require remediation or restitution.

The veto shall identify its object, scope, authority, and consequence. The community is not required to disclose sacred or protected knowledge to justify the determination; a bounded attestation may be sufficient where the covenant so provides.

## 7.5 No automatic economic collapse

A covenant breach, ecological alert, or legitimacy withdrawal shall not automatically:

- invalidate Hearts held by individuals;
- terminate AUBI Layer 1;
- destroy unrelated provider balances;
- suspend essential services;
- erase valid contracts or wages;
- make Indigenous authorities responsible for uncontrolled economic collapse.

Financial consequences follow Financial Systems v3.4 and the protected-continuity requirements of the Constitutional Interface Specification.

## 7.6 Consequence ladder

A covenant may use a graduated sequence:

1. notice and evidence preservation;
2. immediate suspension or containment of the specific harmful activity;
3. affected-party response and Indigenous review;
4. mediation or adjudication where accepted and appropriate;
5. remediation, restitution, or restoration plan;
6. restriction of specific permissions, funding, or charter powers;
7. suspension or withdrawal of BAZ covenant authorization;
8. orderly institutional or financial resolution;
9. review of restoration conditions.

The sequence may be accelerated where immediate irreversible harm is credibly established, while preserving essential material continuity.

## 7.7 Internal plurality and standing

Where multiple Indigenous peoples, authorities, or knowledge holders have overlapping standing, the covenant shall not presume a single custodian. It shall specify:

- participating peoples;
- separate or shared consent requirements;
- unresolved territorial claims;
- internal decision procedures identified by each people;
- treatment of dissent;
- whether any decision can bind non-participants;
- a process for later accession or withdrawal.

## 7.8 Technology is optional

A covenant may use paper records, oral protocols, trusted witnesses, conventional legal instruments, community-controlled digital systems, or cryptographic tools.

No legitimacy token, blockchain, multisignature key, or automated threshold is constitutionally required. Technology supports the covenant; it does not create the sovereignty behind it.

# 8. Rights of nature, Commons Trusts, and guardian authority

## 8.1 Rights-of-nature possibility

The v1.0 framework proposed legal personhood and representation for rivers, forests, mountains, species, and ecosystems. This remains a community- and jurisdiction-dependent possibility.

GGF institutions may support rights-of-nature development where authorized, but shall not claim that every Indigenous people shares one ontology or legal model.

## 8.2 Cross-boundary commons

Rivers, watersheds, forests, aquifers, oceans, migratory species, and atmospheric systems cross territorial boundaries. Commons institutions may therefore require authority that overlaps BAZs, municipalities, states, and Indigenous territories.

Such authority shall be based on:

- valid law, treaty, compact, or covenant;
- affected-community participation;
- Indigenous standing where implicated;
- fiduciary duties;
- scientific and long-embedded ecological evidence;
- bounded jurisdiction;
- challenge and review.

## 8.3 Guardian roles

Ecosystem guardians may:

- represent the legally or constitutionally recognized interests of an ecosystem;
- submit ecological evidence;
- require precautionary review;
- object to or condition activities within their mandate;
- seek injunctions or adjudication;
- monitor restoration.

A guardian’s authority derives from the instrument establishing the role. The GGF shall not assume that an Indigenous Elder, scientist, or advocate automatically possesses such authority.

## 8.4 Knowledge disagreement

Where Indigenous knowledge, local observation, scientific models, or other evidence disagree, the system shall not require synthetic consensus.

The record should preserve:

- each claim;
- provenance and authority;
- what cannot be disclosed;
- uncertainty;
- differences in ontology or purpose;
- reversible actions available under uncertainty;
- the precautionary burden where irreversible harm is possible.

A sortition jury or global body shall not decide whether a sacred or Traditional Knowledge claim is metaphysically valid. It may decide a legally assigned dispute using the evidence and authority rules governing that dispute.

## 8.5 Precaution without epistemic capture

A precautionary default may temporarily prevent an irreversible action while evidence remains unresolved. It shall not be used to permanently transfer authority, compel protected disclosure, or convert one knowledge system into the sole universal standard.

# 9. Traditional Knowledge, data, and cultural sovereignty

## 9.1 Knowledge classes

An Indigenous authority may classify knowledge or data as:

- public;
- shared for a specified purpose;
- restricted to named parties;
- community-internal;
- sacred or non-transferable;
- intentionally unrecorded;
- available only through a conclusion, condition, veto, or bounded attestation.

The GGF shall preserve these distinctions through the entire data chain.

## 9.2 Protected non-transfer

Protected non-transfer is a valid and complete interface outcome.

A community may say:

- “the proposed action is not permitted”;
- “the evidence supports a seasonal restriction”;
- “this site may not be mapped publicly”;
- “the model may use this conclusion but not the underlying knowledge”;
- “no digital record may be created.”

Lack of underlying disclosure does not automatically invalidate the decision where the relevant constitutional or covenant authority permits bounded attestation.

## 9.3 Community-controlled storage

Where Indigenous knowledge or data is recorded, the agreement shall specify:

- ownership and stewardship;
- physical or digital location;
- access control;
- permitted uses;
- prohibition on secondary use;
- publication rules;
- retention;
- deletion and withdrawal;
- succession if an institution closes;
- response to subpoena, cyberattack, or state demand;
- benefit sharing;
- breach response and restitution.

## 9.4 Open-source boundary

Open-source commitments apply to GGF institutional code, public models, decision rules, and governance processes. They do not require publication of:

- Traditional Knowledge;
- sacred knowledge;
- personal records;
- community-restricted data;
- protected locations;
- oral knowledge that a community does not wish to record;
- culturally governed algorithms or training data.

An algorithm may be auditable through controlled review, test suites, or bounded attestations without making protected inputs public.

## 9.5 Institutional memory

The GGF shall use federated institutional memory rather than one permanent global repository for all records.

Public constitutional decisions may be archived publicly. Purpose-limited operational records remain under their governing authorities. Indigenous and community-controlled records remain under their own storage, access, and succession protocols.

## 9.6 Appropriation and benefit sharing

No GGF institution may treat public visibility as unrestricted permission to commercialize, train, reproduce, patent, brand, certify, or repackage Indigenous knowledge or cultural expression.

Where a community authorizes use, the agreement shall address:

- attribution as determined by the community;
- collective and individual rights;
- benefit sharing;
- downstream use;
- model or product updates;
- revocation or sunset;
- restoration for misuse.

# 10. Indigenous AI and technology sovereignty

## 10.1 Community-determined technology

Indigenous communities may choose to use, modify, reject, or develop technologies for:

- language revitalization;
- land and water monitoring;
- education;
- archival work;
- secure communication;
- climate adaptation;
- community administration;
- legal evidence;
- controlled knowledge transmission.

No technology is presumed necessary for modern sovereignty.

## 10.2 Indigenous-Governed Digital Tools

The v1.0 term **Indigenous AI Oracles** is retained only as a historical source concept. v1.1 uses **Indigenous-Governed Digital Tools** for any co-developed system.

Such tools may assist with:

- community-defined queries;
- language support;
- pattern detection;
- ecological monitoring;
- access-controlled archives;
- scenario exploration.

They shall not replace knowledge holders, authorities, ceremony, or community judgment.

## 10.3 Authorization requirements

Before a system uses Indigenous knowledge or community data, the record shall state:

- community authority;
- exact data and knowledge scope;
- purpose;
- training, inference, retrieval, or storage use;
- model ownership;
- access;
- audit method;
- benefit sharing;
- prohibited outputs;
- correction;
- model update and deletion process;
- response to leakage;
- withdrawal and sunset.

## 10.4 No automated cultural authority

An AI system shall not:

- decide who is Indigenous;
- certify cultural authenticity;
- select legitimate representatives;
- infer sacred meaning;
- resolve internal political disputes;
- score cultural alignment;
- determine whether FPIC exists;
- authorize knowledge sharing;
- override a community authority;
- make a binding ecological or legal decision by itself.

## 10.5 Cybersecurity and offline sovereignty

Technology plans should include:

- community-controlled encryption and keys;
- offline and low-tech operation;
- local backups where desired;
- recovery after internet shutdown or infrastructure failure;
- protection against remote administrative control;
- clear vendor exit;
- incident notification;
- independent security review acceptable to the community.

## 10.6 Technical refusal

A community may require that an interface operate without AI, blockchain, biometric identity, cloud storage, public dashboards, or automated monitoring. The GGF shall provide a non-digital or lower-technology alternative where participation rights would otherwise become conditional on technology adoption.

# 11. Regenerative economics and financial boundaries

## 11.1 Economic sovereignty

Economic sovereignty may include community authority over:

- land and resource use;
- local enterprises;
- cooperative and reciprocal systems;
- benefit sharing;
- public procurement;
- community funds;
- cultural and ecological work;
- participation in Hearts, Leaves, fiat, time banks, or other systems.

No one currency or ledger defines Indigenous economic sovereignty.

## 11.2 AUBI baseline

AUBI Layer 1 is unconditional material security. It shall not depend on:

- cultural participation;
- ecological work;
- Love Ledger activity;
- community membership tests created by the GGF;
- knowledge disclosure;
- adoption of a BAZ or covenant;
- acceptance of a technological identity system.

Local implementation may respect Indigenous governance and community context without weakening individual rights or the unconditional baseline.

## 11.3 Love Ledger boundary

Love Ledger participation is voluntary and non-monetized.

It shall not be used by default to:

- track benefits owed for Traditional Knowledge;
- verify cultural authenticity;
- create a contribution history;
- feed AUBI entitlement;
- calculate a Gross Planetary Health score;
- rank communities;
- support adverse decisions;
- make private relational life visible.

Communities may use their own gratitude, reciprocity, or narrative practices without adopting the Love Ledger.

## 11.4 Hearts and Leaves

Hearts and Leaves may support authorized care, cultural, ecological, or community activity under AUBI and Financial Systems rules.

Financial translation does not create ownership over:

- knowledge;
- ceremony;
- land relationship;
- spiritual authority;
- cultural identity;
- ecological personhood.

Leaves remain linked to ecological evidence, provenance, Indigenous authority where relevant, and slow-variable review. They are not a universal price for nature.

## 11.5 Protected non-convertibility

The following are not presumed convertible into Hearts, Leaves, fiat, market price, credit, LMCI, or portable reputation:

- dignity;
- sovereignty;
- sacredness;
- consent;
- cultural continuity;
- spiritual sincerity;
- territorial relationship;
- Traditional Knowledge;
- collective identity;
- ecological kinship.

## 11.6 Community Providers and work

Indigenous-governed Community Providers may receive funding and settlement through Work in Liberation, AUBI, Nested Economies, and Financial Systems interfaces.

Participation in formal work systems remains voluntary. Sacred, ceremonial, familial, community, and land-based practices are not treated as formal labour merely because institutions value them.

## 11.7 Reparations and restitution

Reparations and restitution may include:

- land return;
- financial transfer;
- ecological restoration;
- institutional reform;
- language and cultural recovery;
- debt cancellation;
- return of records and cultural property;
- long-term service or infrastructure obligations.

They shall not be conditioned on converting Indigenous life into verified regenerative work or performance metrics.

## 11.8 Sovereign debt

The earlier proposal to service sovereign debt through Hearts, Leaves, care work, or Gross Planetary Health outcomes is not operational in this framework. Financial Systems v3.4 defers Sovereign Debt Transformation to a dedicated future protocol.

Any future proposal requires separate treatment of legal authority, debtor consent, affected-community consent, ecological permanence, additionality, creditor treatment, distribution, and protection against forced labour or knowledge extraction.

# 12. Gross Planetary Health and observability

## 12.1 From universal index to community-defined profile

The v1.0 framework proposed a Gross Planetary Health index combining ecological health, cultural vitality, and community wellbeing.

Version 1.1 preserves GPH as an optional **community-defined profile**, not a universal composite score.

A GPH profile may present separate dimensions such as:

- land and water condition;
- species and habitat relations;
- language vitality;
- cultural transmission;
- material security;
- community health;
- governance autonomy;
- access to territory;
- safety of land defenders;
- restoration and restitution progress;
- future-generation concerns.

## 12.2 No single score

A community is not required to combine dimensions into one number. Some conditions may be:

- qualitative;
- seasonal;
- narrated;
- disputed;
- sacred;
- unavailable to external observers;
- meaningful only within the community’s own interpretive system.

## 12.3 No automatic allocation or intervention

A GPH profile shall not mechanically determine:

- AUBI payments;
- Hearts or Leaves issuance;
- exchange rates;
- Treaty sanctions;
- legitimacy of Indigenous governance;
- BAZ charter status;
- access to funding;
- emergency powers.

It may inform a State Estimate, community planning, or an authorized review.

## 12.4 Observation Contract

Any consequential use of Indigenous, cultural, or ecological indicators requires an Observation Contract identifying:

- purpose;
- decision supported;
- authority;
- indicators and protected exclusions;
- knowledge holders and observers;
- source dependencies;
- uncertainty;
- alternative interpretations;
- prohibited inferences;
- data sovereignty;
- challenge and correction;
- independent outcomes;
- reassessment and sunset.

## 12.5 Missing and unseen populations

Monitoring shall not presume that app users, formal residents, recognized institutions, or survey respondents represent the whole affected population.

The system should consider displaced people, mobile communities, people avoiding institutions, people without digital access, dissenters, informal carers, and others whose absence may itself reflect exclusion or protective non-participation.

## 12.6 Indicators are not judgments

A decline in language use, ecological indicators, participation, formal recognition, or recorded cultural activity may have multiple causes. An increase may likewise reflect institutional campaigns, changed measurement, coercion, or selective reporting.

Indicators authorize questions. They do not by themselves authorize judgment over a people or culture.

# 13. Future generations, youth, and ceremonial governance

## 13.1 Seven-generation orientation

The original framework emphasized long-term responsibility and youth participation. Version 1.1 preserves the general requirement that GGF institutions assess long-horizon and intergenerational consequences.

It does not claim that one “seven generations” formula represents all Indigenous traditions.

## 13.2 Youth authority

Indigenous youth may hold authority, advisory standing, veto, or future-generation roles where their people’s governance establishes it.

GGF institutions shall not appoint youth representatives as substitutes for Indigenous processes. They should support participation costs, accessibility, safety, mentorship, and the right of youth to dissent from senior authorities without being used by external actors to bypass those authorities.

## 13.3 Elders and knowledge holders

Elders and knowledge holders may carry distinct authority and responsibility. The GGF shall not define universal age, lineage, experience, or credential requirements.

Safeguards should address:

- consent;
- workload;
- compensation;
- knowledge boundaries;
- succession;
- conflicts of interest;
- misuse of individual testimony as collective authorization.

## 13.4 Ceremonial governance

The v1.0 vision of governance becoming more deeply relational, seasonal, land-based, and ceremonial remains a community-determined possibility.

GGF institutions shall not:

- standardize ceremony;
- require spiritual participation;
- treat ceremony as a performance indicator;
- use ceremonial language to conceal ordinary institutional power;
- claim that a GGF meeting becomes Indigenous governance by adopting circles, storytelling, or ritual forms.

## 13.5 Future-generations review

A major decision affecting Indigenous territory or cultural continuity should state:

- duration of likely effects;
- irreversible changes;
- affected future rights;
- who supplied long-horizon evidence;
- uncertainty;
- what remains unknowable;
- reversible alternatives;
- restoration obligations.

# 14. Climate adaptation, migration, and sanctuary

## 14.1 Indigenous leadership in adaptation

Indigenous peoples may possess long-embedded knowledge, territorial practice, and governance relevant to climate adaptation. Engagement shall follow community authority and knowledge-protection rules.

The GGF shall not treat climate urgency as a blanket licence to extract knowledge or deploy technology on Indigenous territory.

## 14.2 Displacement and mobility

Climate disruption may affect:

- residence;
- seasonal mobility;
- access to sacred and subsistence areas;
- territorial continuity;
- legal recognition;
- language communities;
- relations between host and displaced peoples.

Sanctuary and migration protocols should preserve identity, collective continuity, safety, and political voice rather than treating displaced Indigenous persons only as individual migrants.

## 14.3 Host-community authority

Indigenous traditions of hospitality may inform community-chosen sanctuary arrangements. They shall not be romanticized into an unlimited duty imposed by outsiders.

Support shall include material resources, infrastructure, land pressure assessment, conflict mediation, and respect for host-community authority.

## 14.4 Return, relocation, and continuity

A relocation plan should distinguish:

- temporary evacuation;
- voluntary mobility;
- forced displacement;
- planned relocation;
- permanent loss of territory;
- right of return;
- cultural and ecological restoration.

Consent to emergency evacuation is not consent to permanent dispossession.

# 15. External legal and institutional interfaces

## 15.1 Existing-system interface

The v1.0 framework identified possible interfaces with UNDRIP, biodiversity and climate agreements, international law, nation-states, corporate regulation, and rights-of-nature systems.

Version 1.1 retains these as areas for legal and political development, not as claims that the relevant institutions already provide every proposed authority.

## 15.2 Treaty for Our Only Home

A revised Treaty should bind its own institutions to:

- recognize Indigenous sovereignty;
- specify jurisdiction;
- protect affected-nation standing;
- require valid consent where Indigenous territory, rights, knowledge, or sacred relations are implicated;
- protect knowledge and material continuity;
- prohibit emergency erasure of permanent rights;
- provide remedies and restitution;
- avoid creating Indigenous authorities.

## 15.3 Meta-Governance

Meta-Governance may:

- coordinate interfaces requested or authorized by relevant parties;
- convene dialogue;
- document unresolved jurisdiction;
- support multilingual and multimodal processes;
- help maintain Observation Contracts and State Estimate Records;
- mediate where parties accept its role.

It may not:

- determine Indigenous legitimacy;
- acquire jurisdiction because coordination failed;
- create an Indigenous council;
- treat non-participation as an adaptation deficit;
- compel data integration;
- use a Variety Gap assessment to override protected knowledge or sovereignty.

## 15.4 Tribunals

A Tribunal may adjudicate matters within valid jurisdiction, including:

- Treaty obligations;
- rights violations;
- covenant procedure;
- institutional abuse;
- corporate and state misconduct;
- restitution;
- data or knowledge breaches.

It shall not claim jurisdiction to decide the truth or spiritual validity of Traditional Knowledge. It may determine whether a legally recognized authority acted within its mandate and whether procedural and individual rights were respected.

## 15.5 Corporate and research institutions

Corporate, academic, philanthropic, and technical actors shall not gain legitimacy through partnership branding alone.

Agreements should disclose:

- ownership;
- funding;
- expected benefit;
- data and intellectual-property terms;
- extraction and supply-chain effects;
- community authority;
- conflicts of interest;
- exit;
- remedy;
- public claims the partner may make.

## 15.6 Office of the Adversary

The Office of the Adversary may review GGF claims, power, and compliance. It does not obtain a right to protected Indigenous knowledge. Its audit may rely on community-authorized reviewers, bounded attestations, procedural evidence, and public institutional records.

# 16. Pathways from invitation to action

The v1.0 framework described transformation through stories of preparing soil, planting seeds, growing roots, weaving connections, and imagining a mature forest. Version 1.1 retains that non-linear framing while adding explicit gates.

## 16.1 Preparing the soil — self-audit before approach

Before approaching an Indigenous people, a GGF body should ask:

- Why are we seeking engagement?
- What power, funding, land, data, or technology do we control?
- What harm could our proposal create?
- What can the community refuse without losing material support?
- What knowledge do we not need?
- Who benefits if the proposal proceeds?
- What restitution or prior obligation already exists?
- Are we approaching the affected people or a convenient intermediary?

## 16.2 First conversations — relationship without extraction

Early engagement should prioritize:

- listening;
- community-selected timing and format;
- compensation for participation;
- no demand for immediate commitment;
- no public announcement without permission;
- explicit discussion of non-participation;
- recognition of prior harms and outstanding claims.

## 16.3 Community-determined proposal development

Where interest exists, parties may co-develop:

- a consent and standing map;
- a territorial and jurisdictional map;
- a knowledge and data protocol;
- a benefit, restitution, or funding agreement;
- a pilot with narrow scope;
- a covenant or compact;
- evaluation and withdrawal rules.

## 16.4 Bounded pilot

A pilot shall specify:

- purpose and scope;
- community authority;
- participants and non-participants;
- rights and protected minimums;
- data boundaries;
- independent review;
- stop conditions;
- rollback;
- who owns infrastructure and records;
- whether scaling is desirable at all.

## 16.5 Network weaving

Communities may choose bilateral, watershed, regional, international, or issue-specific networks. Network growth shall not require centralization or a universal representative body.

## 16.6 Mature relationship

A mature relationship is not measured by depth of integration. It may take the form of:

- a durable covenant;
- limited technical cooperation;
- periodic dialogue;
- a shared commons institution;
- mutual recognition with protected separation;
- respectful non-participation.

# 17. Responsibilities of allies, governments, funders, researchers, and technologists

## 17.1 Allies

Allies should:

- follow Indigenous leadership;
- support existing Indigenous-led initiatives;
- transfer resources without controlling the agenda;
- challenge institutions from which they benefit;
- avoid speaking as Indigenous authorities;
- accept that useful support may be quiet and non-central.

## 17.2 Governments and public institutions

Public institutions should:

- identify their legal and historic obligations;
- distinguish consultation from consent and jurisdiction;
- fund participation and administration;
- protect land defenders and whistleblowers;
- return records, land, authority, or resources where required;
- avoid using GGF participation to bypass direct duties.

## 17.3 Funders

Funding agreements should protect:

- community agenda control;
- administrative costs;
- long time horizons;
- non-public work;
- refusal to scale;
- ownership of outputs;
- independence from donor narratives;
- withdrawal without punitive repayment except for ordinary fraud or misuse established through due process.

## 17.4 Researchers

Researchers shall follow community-defined research protocols and state:

- research question;
- community benefit;
- authority;
- data and knowledge boundaries;
- publication;
- authorship and attribution;
- compensation;
- secondary use;
- return of findings;
- withdrawal and correction;
- long-term storage.

## 17.5 Technologists

Technologists should build for:

- community control;
- minimal data;
- local operation;
- understandable governance;
- interoperability without forced transfer;
- offline resilience;
- security;
- vendor exit;
- deletion and model retirement;
- no hidden reuse.

## 17.6 Artists, educators, and media

Cultural work should distinguish:

- Indigenous-led production;
- invited collaboration;
- allied work about settler or institutional responsibility;
- unauthorized representation.

Public storytelling shall not expose sacred, personal, territorial, or politically sensitive information without authorization.

# 18. Risks, tensions, and unresolved questions

## 18.1 Internal disagreement versus external urgency

External actors may face urgent ecological or rights concerns while affected Indigenous authorities remain internally divided or require time. The framework does not solve this through automatic override.

The response should separate:

- immediate reversible containment;
- long-term authorization;
- rights that remain protected;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- processes acceptable to affected peoples;
- valid external jurisdiction, if any.

## 18.2 Sovereignty versus individual rights

Collective sovereignty does not authorize violence, arbitrary deprivation, or denial of fundamental individual rights. External rights review likewise must not become a pretext for colonial substitution of governance.

The Treaty and Tribunal frameworks must define this boundary with Indigenous-led legal participation.

## 18.3 Ecological authority versus livelihood

A necessary ecological restriction may impose material costs on workers, families, and communities. The system should pair restriction with:

- material continuity;
- transition support;
- restitution where prior external extraction created dependence;
- locally governed replacement capacity;
- long-term outcome review.

## 18.4 Knowledge protection versus public accountability

Institutional authority must be accountable, but accountability does not require exposure of all knowledge. Review may use:

- process records;
- authorized independent reviewers;
- bounded attestations;
- outcome evidence;
- conflict declarations;
- community recall or correction mechanisms.

## 18.5 Technology opportunity versus technological colonialism

Technology can support language, ecology, communication, and sovereignty while also centralizing data, redefining authority, and making withdrawal impossible. Every technological benefit should be tested against control, dependence, reversibility, and non-digital alternatives.

## 18.6 Global coordination versus Indigenous non-participation

Planetary harms may cross territories, including territories of non-participating peoples. Non-participation does not authorize harm to others, but neither does cross-boundary concern create unlimited GGF jurisdiction.

The Constitutional Interface Specification’s decision classes, minimum necessary authority, and failure-to-act standard govern this problem.

## 18.7 Representation versus sovereignty

Increasing Indigenous representation inside a global institution may improve that institution. It does not transfer Indigenous sovereignty into the institution or authorize it to speak for affected peoples.

## 18.8 The framework’s authorship problem

This remains a non-Indigenous, AI-assisted document. Revision can reduce architectural contradiction but cannot convert its origin into Indigenous legitimacy.

The framework should therefore be treated as:

- a self-binding GGF ethics and interface proposal;
- a source of questions and draft safeguards;
- material for Indigenous-led critique;
- replaceable by better Indigenous-developed frameworks.

# 19. Evaluation and accountability

## 19.1 What the GGF may evaluate

The GGF may evaluate its own conduct, including:

- whether it approached the correct authority;
- whether consent was specific and ongoing;
- whether refusal carried a penalty;
- whether data use stayed within purpose;
- whether participation was funded and accessible;
- whether protected knowledge remained protected;
- whether material continuity survived a veto or withdrawal;
- whether remedies were implemented;
- whether the GGF misrepresented endorsement;
- whether authority returned when its justification ended.

## 19.2 What the GGF shall not score

The GGF shall not score:

- authenticity of a culture;
- worth of a people;
- quality of spirituality;
- degree of Indigenous modernity;
- legitimacy of internal governance through a universal formula;
- willingness to participate;
- amount of knowledge shared;
- conformity with the GGF’s preferred BAZ, economic, or technological model.

## 19.3 Accountability channels

Conforming implementation should include:

- a community-selected contact;
- confidential complaints;
- individual and collective standing;
- independent review;
- anti-retaliation;
- public correction of false claims;
- funding for legal and technical support;
- suspension of harmful data or technology use;
- restitution;
- termination of the interface where necessary.

## 19.4 Indigenous-led review gate

No mechanism that claims to institutionalize Indigenous authority should move from provisional design to canonical framework status without Indigenous-led review appropriate to the affected context.

A broad consultation cannot substitute for affected-nation consent to a specific territorial or knowledge interface.

## 19.5 Revision record

Every future version should state:

- who participated in revision;
- whether participants spoke personally or through recognized authority;
- which changes arose from Indigenous direction;
- which remain non-Indigenous proposals;
- unresolved disagreement;
- knowledge intentionally excluded;
- whether any community asked not to be named.

# 21. Implementation priorities

## P0 — Required before canonical use

1. Use the non-certification and Red Lines clauses in all GGF references to Indigenous authority.
2. Remove universal GGF-designed Earth Council and Indigenous Wisdom Council composition rules.
3. Remove direct GPH-to-AUBI, Love Ledger benefit tracking, and automated cultural scoring.
4. Replace automatic Treaty of the Circle currency invalidation with the protected consequence ladder.
5. Align Hearts, Leaves, BAZ, work, and data interfaces with the revised economic frameworks.
6. Add protected non-transfer and non-digital participation.
7. Separate representation, affected-nation consent, and global Indigenous coordination.
8. Prohibit proxy Indigenous authorities.
9. Mark all substantive institutional proposals as provisional pending Indigenous-led development.
10. Establish a revision record showing whose authority and input supported later changes.

## P1 — Co-development work

1. Develop an Indigenous–GGF engagement and consent template with Indigenous legal experts and communities.
2. Co-develop one or more Treaty of the Circle variants for communities that request them.
3. Develop protected knowledge, data, and AI protocols under community control.
4. Create legal and material-continuity options for covenant suspension or withdrawal.
5. Develop affected-nation standing and overlapping-territory procedures.
6. Co-design rights-of-nature and Commons Trust interfaces where communities choose them.
7. Develop climate displacement and sanctuary protocols protecting collective continuity.
8. Create funding models that support Indigenous administration without donor control.
9. Establish independent complaint and restitution pathways.
10. Identify which parts of this document should be replaced by Indigenous-authored frameworks.

## P2 — Pilots and learning

Any pilot should be community-requested or jointly initiated and should test a narrow interface such as:

- community-controlled data storage;
- a bounded ecological attestation;
- language technology under local control;
- a watershed covenant;
- a rights-of-nature guardian interface;
- non-digital consent records;
- a GPH profile without composite scoring;
- a funding agreement with withdrawal and data protections.

Pilot evaluation should ask whether sovereignty, safety, control, and withdrawal became more practical—not merely whether GGF integration increased.

# A vision of flourishing

The original v1.0 offered a vision in which lands and waters recover, languages return, technology serves life, economies circulate rather than extract, and governance becomes more relational and intergenerational.

Version 1.1 retains that horizon without claiming one path toward it.

Flourishing may appear as:

- Indigenous peoples exercising practical authority over their territories and futures;
- land return and ecological restoration;
- rivers, forests, species, and sacred places protected through Indigenous law, public law, covenants, or rights-of-nature institutions chosen for the context;
- children learning and living in ancestral languages;
- knowledge transmitted without being extracted;
- economic security without compulsory cultural performance;
- technology controlled, refused, or reshaped by communities;
- cities understanding and repairing their dependence on rural and Indigenous territories;
- institutions capable of hearing “no” without punishing the speaker;
- planetary cooperation that does not require cultural absorption;
- ceremonies, laws, councils, movements, households, and relationships taking forms no global framework could have designed in advance.

The success of this framework is not that Indigenous peoples become deeply integrated into the GGF.

Its success is that GGF institutions become capable of approaching Indigenous sovereignty without assuming ownership of the relationship—and capable of stepping back when they are not invited.

---

# Closing invitation

This framework remains an offer for dialogue, critique, replacement, and refusal.

For Indigenous peoples and communities, the central question is not whether the proposals fit the GGF. It is whether any part of the GGF can serve priorities already defined through your own relationships, laws, responsibilities, and visions.

For GGF developers and allied institutions, the central question is more demanding:

> **Can we build systems that accept Indigenous authority not only when it enriches our worldview, but when it limits our plans, withholds knowledge, refuses our technologies, redirects resources, or requires us to relinquish power?**

Where the answer is no, the framework is not yet ready for the relationship it seeks.
