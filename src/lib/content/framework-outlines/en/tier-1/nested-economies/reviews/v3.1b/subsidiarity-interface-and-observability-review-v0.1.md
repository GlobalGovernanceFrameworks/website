# Nested Economies Subsidiarity, Interface, and Observability Review v0.1

## Can local economies coordinate across scales without surrendering autonomy, hiding exclusion, or collapsing distinct forms of value into one dashboard?

**Status:** Internal bounded architectural review
**Primary object:** Nested Economies Framework v3.1b
**Compatibility targets:** AUBI Framework v2.5; Financial Systems Framework v3.4
**Review scope:** Scale authority, regional coordination, cross-scale interfaces, solidarity transfers, monitoring, Community Weaver roles, implementation readiness, and framework boundaries
**Out of scope:** External empirical verification of illustrative targets, budgets, timelines, case studies, and comparative claims

---

# Executive summary

## Overall verdict

**Nested Economies v3.1b has the right constitutional problem but has not yet fully specified its solution.**

The framework correctly asks how local economies can preserve autonomy while participating in larger systems of trade, solidarity, standards, crisis response, and planetary stewardship. It clearly positions itself as the framework governing the “where” and “how” of economic activity, while AUBI governs social protection and Financial Systems governs monetary infrastructure.

Its strongest surviving commitments are:

* local BAZ-level economic governance;
* interoperability without complete regulatory uniformity;
* a distinction between Hearts and the non-monetized Love Ledger;
* voluntary bioregional cooperation;
* global standards with local operational control;
* BAZ objection rights through NARP;
* Earth Council protection of Indigenous sovereignty;
* slow-variable safeguards for ecological standards;
* accessible and offline implementation pathways.

However, the framework contains four central architectural contradictions.

### 1. Two-tier governance versus regional authority

The framework says governance has only two tiers—local BAZ Councils and the global Social Resilience Council—while also assigning regional councils, regional budgets, regional legal compacts, regional redistribution, regional Love Ledger nodes, regional wage indices, and regional trade responsibilities.
It therefore does not yet distinguish:

* a **scale of activity**;
* a **voluntary coordination network**;
* a **delegated administrative function**;
* and a **governance authority with coercive decision rights**.

### 2. Interoperability versus data and value collapse

The interface section describes direct translation of care acts, ecological work, time-bank hours, fiat donations, and carbon credits into Hearts or Leaves through compact formulas.

This risks treating interoperability as universal convertibility and treating local, cultural, ecological, and relational evidence as though it were already commensurable.

### 3. Solidarity versus an unexplained fixed transfer

The framework repeatedly specifies a 10% regional solidarity redistribution, but does not define:

* the taxable base;
* payer and recipient;
* needs assessment;
* authority;
* hardship exception;
* distribution formula;
* duration;
* challenge rights;
* or evidence that the transfer addresses the underlying disparity.

### 4. Monitoring versus LMCI pipeline collapse

The framework defines “LMCI” as 40% Hearts transactions, 30% care hours logged, and 30% interoperability, displayed in real time.

That is not LMCI-M. It is an operational integration indicator combining monetary activity, recorded care, and technical connectivity. AUBI v2.5 explicitly requires LMCI-M, LMCI-A, operational telemetry, and the Love Ledger Narrative Feed to remain separate.

## Central review claim

> **Nested Economies v3.1b identifies the need for economic subsidiarity, but it does not yet provide an operational test for deciding what should remain local, what may cross a boundary, what requires collective authority, or when interoperability has become dependence, extraction, or forced legibility.**

## Readiness verdict

**Amber and suitable for immediate revision to v3.2.**

Unlike the supplied Financial Systems v3.3 source, this document does not require wholesale reconstruction. It is a coherent outline, though it contains a small truncation in §3 and should not be represented as a complete operational specification.

The correct next step is a focused v3.2 revision that:

1. specifies the subsidiarity test;
2. resolves the status of the regional scale;
3. introduces cross-scale interface contracts;
4. replaces the fixed solidarity rule with a governed solidarity protocol;
5. separates measurement systems;
6. supplies the missing decision-rights matrix;
7. aligns implementation readiness with AUBI v2.5;
8. limits Community Weaver epistemic concentration.

# 1. Review method

The review asks six questions:

1. **Authority:** What decisions belong at each scale?
2. **Regional status:** Is the regional level a government, network, service layer, or temporary delegation?
3. **Interface:** What value, information, obligations, and rights may cross boundaries?
4. **Solidarity:** How are transfers diagnosed, authorized, contested, and ended?
5. **Observability:** Can the framework distinguish autonomy from isolation, interoperability from dependence, and equity from forced uniformity?
6. **Compatibility:** Does the framework align with AUBI v2.5 and Financial Systems v3.4?

Each mechanism is assessed against:

* **subsidiarity fitness**;
* **interface clarity**;
* **state observability**;
* **decision authority**;
* **contestability**;
* **data and cultural sovereignty**;
* **outcome validation**.

Ratings are qualitative:

* **Green:** operationally coherent;
* **Amber:** directionally sound but under-specified;
* **Red:** supports conflicting interpretations or unsafe action;
* **Protected:** intentionally not standardized or made fully observable;
* **Deferred:** belongs primarily in another framework.

No composite score is used.

# 2. Scope and source integrity

## 2.1 The document is an outline

The title identifies v3.1b as a “Revised Outline,” and many sections consist of one-line mechanisms, targets, budgets, or placeholder visualizations rather than complete governance protocols.

This matters because absence of detail does not always mean the framework has made a bad architectural choice. In several places, the required protocol is merely named but not supplied.

Examples include:

* “Cross-Level Trust Protocol”;
* “decision rights matrix”;
* arbitration panels;
* biennial evaluation;
* crisis protocols;
* interface standards.

These should be treated as **unimplemented specifications**, not as functioning safeguards.

## 2.2 Small source truncation

A short truncation marker appears inside §3.1, after which the text resumes with regional actors and budgets.

This prevents a complete review of the original local and regional implementation subsections, but the surviving structure is sufficient for the bounded architectural findings.

## 2.3 Stale framework references

The document refers to AUBI v5.0, while the current compatibility target is AUBI v2.5.

Its NARP reference also points to Financial Systems §14.3, whereas Financial Systems v3.4 has been reorganized.

These are straightforward v3.2 repairs.

# 3. The framework’s unique function

Nested Economies should not duplicate:

* AUBI payout and entitlement rules;
* Hearts issuance, reserves, redemption, demurrage, or ICTL rate-setting;
* Work in Liberation’s labor and provider governance;
* Meta-Governance’s general institutional coordination.

Its distinctive object should be:

> **The constitutional and operational rules governing how relatively autonomous economies relate across boundaries.**

This includes:

* which decisions remain local;
* which externalities create legitimate cross-boundary claims;
* how standards are mutually recognized;
* how solidarity obligations are allocated;
* how local economies connect without being absorbed;
* how common infrastructure is governed;
* how communities exit, fork, or negotiate exceptions;
* what information must cross a boundary;
* what information may remain local;
* how disputes between scales are adjudicated.

The current framework gestures toward this role but spends substantial space restating Hearts, AUBI, Love Ledger, and implementation targets rather than fully specifying the boundary architecture.

### Review verdict

**Amber-green conceptually; amber-red operationally.**

v3.2 should become a sharper **economic subsidiarity and cross-scale interface framework**.

# 4. Scale architecture

## 4.1 Local scale

The framework assigns local economies:

* community-owned Hearts wallets;
* Community Providers;
* local care economies;
* participatory budgeting;
* land trusts;
* local bylaws;
* BAZ Council governance;
* day-to-day provider chartering.
  This is directionally coherent.

However, “local sovereignty” is partly defined through wallet ownership and local transaction share. Wallet control is only one component of sovereignty. A community may control its wallets while remaining dependent on:

* external reserve funding;
* external essential goods;
* proprietary software;
* external validators;
* a global exchange rate;
* a narrow provider base;
* a dominant local intermediary.

### v3.2 should define local economic sovereignty as a multidimensional condition

At minimum:

1. **decision autonomy** — meaningful authority over local economic rules;
2. **exit and fork capacity** — ability to leave or modify arrangements without catastrophic loss;
3. **material capability** — ability to meet essential needs or secure reliable access;
4. **fiscal capacity** — ability to sustain local obligations;
5. **provider diversity** — absence of critical dependence on one provider;
6. **technical autonomy** — data portability and offline fallback;
7. **epistemic autonomy** — ability to preserve local categories and submit counter-models;
8. **rights integrity** — local autonomy does not shield exploitation or exclusion.

No single percentage should collapse these dimensions.

---

## 4.2 Global scale

The Social Resilience Council is assigned:

* global Hearts standards;
* supply management;
* GCF management;
* crisis coordination;
* technology-commons governance;
* cross-scale stakeholder coordination.
  The framework also gives the Earth Council veto power where Indigenous sovereignty or ecological harm is implicated.

The direction is broadly consistent with AUBI and Financial Systems, but v3.2 should not itself restate or expand the SRC’s monetary authority. Financial Systems v3.4 is now authoritative for financial Observation Contracts, NARP, Treasury rules, ICTL decisions, and source-independence requirements.

### Global authority should be limited to conditions that satisfy at least one of these tests

* material cross-boundary externality;
* interoperability requirement;
* shared commons obligation;
* rights floor;
* systemic financial risk;
* collective crisis;
* inability of affected local units to coordinate voluntarily;
* duty created by a prior compact.

Global preference for uniformity is not sufficient.

---

## 4.3 The regional contradiction

The framework states that regional cooperation is voluntary and that no intermediate governance layer exists. Yet it assigns the regional scale:

* councils and alliances;
* trade compacts;
* budgets;
* legal pathways;
* solidarity redistribution;
* wage indices;
* Love Ledger nodes;
* trade-zone functions;
* conflict-resolution roles.
  This is the review’s most important structural finding.

### Three different things are being called “regional”

#### A. Regional economic space

A factual pattern of trade, watershed dependence, labor mobility, infrastructure, or ecological connection.

This does not itself possess authority.

#### B. Voluntary BAZ network

Several BAZs enter a compact for:

* shared infrastructure;
* procurement;
* trade;
* mutual aid;
* technical standards;
* dispute resolution.

Its authority comes from the compact and remains limited to delegated functions.

#### C. Regional public authority

An institution able to levy, redistribute, regulate, sanction, or bind member BAZs.

This is a governance tier, whatever it is called.

v3.1b moves among A, B, and C without marking the transition.

---

## 4.4 Recommended resolution: two constitutional tiers, optional coordination layers

v3.2 can preserve its two-tier constitutional design while recognizing optional regional coordination.

### Constitutional tiers

* **BAZ Councils:** original local authority.
* **Global GGF bodies:** authority only in constitutionally specified domains.

### Coordination layers

* voluntary;
* function-specific;
* created through compact;
* revocable;
* non-sovereign by default;
* unable to expand their own mandate;
* transparent about delegated powers;
* subject to exit, audit, and sunset.

A regional body becomes a genuine governance authority if it can:

* levy compulsory contributions;
* bind non-consenting BAZs;
* impose sanctions;
* control market access;
* alter rights;
* permanently manage shared assets;
* or prevent exit.

Such authority requires explicit constitutional authorization rather than being smuggled in as “coordination.”

### Verdict

**Current status: Red ambiguity.**
**Repair difficulty: Moderate.**

# 5. Subsidiarity protocol

v3.1b states a commitment to local autonomy but does not supply a test for assigning decisions.

v3.2 should add an **Economic Subsidiarity Test**.

## 5.1 Presumption

> **Authority remains at the lowest scale capable of addressing the condition without imposing ungoverned material harm on other communities, shared commons, or protected rights.**

## 5.2 Burden of justification

Any upward transfer of authority must state:

1. the condition the lower scale cannot adequately address;
2. the affected external parties;
3. evidence of cross-boundary harm or coordination failure;
4. why voluntary compact is insufficient;
5. the minimum authority required;
6. the duration;
7. preserved local discretion;
8. review and return conditions;
9. exit or fork rights;
10. who may challenge the transfer.

## 5.3 Capability is not mere administrative capacity

A low-capacity BAZ should not lose authority merely because it lacks staff, technology, or funding.

Before centralization, the system should consider:

* capacity grants;
* shared services;
* technical assistance;
* temporary delegation;
* federated administration;
* mutual aid;
* slower implementation.

Otherwise, resource inequality becomes authority inequality.

## 5.4 Return principle

Authority transferred upward must return when:

* the condition ends;
* local capability is restored;
* a narrower mechanism becomes available;
* the coordination layer repeatedly exceeds mandate;
* affected BAZs withdraw under the compact;
* independent review finds no continuing necessity.

# 7. Cross-scale interface review

## 7.1 Current interface model

The framework describes the ICTL as the cross-level protocol for value and data and proposes open APIs for translating care, ecological work, fiat, time-bank hours, and carbon credits into Hearts or Leaves.

It also calls for:

* encrypted open-source platforms;
* regional Love Ledger nodes;
* transparent Love Ledger reporting;
* digital access as a minimum requirement.

This combines four different interfaces:

1. financial settlement;
2. contribution validation;
3. ecological evidence;
4. relational narrative.

They should not share one generic interface contract.

---

## 7.2 Interoperability is not universal convertibility

A system is interoperable when participants can coordinate across boundaries while preserving:

* semantic integrity;
* local governance;
* rights;
* provenance;
* context;
* reversibility.

It does not require that every form of value be converted into a common unit.

### Examples

* A local care credential may be **recognized** without being assigned an identical global price.
* An ecological record may be **verified** without being freely convertible into Hearts.
* A time-bank hour may remain local while being accepted as evidence in a mutual-aid compact.
* A sacred or relational contribution may remain non-transferable and non-monetized.
* Two BAZs may settle a trade without exchanging individual care records.

v3.2 should distinguish:

* **recognition**;
* **authentication**;
* **settlement**;
* **conversion**;
* **portability**;
* **aggregation**;
* **publication**.

Permission for one does not imply permission for the others.

---

## 7.3 Cross-Scale Interface Contract

Every material cross-scale interface should specify:

1. participating jurisdictions;
2. purpose;
3. authority;
4. object being exchanged;
5. whether the interface performs recognition, verification, settlement, conversion, aggregation, or publication;
6. required data;
7. prohibited data;
8. local semantic fields preserved;
9. transformation or conversion method;
10. uncertainty and loss introduced by translation;
11. provenance;
12. data owner;
13. storage location;
14. retention and deletion;
15. who can inspect or correct;
16. exit and data-portability rules;
17. failure and fallback mode;
18. protocol version;
19. change process;
20. dispute and appeal rights.

This is the scale-specific equivalent of the AUBI and Financial Systems Observation Contract.

---

## 7.4 Information classes

v3.2 should define four cross-boundary information classes.

### Class I: Required institutional information

Examples:

* aggregate reserve compliance;
* settlement status;
* compact obligations;
* institutional audit records;
* shared-commons impacts.

This may cross boundaries when necessary for accountability.

### Class II: Aggregate operational telemetry

Examples:

* transaction volume;
* provider capacity;
* trade flows;
* infrastructure load;
* access gaps.

These may support coordination but are not LMCI-M and do not by themselves prove flourishing.

### Class III: Voluntary narrative and contextual evidence

Love Ledger recognition and community narratives belong here.

AUBI v2.5 defines the Love Ledger Narrative Feed as voluntary and non-monetized, and prohibits mechanically converting it into LMCI-M, LMCI-A, Hearts, fiat, or entitlement.

### Class IV: Protected local, sacred, or personal knowledge

This may remain:

* local;
* non-digitized;
* non-comparable;
* stewarded by an Indigenous or community authority;
* represented externally only through a conclusion, veto, or bounded attestation.

Interoperability must support **protected non-transfer**, not merely data transfer.

# 8. Love Ledger and minimum-participation requirements

v3.1b lists transparent Love Ledger reporting and basic digital access as minimum requirements.

This conflicts with:

* voluntary informal recognition;
* offline access;
* protected unobservability;
* cultural sovereignty;
* the distinction between institutional transparency and individual visibility.

## Required v3.2 replacement

Minimum requirements should concern institutional capacity:

* a functioning local ledger for formal Hearts transactions;
* offline or low-tech access;
* transparent institutional rules;
* correction and appeal;
* data portability;
* privacy-preserving aggregate reporting;
* voluntary Love Ledger participation.

No person or community should be required to log informal care or relational life as a condition of participation in the formal economy.

Financial Systems v3.4 likewise states that financial transparency applies primarily to institutions, algorithms, aggregate flows, and public authority, rather than permanently exposing individuals.

### Verdict

**Current status: Red compatibility conflict.**
**Repair difficulty: Low.**

# 9. Value-conversion audit

## 9.1 Care acts

The conversion table uses:

> Proof of Care score × impact multiplier.

This is too compact to represent the safeguards now present in AUBI v2.5.

A care contribution may require distinct evidence concerning:

* occurrence;
* recipient acceptance;
* duration;
* role;
* additionality;
* safeguarding;
* uncertainty;
* non-monetization preference.

The conversion cannot be owned by Nested Economies. It belongs under AUBI LMCI-A and Work in Liberation validation.

## 9.2 Ecological work

The Green Job Score and Leaves multiplier belong under:

* AUBI ecological contribution rules;
* FLP standards;
* Financial Systems settlement;
* slow-variable governance;
* relevant ecological frameworks.

Nested Economies should specify when another BAZ recognizes the claim, not define the valuation formula.

## 9.3 Time banks

“Hours × regional wage index ÷ global Hearts index” creates several issues:

* one hour may not be equivalent across activities;
* wage indices may reproduce existing market inequality;
* a global index may override local reciprocity norms;
* unpaid relational practices may become monetized;
* regional index authority is undefined.

v3.2 should treat time-bank conversion as an optional compact-specific rule requiring:

* participant consent;
* purpose;
* local governance;
* distributional review;
* exit;
* no automatic portability.

## 9.4 Carbon credits

“Tonnage × eco-impact factor” understates:

* additionality;
* permanence;
* leakage;
* uncertainty;
* Indigenous land rights;
* baseline manipulation;
* ecological co-harms.

Nested Economies should defer ecological valuation to the relevant ecological and financial protocols.

## 9.5 Fiat donations and Heartstarter

The current 1:1 reference should be aligned with Financial Systems v3.4’s current Heartstarter and reserve architecture rather than restated as a universal conversion formula.

### Overall verdict

**The conversion table should be replaced by an interface-authority table.**

Nested Economies should say:

* what system owns valuation;
* what system validates evidence;
* what system settles;
* what local discretion remains;
* what may not be converted.

# 10. Solidarity redistribution review

## 10.1 Current rule

The framework twice specifies a 10% regional redistribution of Hearts trade revenue.

The source does not define “trade revenue.” It might mean:

* transaction fees;
* Treasury revenue;
* provider gross revenue;
* net trade surplus;
* tax revenue;
* ICTL fees;
* value of cross-BAZ trade.

These are materially different bases.

## 10.2 Indistinguishable-state problem

A BAZ with high trade revenue may be:

1. highly productive;
2. benefiting from inherited infrastructure;
3. extracting resources from another BAZ;
4. acting as a transit or settlement hub;
5. temporarily receiving disaster-related demand;
6. operating with high external inputs and low margin;
7. accumulating because of ICTL mispricing;
8. reporting more completely than peers;
9. serving a large external population;
10. exploiting labor or ecological assets.

A receiving BAZ with low revenue may be:

1. deprived of investment;
2. carrying ecological stewardship obligations;
3. isolated by infrastructure failure;
4. voluntarily low-commercial;
5. materially sufficient through non-market provision;
6. suffering governance capture;
7. experiencing temporary disaster;
8. excluded by interface rules;
9. under-reporting activity;
10. dependent on repeated transfers.

A fixed transfer cannot distinguish these conditions.

---

## 10.3 Solidarity must not become permanent dependency or punishment

A transfer mechanism should not:

* punish regenerative low-consumption economies;
* reward extraction;
* centralize power in the allocator;
* require full local legibility;
* make communities compete to prove deprivation;
* continue after the underlying condition changes;
* conceal structural trade imbalance;
* substitute for restitution where specific harm occurred.

---

## 10.4 Proposed Solidarity Protocol

### Trigger classes

* acute shock;
* structural capability gap;
* shared infrastructure;
* ecological stewardship burden;
* reparative obligation;
* transition cost;
* cross-boundary externality;
* equalization of essential-service capacity.

### Required State Estimate

Before a non-emergency transfer:

* condition;
* affected population;
* causal hypotheses;
* evidence;
* uncertainty;
* local account;
* contribution of existing trade rules;
* distributional effects;
* alternatives;
* duration;
* expected independent outcomes.

### Contribution bases

Possible bases may include:

* compact fee;
* net fiscal capacity;
* ICTL fee share;
* GCF allocation;
* commons dividend;
* negotiated contribution;
* responsibility for caused harm.

No one percentage should apply to all classes.

### Governance

* voluntary compact where obligations are voluntarily created;
* constitutional authorization where rights or commons duties are involved;
* affected-BAZ participation;
* public formula;
* hardship exemption;
* appeal;
* sunset;
* independent review.

### End conditions

Transfers should end, change, or convert into another instrument when:

* the capability gap closes;
* the emergency ends;
* the original diagnosis is falsified;
* local capacity is restored;
* the transfer creates harmful dependence;
* the contributing BAZ enters hardship;
* a structural rule change is more appropriate.

### v3.2 recommendation

Replace “10% solidarity redistribution” with:

> **A pilot default contribution band, to be specified by compact and activated only through the Solidarity Protocol.**

A 10% figure may remain as an illustrative pilot parameter, not a constitutional universal.

# 11. Monitoring and observability

## 11.1 Current headline targets

The framework targets:

* 70% local sovereignty;
* 80% equitable resource access;
* 90% interoperability.

These are not operationally defined.

The percentages imply a level of precision unsupported by the framework and risk obscuring conflicts among dimensions.

## 11.2 Current LMCI error

The operational formula called LMCI combines:

* Hearts transactions;
* logged care hours;
* interoperability.

This formula has several problems:

1. transaction volume is not flourishing;
2. logged care is affected by observation and incentive architecture;
3. interoperability is a system property, not Love, Meaning, or Connection;
4. the formula rewards legibility;
5. a system can raise the score by increasing transactions or logging;
6. informal communities may appear worse because they record less;
7. technical integration could offset worsening wellbeing.

### Required correction

Delete this formula as LMCI.

Do not simply rename it as another composite score.

Instead create a **Nested Economy Condition Dashboard** with separate dimensions.

# 12. Indistinguishable-state tests

## Test 1: High local transaction share

A BAZ reports 70% of transactions in Hearts.

Possible states:

* thriving local economy;
* lack of fiat access;
* coercive local acceptance;
* isolation from external markets;
* artificially high internal wash transactions;
* successful sufficiency;
* dependence on one local monopoly.

**Verdict:** Local transaction share is an alert or descriptive indicator, not a sovereignty diagnosis.

---

## Test 2: High interoperability

A BAZ has 95% technical compatibility.

Possible states:

* healthy voluntary integration;
* forced standardization;
* dependency on one external platform;
* extensive data extraction;
* loss of local valuation practices;
* shallow API compatibility without practical access.

**Verdict:** Interoperability must include autonomy, fallback, semantic preservation, and exit.

---

## Test 3: Low cross-BAZ trade

Possible states:

* isolation;
* self-sufficiency;
* inaccessible ICTL;
* local preference;
* discrimination;
* infrastructure failure;
* ecological restraint.

**Verdict:** Low trade cannot automatically justify integration pressure.

---

## Test 4: Low Love Ledger activity

Possible states:

* weak relational life;
* privacy preference;
* strong unlogged care;
* cultural rejection of recording;
* inaccessible technology;
* distrust;
* administrative failure.

**Verdict:** Low logging cannot establish low care or low social connection.

---

## Test 5: Persistent solidarity receipt

Possible states:

* unresolved structural deprivation;
* ecological stewardship burden;
* extraction by donor regions;
* poor local governance;
* harmful dependency;
* measurement bias;
* intentionally low commercialization.

**Verdict:** Receipt volume cannot determine success or failure without causal and outcome review.

---

## Test 6: Regional coordination success

A regional network completes projects quickly.

Possible states:

* effective compact;
* domination by one BAZ;
* suppressed objections;
* narrow participation;
* hidden burden shifting;
* genuine shared capability.

**Verdict:** Speed and project completion do not establish legitimate subsidiarity.

# 13. Community Weaver audit

## 13.1 Current role concentration

Community Weavers are assigned:

* training;
* Love Ledger support;
* Hearts support;
* participation facilitation;
* contribution validation;
* trust-building;
* Indigenous-protocol alignment;
* local-global liaison.

This makes the Weaver simultaneously:

* system promoter;
* educator;
* observer;
* validator;
* cultural interpreter;
* access intermediary;
* governance messenger.

Their compensation also comes through the system whose success they help report.

### Risk

Several nominally separate observation channels may share:

* the same observer;
* the same incentive;
* the same relationship network;
* the same reporting tool;
* the same institutional sponsor.

AUBI v2.5 requires an Observation Independence Map whenever channels share observers, platforms, incentives, algorithms, or governance authorities.

## 13.2 Recommended role separation

Community Weavers may:

* educate;
* facilitate;
* support access;

* identify unmet needs;
* relay community interpretations.

They should not alone:

* determine fraud;
* validate their own programme success;
* certify LMCI-M;
* adjudicate appeals;
* represent an Indigenous community without authorization;
* decide whether regional integration is legitimate;
* control both data entry and audit.

### Minimum safeguards

* secondary review;
* independent sampling;
* participant correction;
* confidential complaint route;
* anti-retaliation;
* workload and burnout monitoring;
* rotating audit;
* explicit conflict declarations;
* no sole-source diagnosis.

# 20. Final assessment

Nested Economies v3.1b is not mainly suffering from too little central coordination or too much local autonomy.

Its central problem is that **the boundary between them is still informal**.

The framework says:

* local economies should be sovereign;
* regional systems should coordinate;
* global institutions should set standards;
* resources should be redistributed;
* values and information should interoperate.

But it does not yet specify:

* when coordination becomes authority;
* when authority must return;
* when a regional body becomes a third tier;
* what must cross a boundary;
* what may remain local;
* who owns translation;
* how much information is enough;
* how solidarity avoids extraction or dependency;
* how autonomy is distinguished from isolation;
* how interoperability is distinguished from absorption.

The key constitutional principle for v3.2 should be:

> **Coordinate at the scale of the interdependence; govern at the lowest capable scale; transfer only the authority and information necessary for the shared problem; and return both when the justification ends.**

Its interface corollary should be:

> **Interoperability does not require universal convertibility, uniform measurement, or total legibility. A system is interoperable when communities can coordinate across boundaries while preserving semantic integrity, protected difference, meaningful exit, and local authority.**

Its observability corollary should be:

> **No measure of transaction share, technical compatibility, logged care, trade volume, or transfer receipt is sufficient by itself to diagnose sovereignty, flourishing, equity, or successful integration.**

With those principles, v3.2 can move beyond “local circles connected to a global sphere” and specify the actual engineering of the connections: what passes through them, who controls them, how they fail, and how communities remain free on both sides.
