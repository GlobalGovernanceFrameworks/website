# Executive Summary

The **Nested Economies Framework** governs how relatively autonomous local economies relate across boundaries. It is the GGF's architecture for **economic subsidiarity**: deciding which economic choices remain local, which interdependencies justify cooperation, which common standards are necessary, what authority may be delegated upward, what information may cross a boundary, and when delegated authority or data must return.

The framework's vision is an economy of **Right Relationship** in which communities can meet needs, create meaning, sustain ecosystems, and cooperate globally without being forced into a single institutional form, value language, data model, or development path.

Its core architecture combines:

- **Bioregional Autonomous Zones (BAZs)** as the primary sites of local economic authority;
- **Hearts** as the formal regenerative currency governed by AUBI and Financial Systems;
- **Leaves** as ecological records and reward instruments governed by AUBI, FLP standards, ecological protocols, and slow-variable safeguards;
- the **Love Ledger** as a voluntary, non-monetized system of relational recognition;
- **voluntary coordination layers** for trade, mutual aid, shared infrastructure, and solidarity;
- global institutions with authority limited to constitutionally specified commons, rights, interoperability, systemic-risk, and crisis domains;
- **Cross-Scale Interface Contracts** specifying what passes between systems and what remains local;
- an **Economic Subsidiarity Test** governing any transfer of authority;
- **Solidarity Protocols** that respond to diagnosed needs rather than applying a universal transfer rule;
- separate monitoring of autonomy, material access, interoperability, solidarity, resilience, rights, and population-level flourishing.

Three constitutional principles govern the framework:

> **Coordinate at the scale of the interdependence; govern at the lowest capable scale; and transfer only the authority necessary for the shared problem.**

> **Interoperability does not require universal convertibility, uniform measurement, or total legibility.**

> **Delegated authority, compulsory information, and extraordinary solidarity obligations must end or return when their justification ends.**

The framework uses **two constitutional governing tiers**:

1. **BAZ-level authority**, where original economic authority is presumed to reside; and
2. **global GGF authority**, limited to constitutionally defined cross-boundary domains.

Between them, BAZs may create **optional coordination layers**. These are not governments by default. They receive only explicit, function-specific, revocable powers through compacts. A regional body becomes a genuine third governing tier if it can compel contributions, bind non-consenting BAZs, sanction participants, restrict exit, control rights, or expand its own mandate. Such powers require explicit constitutional authorization and may not arise through administrative convenience.

The framework does not define AUBI entitlement, Hearts issuance, Treasury reserves, demurrage, fraud sanctions, ICTL rates, or work-valuation standards. Those remain governed by their authoritative companion frameworks. Nested Economies defines the **political, subsidiarity, interface, and solidarity conditions** under which those systems connect across places.

# 1. Purpose, Boundaries, and Design Commitments

## 1.1 Purpose

The framework creates rules for resilient economic integration without compulsory homogenization. It addresses a recurring governance problem: local autonomy can become isolation, while interoperability can become absorption. Effective coordination requires connections strong enough to manage real interdependence but bounded enough to preserve local agency, cultural meaning, privacy, and the ability to leave.

Nested Economies therefore governs:

- allocation of decision authority across scales;
- creation and dissolution of BAZ-to-BAZ compacts;
- political conditions for cross-scale financial and technical interfaces;
- recognition of local credentials and practices;
- solidarity and equalization among differently situated economies;
- shared infrastructure and knowledge commons;
- cross-boundary externalities;
- local counter-model and challenge rights;
- exit, fork, fallback, and graceful degradation;
- monitoring of whether integration preserves autonomy and improves actual conditions.

## 1.2 Boundaries

Nested Economies does **not** itself govern:

- AUBI Layer 1–3 eligibility or payout calculations;
- LMCI-M or LMCI-A methodology;
- Hearts issuance, reserve ratios, redemption, or demurrage;
- financial anomaly or fraud determinations;
- ICTL rate calculations or settlement controls;
- Green Job Score or care-work valuation standards;
- Community Provider professional standards;
- ecological verification methods;
- Treaty enforcement outside economic-interface questions.

Those functions remain with AUBI, Financial Systems, Work in Liberation, ecological governance frameworks, and the Treaty architecture. This framework specifies **how their decisions and records cross jurisdictional boundaries** and which local rights remain protected.

## 1.3 Right Relationship

Economic coordination is legitimate when it supports balanced, regenerative relationships among:

- individuals and communities;
- formal and informal economies;
- local autonomy and shared obligation;
- present needs and future generations;
- human and non-human communities;
- economic exchange and non-monetized life;
- technological interoperability and cultural sovereignty.

Right Relationship is not reduced to transaction volume, formal participation, or standardized metrics. Communities may choose lower commercialization, retain local forms of reciprocity, protect sacred practices from valuation, or preserve non-comparable categories while still participating in shared systems.

## 1.4 Constitutional Design Commitments

All Nested Economies mechanisms must preserve:

1. **Subsidiarity:** original authority remains local unless a justified cross-boundary need requires limited transfer.
2. **Capability support before centralization:** weak capacity triggers support, not automatic loss of authority.
3. **Purpose-limited delegation:** coordination bodies receive only specified powers.
4. **Return of authority:** delegated powers expire, return, or narrow when their justification ends.
5. **Protected difference:** interoperability permits local exceptions and non-comparable elements.
6. **Meaningful exit and fork:** communities can leave arrangements without losing identity, essential entitlements, or their own records.
7. **Rights floors:** local autonomy cannot shield exploitation, discrimination, ecological destruction, or violations of Treaty rights.
8. **Protected unobservability:** not all relational, cultural, spiritual, ecological, or personal knowledge must become transferable data.
9. **Contestability:** affected communities can challenge models, mappings, classifications, standards, and authority transfers.
10. **Outcome validation:** integration is judged by material, social, distributive, ecological, and autonomy outcomes—not only by technical connection or transaction growth.

# 2. Core Economic Architecture

## 2.1 Hearts

**Hearts** are the formal regenerative currency used for care, community services, and related economic exchange. Their issuance, reserve backing, redemption, circulation, demurrage, and financial controls are governed by **Financial Systems** and the relevant AUBI provisions.

Nested Economies governs:

- where Hearts are accepted;
- how BAZs recognize one another's providers and transactions;
- how local rules interact with common standards;
- how financial interfaces preserve local autonomy;
- how trade and solidarity compacts use Hearts;
- how BAZs retain fallback and exit capacity.

A BAZ may not redefine Hearts financial controls in ways that compromise system-wide settlement, participant rights, or shared obligations. Global financial authorities may not use common standards to determine unrelated local priorities or erase protected local distinctions.

## 2.2 Leaves

**Leaves** are non-fungible ecological records and reward instruments. They are not a second general-purpose currency and are not automatically interchangeable with Hearts.

Recognition or settlement of Leaves across BAZs requires:

- ecological verification under the relevant framework;
- FLP valuation standards where work valuation is involved;
- AUBI rules where Leaves affect Layer 2 allocation;
- Financial Systems rules where settlement or translation occurs;
- Slow-Variable Decision Protocol review where long-term ecological conditions are affected;
- Indigenous and community authority where land, sacred sites, or traditional knowledge are implicated.

A Cross-Scale Interface Contract must state whether a Leaf is being **recognized, verified, settled, converted, aggregated, or published**. Permission for one operation does not imply permission for the others.

## 2.3 Love Ledger

The **Love Ledger** recognizes informal care, gratitude, reciprocity, cultural contribution, and relational life through voluntary, non-monetized records and narratives.

Love Ledger participation:

- is never required for access to AUBI, Hearts, public services, trade, or BAZ membership;
- may remain local, private, oral, non-digital, or community-stewarded;
- does not mechanically feed LMCI-M, LMCI-A, ICTL rates, creditworthiness, fraud models, or individual entitlement;
- may supply voluntary context or hypotheses where participants consent;
- may not be used to rank human worth or create a portable social reputation score.

Institutional transparency applies to Love Ledger governance, access rules, data use, and aggregate system operation—not to making people's relational lives publicly visible.

## 2.4 AUBI

The four-layered AUBI architecture supplies the universal material-security, contribution-recognition, equity, and stabilization functions used within nested economies.

Nested Economies does not repeat AUBI rules. It ensures that:

- AUBI rights survive movement across BAZ boundaries;
- local interfaces do not silently change entitlement;
- BAZ compacts do not require surrender of Layer 1 protection;
- Layer 2 records remain separate from population flourishing measurement;
- Layer 3 needs and reparations are not treated as trade or contribution rewards;
- mobile, stateless, displaced, and nomadic people are not excluded by jurisdictional gaps.

## 2.5 Formal and Informal Economic Coexistence

Local economic health includes formal providers, household provision, mutual aid, commons activity, non-monetized care, subsistence, cultural exchange, and market activity.

The framework does not assume that more formal transactions are always better. A BAZ may legitimately maintain:

- strong non-monetized care;
- low commercial exchange;
- collective provisioning;
- local gift economies;
- seasonal exchange rhythms;
- protected sacred or cultural activities;
- non-transferable local credits alongside Hearts, where compatible with financial and rights rules.

Operational systems must not pressure communities to increase transaction volume merely to improve a dashboard.

# 3. Scale Architecture

## 3.1 Two Constitutional Governing Tiers

The framework uses two constitutional governing tiers.

### BAZ authority

A **BAZ Council** holds original authority over local economic governance within the limits of Treaty rights, shared commons duties, AUBI entitlements, and Financial Systems standards.

### Global GGF authority

Global bodies exercise authority only in constitutionally specified domains, including:

- system-wide financial stability;
- global commons obligations;
- Treaty rights;
- interoperability standards necessary for cross-BAZ settlement;
- systemic externalities;
- planetary crisis response;
- adjudication where lower-scale processes cannot resolve cross-boundary conflict.

No global body acquires general economic planning authority merely because it operates common infrastructure.

## 3.2 Local BAZ Economy

A BAZ may govern:

- local provider chartering;
- local participatory budgeting;
- community procurement;
- land trusts and commons institutions;
- service priorities;
- local accessibility and outreach;
- local compact participation;
- local recognition practices;
- local data stewardship;
- implementation of AUBI and Hearts within governing framework constraints.

Local authority is not absolute. It remains subject to:

- universal rights;
- protected AUBI obligations;
- ecological and intergenerational safeguards;
- valid cross-boundary compact duties;
- financial settlement and reserve rules;
- independent review where local authority harms protected groups.

## 3.3 Regional Economic Space

A **regional economic space** is a factual pattern of interdependence, such as:

- a watershed;
- commuting or labor mobility;
- a supply network;
- a shared energy grid;
- a transport corridor;
- a cultural region;
- a food system;
- a group of trading BAZs.

A regional economic space does not itself possess authority.

## 3.4 Voluntary Coordination Layer

Two or more BAZs may create a **coordination layer** through a compact for a defined purpose, including:

- shared infrastructure;
- mutual procurement;
- trade facilitation;
- emergency mutual aid;
- technical services;
- ecological stewardship;
- dispute resolution;
- solidarity pooling;
- shared learning;
- administrative services.

A coordination layer is:

- function-specific;
- created by explicit compact;
- governed by participating BAZs;
- unable to expand its own mandate;
- transparent about delegated authority;
- subject to periodic review;
- terminable under stated exit conditions;
- non-sovereign unless separately authorized.

## 3.5 When a Coordination Layer Becomes a Governing Tier

A coordination layer exercises governmental authority if it can independently:

- levy compulsory taxes or contributions;
- bind a non-consenting BAZ;
- impose sanctions;
- exclude a BAZ from essential infrastructure without due process;
- alter rights or AUBI access;
- prevent exit;
- permanently control shared assets;
- create new powers without renewed authorization;
- override local law outside the delegated function.

Such powers require explicit constitutional authorization, representation, appeal, audit, sunset, and authority-return rules. They may not arise through technical standards, grant conditions, platform control, or administrative custom.

## 3.6 Global Standard versus Local Implementation

A global standard is legitimate when it defines the minimum compatibility, rights, security, or commons conditions necessary for shared coordination.

A global standard may specify:

- settlement message fields;
- minimum rights and appeal procedures;
- auditability of public authority;
- security requirements;
- reserve or redemption rules under Financial Systems;
- ecological safeguards;
- shared-commons limits.

It should not prescribe:

- local cultural meaning;
- unnecessary personal data;
- one institutional form where several are compatible;
- local service priorities unrelated to cross-boundary effects;
- compulsory monetization of informal activity;
- uniform comparison of sacred or non-comparable knowledge.

# 4. Economic Subsidiarity Test

## 4.1 Presumption

> **Authority remains at the lowest scale capable of addressing the condition without imposing ungoverned material harm on other communities, shared commons, or protected rights.**

The party proposing upward transfer bears the burden of justification.

## 4.2 Trigger

The Economic Subsidiarity Test is required before:

- transferring a local power upward;
- creating a compulsory regional function;
- imposing a global standard that materially changes local operations;
- conditioning access to essential common infrastructure;
- centralizing data or validation;
- mandating a solidarity contribution;
- overriding a local exception;
- extending emergency coordination authority;
- preventing a BAZ from exiting a compact.

## 4.3 Test Sequence

### Step 1: Define the condition

State the actual condition requiring response. Do not define the problem as mere non-compliance with the proposed solution.

### Step 2: Map affected parties and boundaries

Identify:

- affected BAZs;
- mobile and unrepresented populations;
- non-member communities;
- ecological systems;
- future generations;
- shared infrastructure;
- rights holders.

### Step 3: Test local capability

Assess whether the lower scale can address the condition with:

- its existing authority;
- additional resources;
- shared technical support;
- temporary personnel;
- mutual aid;
- slower implementation;
- administrative simplification;
- local institutional redesign.

Lack of funding or administrative staff does not by itself justify loss of authority.

### Step 4: Test voluntary coordination

Before compulsory transfer, consider whether the condition can be addressed through:

- bilateral agreement;
- multilateral compact;
- shared service;
- mutual recognition;
- common procurement;
- federated administration;
- reciprocal support;
- bounded arbitration.

### Step 5: Identify the minimum necessary authority

Specify the narrowest power required. Separate:

- information access;
- coordination;
- standard setting;
- funding;
- administration;
- adjudication;
- enforcement.

Need for one does not establish need for all.

### Step 6: Protect local discretion

List decisions that remain local and local variants that remain compatible.

### Step 7: Assess rights, distribution, and cultural effects

Examine whether the transfer:

- burdens lower-capacity BAZs;
- excludes people not visible to administrative systems;
- erases Indigenous or local categories;
- creates dependency;
- concentrates infrastructure control;
- makes exit prohibitively costly;
- shifts risk without representation.

### Step 8: Establish review, sunset, and return

Every authority transfer must state:

- start date;
- duration;
- review cadence;
- success and failure conditions;
- authority-return condition;
- data-return or deletion rules;
- exit pathway;
- restoration where the transfer caused harm.

## 4.4 Subsidiarity Decision Record

Each consequential subsidiarity decision publishes:

- proposed authority change;
- condition and evidence;
- alternative responses;
- affected parties;
- local capability assessment;
- voluntary-coordination assessment;
- minimum power required;
- retained local powers;
- uncertainty and disagreement;
- selected arrangement;
- duration and sunset;
- challenge rights;
- authority-return trigger.

Where evidence does not establish that upward transfer is necessary, the finding is:

> **Centralization not justified—support or voluntary coordination required.**

## 4.5 Authority Return

Delegated authority returns or narrows when:

- the triggering condition ends;
- local capability is restored;
- a less intrusive mechanism becomes available;
- the coordination layer exceeds mandate;
- the arrangement fails its independent outcomes;
- the authorizing compact expires;
- participating BAZs validly withdraw;
- the transfer creates greater harm than the condition it addressed.

Improved administrative speed alone does not justify retaining authority.

## 5.2 Decision Separation

The following functions should remain institutionally distinguishable where consequence is material:

- proposing a change;
- supplying observations;
- estimating the condition;
- authorizing action;
- administering action;
- auditing outcomes;
- adjudicating disputes.

One small institution may perform several functions during early piloting only where conflicts are declared, secondary review exists, and no fundamental right depends on a single unreviewed judgment.

## 5.3 Standing

Standing to submit evidence or challenge a cross-scale decision extends to:

- member BAZs;
- materially affected non-member BAZs;
- transition-stage BAZs;
- Indigenous nations and knowledge authorities;
- mobile, nomadic, displaced, and stateless populations;
- affected providers and participants;
- shared-commons guardians;
- relevant Offices of the Adversary and independent auditors.

Voting rights may differ by compact, but lack of formal membership does not erase affected-party standing.

# 6. Cross-Scale Interface Contracts

## 6.1 Purpose

A **Cross-Scale Interface Contract** defines how institutions coordinate without assuming that all value, data, identities, or practices are directly comparable.

It applies whenever a material object crosses a BAZ or framework boundary, including:

- financial settlement;
- provider recognition;
- credentials;
- ecological records;
- aggregate telemetry;
- solidarity claims;
- shared infrastructure access;
- governance decisions;
- public reporting.

## 6.2 Interface Operations

Each interface must identify which operations it performs:

1. **Recognition** — accepting that a local status or record has standing.
2. **Authentication** — confirming provenance or authority.
3. **Verification** — assessing whether a claim satisfies a defined standard.
4. **Settlement** — completing a financial obligation.
5. **Conversion** — transforming one unit or claim into another.
6. **Portability** — enabling use in another jurisdiction.
7. **Aggregation** — combining records for analysis or governance.
8. **Publication** — making information public or widely accessible.

Authorization for one operation does not authorize the others.

## 6.3 Required Contract Fields

Every material interface records:

1. participating jurisdictions and institutions;
2. purpose;
3. legal or constitutional authority;
4. object being exchanged;
5. permitted operations;
6. prohibited operations;
7. required data;
8. prohibited or protected data;
9. local semantic fields preserved;
10. transformation, mapping, or settlement method;
11. uncertainty and information loss;
12. provenance;
13. data owner and steward;
14. storage location and access control;
15. retention, return, and deletion;
16. correction and challenge process;
17. accessibility and offline fallback;
18. failure and rollback mode;
19. exit and portability;
20. protocol version and change process;
21. audit and outcome measures;
22. dispute resolution.

## 6.4 Semantic Preservation

An interface must disclose what local meaning is lost during translation.

Where a local category has no faithful global equivalent, the system may use:

- a locally preserved field;
- a bounded attestation;
- a non-comparable category;
- a narrative appendix;
- a protected exception;
- no translation.

The absence of a standardized field does not imply absence of value.

## 6.5 Data Minimization

Interfaces transfer only information required for their purpose.

For example, cross-BAZ settlement may require:

- transaction amount;
- valid wallet or institutional identifiers;
- settlement status;
- relevant compliance attestations.

It does not ordinarily require:

- a person's complete care history;
- Love Ledger narratives;
- health or disability details;
- cultural identity records;
- generalized reputation;
- unrelated location history;
- sacred knowledge.

## 6.6 Reliability and Source Independence

Interfaces publish whether nominally distinct channels share:

- observers;
- platforms;
- algorithms;
- funding bodies;
- governance authorities;
- incentives;
- source records.

Shared sources must not be represented as independent corroboration.

## 6.7 Interface Change Governance

Changes to:

- mappings;
- required fields;
- conversion methods;
- eligibility rules;
- aggregation;
- retention;
- publication;
- reliability flags;
- human/algorithmic balance

are governance changes, not neutral maintenance.

Material financial changes follow Financial Systems NARP. Material compact changes require affected-BAZ amendment procedures. Ecological and cultural changes require the applicable slow-variable, Indigenous, and consent safeguards.

# 7. Information Classes and Protected Non-Convertibility

## 7.1 Class I — Required Institutional Information

Information may be required across boundaries where necessary for public accountability or shared obligations, including:

- aggregate reserve compliance;
- settlement status;
- institutional audit records;
- compact finances;
- shared-infrastructure performance;
- rights and appeal performance;
- shared-commons impacts.

Institutional transparency does not authorize unnecessary individual exposure.

## 7.2 Class II — Aggregate Operational Telemetry

Operational telemetry may include:

- trade flows;
- settlement success;
- provider capacity;
- waiting times;
- infrastructure load;
- failed transactions;
- access gaps;
- administrative backlog;
- compact participation.

Operational telemetry may trigger review. It is not LMCI-M and does not prove flourishing, sovereignty, or equity merely because activity rises.

## 7.3 Class III — Voluntary Narrative and Contextual Evidence

This class includes:

- Love Ledger narratives;
- voluntary community accounts;
- qualitative experience;
- contextual interpretations;
- locally authorized cultural reports.

These records may contextualize decisions or generate hypotheses. They are not automatically monetized, scored, aggregated, or published.

## 7.4 Class IV — Protected Local, Sacred, Personal, or Ecological Knowledge

Protected information may remain:

- local;
- non-digital;
- non-comparable;
- held by a knowledge authority;
- available only as a bounded conclusion;
- represented through a veto, condition, or attestation;
- unavailable to general audit.

The system must support **protected non-transfer** as an interface outcome.

## 7.5 Protected Non-Convertibility

The following are not presumed convertible into Hearts, Leaves, market price, LMCI, credit, or portable reputation:

- human dignity;
- sacred knowledge;
- spiritual sincerity;
- informal affection or gratitude;
- cultural identity;
- community membership;
- political loyalty;
- private relationship quality;
- Treaty rights;
- AUBI Layer 1 entitlement;
- Indigenous sovereignty;
- ecological existence value.

A specific contribution or ecological outcome may be recognized under an authorized protocol without converting the underlying person, relationship, culture, or living system into a financial unit.

# 8. Value Recognition, Validation, and Settlement

## 8.1 Separation of Functions

Nested Economies distinguishes:

- **local recognition** of a practice or contribution;
- **validation** under AUBI, Work in Liberation, FLP, or ecological standards;
- **financial settlement** under Financial Systems;
- **cross-BAZ recognition** through a compact;
- **population-level evaluation** through independent LMCI-M and other outcome systems.

No one framework or data stream owns all five functions.

## 8.2 Care Contributions

Care contributions are validated under AUBI LMCI-A and relevant Work in Liberation protocols. Cross-BAZ recognition may accept a valid contribution credential without importing all underlying personal evidence.

An interface must preserve distinctions among:

- occurrence;
- recipient acceptance where appropriate;
- duration or scope;
- role;
- additionality;
- safeguarding;
- outcome evidence;
- uncertainty;
- participant preference for non-monetized recognition.

Nested Economies does not define a universal “Proof of Care score × multiplier” conversion.

## 8.3 Ecological Contributions

Ecological work and Leaves are governed by ecological verification, AUBI, FLP standards, Financial Systems settlement, and slow-variable safeguards.

Cross-BAZ recognition must disclose:

- baseline;
- additionality;
- permanence;
- leakage;
- uncertainty;
- land and knowledge authority;
- co-benefits and co-harms;
- reversal conditions.

A compact may recognize an ecological record without making it freely convertible.

## 8.4 Time Banks and Local Credits

A time bank or local credit may interoperate through:

- mutual recognition;
- reciprocal access;
- compact-specific settlement;
- bounded conversion;
- non-convertible credential recognition.

There is no universal formula based on hours, regional wages, and a global Hearts index.

Any conversion requires:

- participant consent;
- a stated purpose;
- local authorization;
- distributional review;
- transparent method;
- exit and fallback;
- no compulsory monetization of previously non-monetized practices.

## 8.5 Fiat and Heartstarter

Fiat contributions and Heartstarter capitalization follow Financial Systems §5. Nested Economies may coordinate applications, local matching, and cross-BAZ learning but does not redefine reserve, ceiling, or release rules.

## 8.6 Carbon and Other Ecological Credits

Carbon or biodiversity credits are not translated through a simple tonnage multiplier. Any interface defers to the relevant ecological framework and must address rights, additionality, permanence, leakage, uncertainty, and affected communities.

# 9. Voluntary BAZ Compacts and Coordination Layers

## 9.1 Compact Purposes

BAZs may form compacts for:

- trade;
- shared infrastructure;
- energy;
- food systems;
- transport;
- mutual aid;
- ecological stewardship;
- technical services;
- provider recognition;
- emergency response;
- solidarity;
- research and learning.

A compact must not become a general authority merely because several functions are convenient to combine.

## 9.2 Compact Requirements

Every compact specifies:

1. purpose and scope;
2. members and affected non-members;
3. delegated powers;
4. prohibited powers;
5. decision rule;
6. representation;
7. funding and cost allocation;
8. institutional host;
9. data and interface rules;
10. audit;
11. dispute resolution;
12. emergency authority;
13. amendment;
14. exit;
15. asset and liability allocation on exit;
16. sunset and renewal;
17. authority-return conditions.

## 9.3 Delegation

Delegated functions may include:

- administering a shared service;
- maintaining infrastructure;
- coordinating procurement;
- operating a common technical node;
- managing a bounded fund;
- facilitating dispute resolution.

Delegation does not transfer residual sovereignty. Silence in a compact is interpreted in favor of retained BAZ authority.

## 9.4 Representation

Compact governance should reflect:

- participating BAZs;
- population where appropriate;
- affected Indigenous nations;
- small-BAZ protection;
- mobile and cross-boundary populations;
- users of shared infrastructure;
- ecological guardians where relevant.

No single formula is required for all compacts, but representation and power asymmetries must be explicit.

## 9.5 Exit

A BAZ may exit according to the compact without losing:

- AUBI rights;
- its own institutional identity;
- access to its own data;
- undisputed balances;
- records required to continue local operations;
- rights to essential services during an orderly transition.

Exit may include fair settlement of shared liabilities and protection against strategic abandonment. Exit costs must not be designed to make withdrawal impossible.

## 9.6 Forking and Alternative Standards

A BAZ or group of BAZs may fork a coordination service or adopt an alternative compatible implementation where:

- settlement and rights obligations remain met;
- shared assets are handled fairly;
- data portability exists;
- affected parties receive notice;
- the fork does not evade valid ecological or human-rights duties.

Forkability is a safeguard against platform capture and institutional stagnation.

# 10. Solidarity and Resource Justice

## 10.1 Purpose

Solidarity addresses unequal capacity, shocks, inherited injustice, ecological burdens, and shared obligations without turning every difference into a demand for uniformity.

Solidarity is not synonymous with permanent fiscal redistribution. It may take the form of:

- grants;
- shared services;
- liquidity support;
- technical assistance;
- infrastructure;
- debt relief;
- reparative transfer;
- provider support;
- mutual aid;
- temporary equalization;
- recognition of ecological stewardship burden.

## 10.2 Trigger Classes

A Solidarity Protocol may be activated for:

1. **Acute shock:** disaster, conflict, infrastructure failure, or sudden displacement.
2. **Structural capability gap:** persistent inability to provide essential services.
3. **Shared infrastructure:** jointly beneficial investment beyond one BAZ's capacity.
4. **Ecological stewardship burden:** costs borne for preserving shared ecosystems.
5. **Reparative obligation:** specific historical, ecological, or economic harm.
6. **Transition cost:** temporary burden from adopting shared standards or ending extraction.
7. **Cross-boundary externality:** costs imposed by another economy or common rule.
8. **Essential-service equalization:** minimum capability required to protect rights.

## 10.3 Solidarity State Estimate

Before a non-emergency transfer, the responsible body publishes:

- trigger class;
- affected population and BAZs;
- current condition;
- plausible causes;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- local account;
- role of existing trade or interface rules;
- distributional pattern;
- informal and non-market provision;
- alternative interventions;
- proposed transfer or support;
- expected independent outcomes;
- duration and exit conditions.

Where the evidence cannot distinguish a capacity gap from measurement failure, voluntary low commercialization, extractive trade rules, or local governance failure, the official conclusion may be:

> **Solidarity need established; instrument unresolved—diagnosis and local co-design required.**

## 10.4 Contribution Bases

A solidarity arrangement may use:

- negotiated member contribution;
- compact fee;
- ICTL or settlement-fee share;
- GCF allocation;
- commons dividend;
- net fiscal-capacity assessment;
- responsibility for caused harm;
- voluntary mutual-aid contribution;
- resource-tax allocation.

The previous 10% rule is retained only as an **illustrative pilot parameter**. A compact may choose a contribution of up to 10% of a precisely defined eligible net coordination revenue base, but no universal 10% levy applies across all BAZs or transfer purposes.

## 10.5 Recipient Logic

Recipient eligibility may consider:

- essential-service gaps;
- shock severity;
- capacity and infrastructure;
- ecological stewardship;
- reparative claim;
- population and access;
- external dependency;
- local contribution capacity;
- administrative burden;
- missing populations.

Low formal revenue alone does not establish need, because it may reflect sufficiency, informal provision, low commercialization, or incomplete reporting.

## 10.6 Safeguards

Every compulsory or shared-fund solidarity arrangement includes:

- affected-BAZ participation;
- public method;
- hardship relief for contributors;
- no requirement to expose unnecessary personal or sacred data;
- distributional review;
- appeal;
- administrative support;
- anti-capture audit;
- sunset and reassessment;
- a pathway from transfer to capability where appropriate.

## 10.7 Restitution versus Solidarity

Where one actor caused identifiable harm, the response may be **restitution or reparation**, not generalized solidarity. A solidarity pool must not conceal responsibility for extraction, pollution, dispossession, or exploitative trade.

## 10.8 End and Conversion Conditions

A transfer changes, ends, or converts to another instrument when:

- the shock ends;
- the capability gap closes;
- the diagnosis is falsified;
- the transfer creates harmful dependency;
- the contributing community enters hardship;
- an infrastructure investment replaces recurring support;
- a rule change is more appropriate than continued transfer;
- affected BAZs agree to a new compact.

Transfer volume is not itself evidence of success.

# 12. Governance, NARP, and Slow Variables

## 12.1 Social Resilience Council

The **Social Resilience Council (SRC)**:

- maintains global economic coordination standards within its mandate;
- manages relevant GCF interfaces;
- coordinates with Financial Systems and AUBI;
- supports BAZ and compact capacity;
- receives cross-scale evidence;
- cannot use common infrastructure to acquire residual local authority;
- remains subject to AUBI governance, Accountable Coordination Audit, BAZ reformation triggers, Office of the Adversary review, Earth Council safeguards, and Meta-Governance oversight.

Financial supply, reserve, Treasury, ICTL, and NARP authorities are governed by Financial Systems.

## 12.2 Fractal Labor Parliament

The **Fractal Labor Parliament (FLP)** defines work-valuation and occupational standards under its governing frameworks. Nested Economies may facilitate cross-BAZ recognition but does not create independent wage, care, or Green Job Score formulas.

## 12.3 Earth Council

The **Earth Council** may intervene where cross-scale economic arrangements threaten:

- Indigenous sovereignty;
- ecological integrity;
- sacred sites;
- Treaty rights;
- long-term commons conditions.

Earth Council authority does not convert protected knowledge into a general data obligation.

## 12.4 NARP

Material changes to ICTL, financial interface standards, settlement, or financial observation methods follow **Financial Systems §12, Notice and Adaptive Response Protocol**.

Nested Economies extends the same principles to non-financial cross-scale changes:

- publication;
- plain-language explanation;
- affected-BAZ analysis;
- alternative designs;
- local counter-models;
- uncertainty;
- objection rights;
- amendment;
- sunset and reversal.

## 12.5 Slow-Variable Decision Protocol

Changes qualify for AUBI's **Slow-Variable Decision Category (SVDC)** where they materially affect long-horizon ecological conditions observed primarily through multigenerational or place-embedded knowledge.

Examples include:

- ecological exchange standards;
- carbon or biodiversity mappings;
- shared watershed extraction rules;
- long-term infrastructure affecting habitat;
- Leaves multipliers tied to slow ecological outcomes;
- authority transfers affecting Indigenous stewardship.

Such decisions require the applicable LEKH quorum and community authority.

## 12.6 Cross-Level Trust Protocol

The inherited **Cross-Level Trust Protocol** is operationalized through:

- Economic Subsidiarity Test;
- Cross-Scale Interface Contracts;
- decision-rights matrix;
- institutional and compact audits;
- Observation Independence Maps;
- participant and BAZ correction rights;
- outcome review;
- authority-return tests.

A biannual review is the default pilot cadence, with higher frequency for high-risk or crisis arrangements.

# 13. Monitoring and the Nested Economy Condition Dashboard

## 13.1 No Composite Sovereignty or Integration Score

The inherited goals of 70% sovereignty, 80% equity, and 90% interoperability remain historical aspirations, not constitutional thresholds or composite scores.

Sovereignty, equity, and interoperability are multidimensional. A single percentage may hide:

- coercion;
- exclusion;
- dependency;
- subgroup harm;
- poor fallback;
- data extraction;
- local monopoly;
- forced standardization.

v3.2 therefore uses a multidimensional dashboard with uncertainty and narrative interpretation.

## 13.2 Information-System Separation

Four information products remain distinct:

| Information product | Purpose | Prohibited interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **LMCI-M** | Independent population-level flourishing estimate | Not derived from transaction volume, logged care, or interoperability |
| **LMCI-A** | Contribution-level allocation | Not a community wellbeing or sovereignty score |
| **Operational telemetry** | Functioning of trade, settlement, providers, infrastructure, access, and administration | More activity is not proof of flourishing |
| **Love Ledger Narrative Feed** | Voluntary relational recognition and context | Not mechanically monetized, scored, or aggregated |

The previous formula of 40% Hearts transactions, 30% logged care hours, and 30% interoperability is retired and may not be labelled LMCI.

## 13.3 Local Autonomy Dimension

Monitor separately:

- decisions retained locally;
- delegated functions;
- concentration of external authority;
- exit feasibility;
- fork feasibility;
- external reserve and funding dependence;
- essential-provider concentration;
- platform dependence;
- data portability;
- local exception preservation.

## 13.4 Material Access Dimension

Monitor:

- access to food, housing, utilities, transport, care, and communication;
- unmet need and failed requests;
- affordability;
- geographic access;
- linguistic and disability access;
- digital and offline access;
- external-supply vulnerability;
- missing and underserved populations.

## 13.5 Interoperability Dimension

Monitor:

- settlement success;
- interface uptime;
- processing latency;
- dispute and correction rates;
- portability;
- compatibility;
- fallback performance;
- locally preserved fields and exceptions;
- semantic-loss reports;
- dependency on a single platform or gateway.

High technical compatibility does not establish legitimate interoperability if exit, semantic integrity, privacy, or local control are absent.

## 13.6 Solidarity Dimension

Monitor:

- diagnosed need;
- adequacy;
- distribution;
- duration;
- administrative burden;
- capability development;
- dependency trajectory;
- contributor burden;
- outcomes;
- unresolved causal disagreement.

## 13.7 Local Economic Resilience Dimension

Monitor:

- provider diversity;
- reserve and Treasury status through Financial Systems;
- supply-chain diversity;
- shock recovery;
- administrative capacity;
- mutual-aid capacity;
- informal-care burden;
- dependence on one external buyer or supplier;

## 13.8 Rights and Legitimacy Dimension

Monitor:

- participation and consent;
- correction and appeal access;
- unresolved disputes;

- retaliation complaints;
- trust;

- institutional capture warnings.

## 13.9 LMCI-M Context

LMCI-M is displayed separately as an independent population-level estimate. It may show that a technically integrated economy is not improving love, meaning, connection, ecological health, or material security.

LMCI-M movement does not by itself prove that a specific interface or compact caused the change.

## 13.10 Uncertainty and Partial Identification

Dashboard publication includes:

- data age;
- missingness;
- subgroup coverage;
- source dependencies;
- uncertainty intervals or qualitative confidence;
- contested interpretations;
- method versions;
- known blind spots.

Where several materially different conditions fit the observed data, the dashboard states that the condition is unresolved rather than presenting a false singular diagnosis.

# 14. Missing Populations, Observer Independence, and Community Weavers

## 14.1 Missing-Population Sentinel

Administrative systems observe people already connected to formal institutions. Each BAZ and major compact therefore maintains privacy-preserving channels to estimate populations who are:

- eligible but unenrolled;
- geographically isolated;
- digitally excluded;
- disabled or language-excluded;
- stateless, displaced, mobile, or nomadic;
- distrustful of official systems;
- dependent on informal provision;
- excluded by local power structures.

Sources may include:

- trusted community organizations;
- mutual-aid groups;
- anonymous reasons-for-non-participation surveys;
- demographic discrepancy analysis;
- service-access data;
- outreach records;
- independent sampling.

Absence from the ledger is not evidence of absence of need.

## 14.2 Community Weaver Role

Community Weavers may:

- train participants;
- facilitate Hearts and Love Ledger access;
- support accessible and offline participation;
- help communities articulate needs;
- relay local interpretations;
- support evidence submission;
- identify interface friction;
- facilitate stakeholder dialogue.

## 14.3 Prohibited Sole-Source Roles

A Community Weaver may not alone:

- determine fraud;
- adjudicate an appeal;
- certify LMCI-M;
- validate their own programme's success;
- represent an Indigenous nation without authorization;
- determine whether integration or solidarity is legitimate;
- control both primary data entry and independent audit;
- judge cultural or spiritual sincerity.

## 14.4 Observer-Independence Safeguards

Each BAZ and coordination layer maintains an **Observation Independence Map** identifying shared:

- observers;
- data sources;
- platforms;
- algorithms;
- funders;
- governance bodies;
- incentives.

Minimum safeguards include:

- secondary review;
- independent sampling;
- participant correction;
- confidential complaint route;
- anti-retaliation;
- conflict declarations;
- workload and burnout monitoring;
- rotating audit;
- no single-source adverse decision.

## 14.5 Compensation and Incentive Disclosure

Where Weavers, validators, providers, or coordination bodies are compensated through the systems they observe, the relationship is disclosed. Their evidence remains admissible but is not treated as independent corroboration without another channel.

# 15. Implementation Stages

## 15.1 Stage 1 — Convening and Boundary Mapping

A community begins by:

- forming a Convening Circle;
- identifying recurring needs;
- mapping existing formal, informal, cultural, and commons economies;
- identifying external dependencies;
- identifying practices that should remain non-monetized or non-transferable;
- identifying excluded and unrepresented populations;
- mapping neighboring BAZs and shared ecological systems.

No formal BAZ Council or cross-scale authority is created at this stage.

## 15.2 Stage 2 — Proto-Economy

The community:

- establishes a Proto-Treasury under AUBI and Financial Systems rules;
- recruits initial Community Providers;
- creates accessible formal transaction records;
- provides offline and low-tech access;
- establishes correction and complaint routes;
- trains Community Weavers with secondary-review support;
- uses Heartstarter where authorized.

## 15.3 Stage 3 — Local Operating Capacity

The community demonstrates:

- practical provider diversity;
- reserve compliance;
- functioning low-velocity diagnostic;
- access and waiting-time monitoring;
- Missing-Population Sentinel;
- administrative-capacity monitoring;
- fraud and correction processes;
- separation of formal telemetry, LMCI, and Love Ledger recognition;
- independent review for consequential decisions.

Low activity caused by sufficiency, seasonality, or voluntary non-monetized provision does not force formal expansion.

## 15.4 Stage 4 — BAZ Governance Formalization

Before formalization, the BAZ establishes:

- a local decision-rights matrix;
- Observation Contracts and State Estimate Records where required;
- individual and provider epistemic rights;
- an Observation Independence Map;
- exit and fork provisions;
- local data-sovereignty rules;
- protected unobservability;
- administrative and appeal capacity;
- clear interfaces with AUBI, Financial Systems, and Work in Liberation.

## 15.5 Stage 5 — Bilateral Interface

The BAZ tests one or more limited interfaces with another BAZ:

- provider recognition;
- trade settlement;
- mutual aid;
- shared service;
- data portability;
- ecological coordination.

Each uses a Cross-Scale Interface Contract, dispute process, fallback, and exit.

## 15.6 Stage 6 — Networked Coordination

Several BAZs may establish:

- shared infrastructure;
- procurement;
- an optional solidarity arrangement;
- technical services;
- a coordination compact.

The arrangement remains non-sovereign unless expressly authorized.

## 15.7 Stage 7 — Global Integration

A mature BAZ may connect to:

- ICTL;
- GCF programmes;
- global settlement and standards;
- cross-regional compacts;
- Treaty commons systems.

Global integration requires demonstrated preservation of:

- local authority;
- rights;
- data portability;
- protected difference;
- NARP standing;
- counter-model capacity;
- fallback and exit.

## 15.8 Minimum Viable Implementation

Where resources are limited:

| Capacity | Prioritize | Defer |
|---|---|---|
| Community group | Need mapping, informal exchange, simple local record, protected non-monetization | Treasury, ICTL, global dashboards |
| Neighborhood or town | Proto-Treasury, providers, correction, offline access | Regional authority, complex conversion |
| BAZ candidate | Provider diversity, reserve stability, diagnostics, missing-population and capacity monitoring | Broad trade network |
| Formal BAZ | Decision rights, interface contracts, appeals, exit, independence map | Global integration until local systems are stable |
| BAZ network | One bounded compact and shared service | General regional government |

# 16. Crisis, Failure, Exit, and Graceful Degradation

## 16.1 Crisis Coordination

A crisis may justify temporary:

- mutual aid;
- shared logistics;
- emergency provider support;
- technical fallback;
- temporary coordination authority;
- solidarity transfer;
- accelerated interface approval.

Crisis authority does not automatically permit:

- diversion of protected AUBI Layer 1;
- permanent centralization;
- compulsory Love Ledger reporting;
- permanent model or sensor changes;
- suspension of Indigenous or individual rights;
- indefinite prevention of exit.

## 16.2 Crisis Constraints

Emergency arrangements:

- state the trigger;
- identify affected groups;
- preserve pre-intervention data;
- define protected minimums;
- expire automatically;
- prohibit renewal by relabelling;
- publish independent outcome measures;
- provide challenge rights;
- undergo after-action review;
- restore delegated authority and data where required.

## 16.3 Interface Failure

Cross-scale interfaces must have fallback modes for:

- network outage;
- settlement failure;
- corrupted data;
- compromised platform;
- disputed mapping;
- institutional capture;
- inability to verify a protected claim.

Fallback may include:

- local operation;
- offline settlement queue;
- manual verification;
- bilateral clearing;
- temporary recognition without conversion;
- suspension of new transactions while preserving existing rights.

## 16.4 Compact Failure

If a compact fails:

- essential services continue during transition;
- liabilities are disclosed;
- records return to their owners;
- shared assets are allocated under the compact;
- AUBI and undisputed balances remain protected;
- failure is documented without forcing a false diagnosis;
- member BAZs may form a narrower replacement.

## 16.5 Centralization Failure

Where an upward authority transfer fails:

- new scope expansion stops;
- independent review begins;
- affected BAZs receive restoration options;
- authority returns or devolves;
- centralized data is returned, deleted, or re-stewarded under the governing contract;
- continuing shared obligations are handled through the least intrusive arrangement.

## 16.6 Local Capture or Rights Failure

Subsidiarity does not protect local abuse. Where a BAZ violates rights, excludes populations, captures appeals, or creates serious external harm:

- affected people retain direct standing;
- independent review may bypass captured local bodies;
- temporary external protection may be authorized;
- the Economic Subsidiarity Test still requires minimum necessary authority;
- restoration and return remain mandatory once protection can safely localize.

## 16.7 Exit without Abandonment

Exit is not instantaneous abandonment of shared duties. A valid exit process balances:

- meaningful freedom to leave;
- fair settlement of liabilities;
- continuity of essential services;
- protection of vulnerable populations;
- environmental duties;
- shared-asset transition;
- prevention of strategic extraction followed by withdrawal.

# 18. Irreducible Design Tensions

## 18.1 Autonomy versus Interdependence

Local freedom can produce uncoordinated harm; shared coordination can erase local agency. The framework manages rather than eliminates this tension through the Subsidiarity Test, limited delegation, and authority return.

## 18.2 Interoperability versus Local Meaning

Common interfaces simplify exchange but compress context. Some loss is unavoidable. The framework requires explicit semantic-loss reporting, local fields, protected exceptions, and non-translation where necessary.

## 18.3 Solidarity versus Dependency

Transfers can protect capability or entrench dependence and allocator power. The Solidarity Protocol requires causal review, outcome monitoring, sunset, and instrument change.

## 18.4 Transparency versus Protected Unobservability

Cross-scale accountability needs evidence, but total legibility chills relational and cultural life. Transparency therefore focuses on institutions, authority, methods, and aggregate effects while preserving personal and sacred boundaries.

## 18.5 Standardization versus Innovation

Common standards enable coordination but may freeze one design. Forkability, local compatible variants, versioning, and NARP protect innovation.

## 18.6 Exit versus Shared Obligation

Meaningful exit prevents capture, but communities cannot evade obligations created through harm, shared assets, or protected rights. Exit is therefore possible but orderly.

## 18.7 Capacity versus Authority

Low-capacity communities need support, yet support can become control. The presumption is capacity-building before centralization and authority return after temporary delegation.

# Conclusion

The Nested Economies Framework does not seek one optimal scale for economic governance. Different problems have different spatial, temporal, cultural, ecological, and institutional boundaries.

A care network may be local. A watershed may require a compact. A currency may need common settlement rules. A planetary commons may require global protection. A sacred practice may remain outside all conversion and aggregation.

The engineering problem is therefore not to choose localism or centralization in the abstract. It is to design boundaries and interfaces that remain accountable under changing conditions.

v3.2 establishes the following operating logic:

1. begin with local authority;
2. identify the actual interdependence;
3. support local capability;
4. use voluntary coordination where sufficient;
5. transfer only the minimum necessary authority;
6. specify what crosses the boundary and what does not;
7. preserve local meaning, rights, and exit;
8. diagnose solidarity needs rather than applying a universal transfer;
9. observe outcomes without collapsing activity into flourishing;
10. return authority, data, and extraordinary obligations when their justification ends.

The framework's central principle is:

> **Coordinate at the scale of the interdependence; govern at the lowest capable scale; transfer only the authority and information necessary for the shared problem; and return both when the justification ends.**

Its interface principle is:

> **A community can connect without becoming identical, cooperate without becoming subordinate, and remain partially unknowable without becoming unaccountable.**

# Appendix F: Glossary

- **AUBI:** Adaptive Universal Basic Income.
- **BAZ:** Bioregional Autonomous Zone.
- **Coordination layer:** A function-specific, delegated, revocable arrangement among BAZs; not a governing tier by default.
- **Cross-Scale Interface Contract:** Protocol defining what crosses a jurisdictional or framework boundary, how it is transformed, and what remains protected.
- **Earth Council:** Treaty body protecting ecological integrity and Indigenous sovereignty.
- **Economic Subsidiarity Test:** Procedure governing any upward transfer of economic authority.
- **FLP:** Fractal Labor Parliament.
- **Hearts:** Formal regenerative currency governed by AUBI and Financial Systems.
- **ICTL:** Inter-Currency Translation Layer governed by Financial Systems.
- **Leaves:** Non-fungible ecological records and reward instruments.
- **LEKH:** Long-Embedded Knowledge Holder.
- **LMCI-A:** Contribution-level allocation system for AUBI Layer 2.
- **LMCI-M:** Independent population-level Love, Meaning, and Connection measurement.
- **Love Ledger:** Voluntary, non-monetized recognition system for informal relational contribution.
- **NARP:** Notice and Adaptive Response Protocol.
- **Protected non-convertibility:** Rule that some values, rights, knowledge, and relationships are not presumed translatable into currency or score.
- **Right Relationship:** Balanced, regenerative relations among people, communities, cultures, ecosystems, and generations.
- **Solidarity Protocol:** Governed process for diagnosing, authorizing, reviewing, and ending cross-BAZ support.
- **SRC:** Social Resilience Council.
- **SVDC:** Slow-Variable Decision Category.
