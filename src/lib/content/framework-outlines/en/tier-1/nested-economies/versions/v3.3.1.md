## Revision Note

v3.3 is a substantive successor to v3.2. It retains Right Relationship, BAZ authority, voluntary cooperation, shared standards, protected non-convertibility, Hearts interoperability, solidarity, Earth Council safeguards, slow-variable governance, exit, and graceful degradation.

The revision changes the theory of subsidiarity. Responsibility is no longer assigned mainly by administrative scale or by the location where consequences appear. Responsibility follows legal and practical control over causal variables, lawful jurisdiction, available capacity, and resources; affected-party standing determines participation, consent where applicable, challenge, and remedy. Where no participant originally controls the relational condition—such as a watershed, grid, labour market, settlement network, transport corridor, or shared infrastructure—the required authority may be jointly constituted rather than transferred upward.

v3.3 does not create a regional sovereign, automatic enforcement power, compulsory compact, or new authority in Meta-Governance. Exact consequence profiles, response states, clocks, handoff states, and closure semantics remain controlled by GMEAIA.

# Executive Summary

The **Nested Economies Framework** governs how relatively autonomous local economies relate across boundaries. It is the GGF architecture for deciding:

- which economic choices remain local;
- which interdependencies require cooperation;
- who controls the causes of a shared condition;
- when authority should be retained, delegated, transferred, concurrent, or jointly constituted;
- what information may cross a boundary;
- how holdout, bargaining asymmetry, refusal, and non-response are handled;
- whether exit is legally and materially usable;
- when delegated authority, shared infrastructure, data, and extraordinary obligations must return.

Its vision is an economy of **Right Relationship** in which communities can meet needs, sustain ecosystems, and cooperate globally without being forced into one institutional form, value language, data model, professional implementation system, or development path.

Its core architecture combines:

- **Bioregional Autonomous Zones (BAZs)** as primary sites of local economic authority;
- **Hearts**, **Leaves**, **AUBI**, and the **Love Ledger** under their controlling frameworks;
- **two constitutional governing tiers**—BAZ and global—without presuming a fixed regional government;
- **voluntary or jointly constituted coordination layers** for shared relational conditions;
- **Cross-Scale Interface Contracts** specifying operations, data, semantic loss, dependency, response, fallback, and exit;
- a **Causal, Economic, and Constitutional Subsidiarity Test**;
- **Solidarity Protocols** that distinguish capacity support, shared obligation, and restitution;
- multidimensional monitoring of autonomy, material access, interoperability, solidarity, resilience, rights, latency, bargaining power, dependency, and material exit;
- framework-level falsification and narrowing conditions.

Four constitutional principles govern the framework:

> **Coordinate at the scale of the interdependence; assign responsibility to the actors controlling its causes; and govern through the least authority capable of addressing the shared condition.**

> **Where the condition exists only through a relationship among autonomous parties, authority may need to be jointly constituted rather than transferred upward.**

> **Interoperability does not require universal convertibility, uniform measurement, total legibility, or one technical substrate.**

> **Delegated authority, compulsory information, material dependency, and extraordinary solidarity obligations must end, return, or be lawfully reconstituted when their justification ends.**

The framework does not define AUBI entitlement, Hearts issuance, Treasury reserves, demurrage, fraud sanctions, ICTL rates, or work-valuation standards. It defines the political, subsidiarity, interface, responsibility, bargaining, and exit conditions under which those systems connect across places.

# 1. Purpose, Boundaries, and Design Commitments

## 1.1 Purpose

The framework creates rules for resilient economic integration without compulsory homogenization. It addresses four linked governance failures:

1. local autonomy becoming isolation or authority hoarding;
2. interoperability becoming absorption or dependency;
3. responsibility being assigned to communities that experience consequences but do not control their causes;
4. coordination bodies and shared infrastructures becoming permanent because their continuation is easier to demonstrate than their necessity.

Nested Economies therefore governs:

- allocation and formation of decision authority across scales;
- causal responsibility for cross-boundary conditions;
- creation, review, and dissolution of BAZ-to-BAZ compacts;
- political conditions for cross-scale financial and technical interfaces;
- recognition of local credentials and practices;
- solidarity, restitution, and capability support;
- shared infrastructure and knowledge commons;
- cross-boundary externalities;
- local counter-model, refusal, response, and challenge rights;
- legal, technical, financial, service, political, and material exit;
- monitoring of whether integration preserves autonomy and improves actual conditions.

## 1.2 Boundaries

Nested Economies does **not** itself govern:

- AUBI Layer 1–3 eligibility or payout calculations;
- LMCI-M or LMCI-A methodology;
- Hearts issuance, reserve ratios, redemption, or demurrage;
- financial anomaly or fraud determinations;
- ICTL rate calculations or settlement controls;
- Green Job Score or care-work valuation standards;
- Community Provider professional standards;
- ecological verification methods;
- Treaty enforcement outside economic-interface questions;
- the exact GMEAIA response, handoff, activation, or closure state machine.

Those functions remain with their controlling frameworks and lawful authorities. Nested Economies specifies how their decisions and records cross jurisdictional boundaries and which local rights, meanings, duties, and responsibilities remain protected.

## 1.3 Right Relationship

Economic coordination is legitimate when it supports balanced, regenerative relationships among:

- individuals and communities;
- formal and informal economies;
- local autonomy and shared obligation;
- present needs and future generations;
- human and non-human communities;
- economic exchange and non-monetized life;
- technological interoperability and cultural sovereignty;
- responsibility and control over causes.

Right Relationship is not reduced to transaction volume, formal participation, standardized metrics, or conformance records. Communities may choose lower commercialization, retain local reciprocity, protect sacred practices from valuation, or preserve non-comparable categories while participating in shared systems.

## 1.4 Constitutional Design Commitments

All Nested Economies mechanisms must preserve:

1. **Causal subsidiarity:** responsibility follows legal and practical control over causal variables, lawful jurisdiction, capacity, and resources—not merely the location of consequences.
2. **Affected standing without burden transfer:** affected parties receive participation, consent where applicable, challenge, and remedy; standing does not make them responsible for causes they do not control.
3. **Capability support before centralization:** weak capacity triggers support, simplification, shared services, or lawful temporary assistance before transfer of authority.
4. **Joint constitution for relational conditions:** where a shared condition exists only through relations among parties, authority may be constituted through a compact rather than described as a local power moved upward.
5. **Purpose-limited authority:** coordination bodies receive only specified powers and may not expand their mandate by technical necessity, funding, platform control, or administrative custom.
6. **Duty attached to retained jurisdiction:** a competent actor retaining authority must disclose whether a response duty exists and, where it does, respond through the GMEAIA-controlled lifecycle.
7. **Return of authority:** delegated powers expire, return, narrow, or are lawfully reconstituted when their justification ends.
8. **Protected difference:** interoperability permits local exceptions, non-comparable elements, bounded attestations, and protected non-transfer.
9. **Material exit and fork:** communities can leave arrangements in law and practice without losing identity, essential entitlements, their own records, or access to continuity arrangements.
10. **Rights floors:** local autonomy cannot shield exploitation, discrimination, ecological destruction, Indigenous-rights violations, or externalized harm.
11. **Protected unobservability:** not all relational, cultural, spiritual, ecological, or personal knowledge must become transferable data.
12. **Contestability:** affected communities can challenge models, mappings, classifications, standards, authority formation, response delay, and exit conditions.
13. **Common-mode resilience:** nominal plurality does not count as independence where actors share the same funder, platform, AI, data, settlement rail, professional network, or failure mode.
14. **Proportional procedure:** documentation follows GMEAIA `CP0`–`CP3` and must not impose protected-domain burden on low-risk activity.
15. **Conformance is not success:** correct records and valid interfaces do not prove autonomy, flourishing, causal effectiveness, legitimacy, or material exit.
16. **Framework falsifiability:** the framework must state evidence that would justify narrowing, replacing, or not scaling its architecture.

# 2. Core Economic Architecture

## 2.1 Hearts

**Hearts** are the formal regenerative currency used for care, community services, and related economic exchange. Their issuance, reserve backing, redemption, circulation, demurrage, and financial controls are governed by **Financial Systems** and the relevant AUBI provisions.

Nested Economies governs:

- where Hearts are accepted;
- how BAZs recognize one another's providers and transactions;
- how local rules interact with common standards;
- how financial interfaces preserve local autonomy;
- how trade and solidarity compacts use Hearts;
- how BAZs retain fallback and exit capacity.

A BAZ may not redefine Hearts financial controls in ways that compromise system-wide settlement, participant rights, or shared obligations. Global financial authorities may not use common standards to determine unrelated local priorities or erase protected local distinctions.

## 2.2 Leaves

**Leaves** are non-fungible ecological records and reward instruments. They are not a second general-purpose currency and are not automatically interchangeable with Hearts.

Recognition or settlement of Leaves across BAZs requires:

- ecological verification under the relevant framework;
- FLP valuation standards where work valuation is involved;
- AUBI rules where Leaves affect Layer 2 allocation;
- Financial Systems rules where settlement or translation occurs;
- Slow-Variable Decision Protocol review where long-term ecological conditions are affected;
- Indigenous and community authority where land, sacred sites, or traditional knowledge are implicated.

A Cross-Scale Interface Contract must state whether a Leaf is being **recognized, verified, settled, converted, aggregated, or published**. Permission for one operation does not imply permission for the others.

## 2.3 Love Ledger

The **Love Ledger** recognizes informal care, gratitude, reciprocity, cultural contribution, and relational life through voluntary, non-monetized records and narratives.

Love Ledger participation:

- is never required for access to AUBI, Hearts, public services, trade, or BAZ membership;
- may remain local, private, oral, non-digital, or community-stewarded;
- does not mechanically feed LMCI-M, LMCI-A, ICTL rates, creditworthiness, fraud models, or individual entitlement;
- may supply voluntary context or hypotheses where participants consent;
- may not be used to rank human worth or create a portable social reputation score.

Institutional transparency applies to Love Ledger governance, access rules, data use, and aggregate system operation—not to making people's relational lives publicly visible.

## 2.4 AUBI

The four-layered AUBI architecture supplies the universal material-security, contribution-recognition, equity, and stabilization functions used within nested economies.

Nested Economies does not repeat AUBI rules. It ensures that:

- AUBI rights survive movement across BAZ boundaries;
- local interfaces do not silently change entitlement;
- BAZ compacts do not require surrender of Layer 1 protection;
- Layer 2 records remain separate from population flourishing measurement;
- Layer 3 needs and reparations are not treated as trade or contribution rewards;
- mobile, stateless, displaced, and nomadic people are not excluded by jurisdictional gaps.

## 2.5 Formal and Informal Economic Coexistence

Local economic health includes formal providers, household provision, mutual aid, commons activity, non-monetized care, subsistence, cultural exchange, and market activity.

The framework does not assume that more formal transactions are always better. A BAZ may legitimately maintain:

- strong non-monetized care;
- low commercial exchange;
- collective provisioning;
- local gift economies;
- seasonal exchange rhythms;
- protected sacred or cultural activities;
- non-transferable local credits alongside Hearts, where compatible with financial and rights rules.

Operational systems must not pressure communities to increase transaction volume merely to improve a dashboard.

# 3. Scale Architecture

## 3.1 Two Constitutional Governing Tiers

The framework retains two constitutional governing tiers.

### BAZ authority

A **BAZ Council** holds original authority over local economic governance within the limits of Treaty rights, shared commons duties, AUBI entitlements, Financial Systems standards, affected Indigenous authority, and duties concerning material externalities.

### Global GGF authority

Global bodies exercise authority only in constitutionally specified domains, including:

- system-wide financial stability;
- global commons obligations;
- Treaty rights;
- interoperability standards necessary for cross-BAZ settlement;
- systemic externalities;
- planetary crisis response;
- adjudication where valid jurisdiction exists.

No global body acquires general economic planning authority merely because it operates common infrastructure, holds more data, or coordinates more jurisdictions.

## 3.2 Local BAZ Economy

A BAZ may govern:

- local provider chartering;
- local participatory budgeting;
- community procurement;
- land trusts and commons institutions;
- service priorities;
- local accessibility and outreach;
- compact participation;
- local recognition practices;
- local data stewardship;
- implementation of AUBI and Hearts within governing framework constraints.

Local authority is not absolute. It remains subject to rights, protected AUBI obligations, ecological and intergenerational safeguards, valid compact duties, financial settlement rules, affected Indigenous authority, and remedy for externalized harm.

## 3.3 Internal Subsidiarity within a BAZ

A BAZ may not invoke local autonomy to centralize unnecessarily against:

- neighborhoods;
- municipalities;
- clans or constituent communities;
- worker and provider bodies;
- affected Indigenous peoples;
- local commons institutions;
- minorities and mobile populations.

The same causal-subsidiarity rule applies internally. A BAZ shall not assign responsibility downward while retaining control over budgets, appointments, infrastructure, targets, data, or permissions required to act.

## 3.4 Regional Economic Space

A **regional economic space** is a factual pattern of interdependence, such as a watershed, labour market, supply network, grid, transport corridor, cultural region, food system, or settlement network.

A regional economic space does not itself possess authority.

## 3.5 Voluntary Coordination Layer

Two or more BAZs may create a coordination layer through a compact for a defined purpose, including shared infrastructure, mutual procurement, trade facilitation, emergency mutual aid, technical services, ecological stewardship, dispute resolution, solidarity pooling, shared learning, or administration.

A coordination layer is:

- function-specific;
- created by explicit compact;
- governed by participating authorities;
- unable to expand its own mandate;
- transparent about authority formation;
- subject to periodic review;
- terminable under stated exit conditions;
- non-sovereign unless separately constituted.

## 3.6 Jointly Constituted Authority

Some shared economic conditions are not adequately described as local powers transferred upward. Where the governed object exists through the relationship among parties, the participating authorities may create **jointly constituted authority**.

Examples include:

- a shared grid;
- a watershed economy;
- a settlement network;
- a labour market spanning jurisdictions;
- common transport or logistics;
- jointly owned infrastructure.

The constituting record must identify:

- participating authorities;
- the shared governance object;
- affected non-parties;
- compact or legal source;
- powers granted and prohibited;
- representation and bargaining safeguards;
- response duties;
- exit and surviving duties;
- dissolution and asset disposition.

Joint constitution does not create residual regional sovereignty. Silence remains in favor of retained authority.

## 3.7 When a Coordination Layer Becomes a Governing Tier

A coordination layer exercises governmental authority if it can independently:

- levy compulsory taxes or contributions;
- bind a non-consenting BAZ;
- impose sanctions;
- exclude a BAZ from essential infrastructure without due process;
- alter rights or AUBI access;
- prevent exit;
- permanently control shared assets;
- create new powers without renewed authorization;
- override local law outside the constituted function.

Such powers require explicit constitutional authorization, representation, appeal, audit, proportional process, sunset, and authority-return rules. They may not arise through technical standards, grant conditions, platform control, interoperability dependency, or administrative custom.

## 3.8 Global Standard versus Local Implementation

A global standard is legitimate when it defines the minimum compatibility, rights, security, or commons conditions necessary for shared coordination.

It may specify settlement fields, minimum rights and appeal, auditability of public authority, security requirements, reserve or redemption rules under Financial Systems, ecological safeguards, or shared-commons limits.

It should not prescribe local cultural meaning, unnecessary personal data, one institutional form where several are compatible, unrelated service priorities, compulsory monetization, or uniform comparison of sacred or non-comparable knowledge.

A compatibility finding states whether a system can safely interoperate. It does not determine political legitimacy, maturity, funding worthiness, or authority.

# 4. Causal, Economic, and Constitutional Subsidiarity Test

## 4.1 Presumption

> **Authority remains at the least intrusive lawful scale or jointly constituted arrangement capable of controlling the relevant causes without imposing ungoverned material harm on other communities, shared commons, or protected rights.**

The party proposing transfer, compulsory coordination, or exclusion from a shared system bears the burden of justification.

## 4.2 Trigger

The Test is required before:

- transferring a local power upward;
- creating a compulsory or jointly constituted regional function;
- imposing a global standard that materially changes local operations;
- conditioning access to essential common infrastructure;
- centralizing data, validation, settlement, or custody;
- mandating a solidarity contribution;
- overriding a local exception;
- extending emergency coordination authority;
- preventing or materially burdening exit;
- assigning responsibility to a BAZ for a condition substantially controlled elsewhere.

The proposer selects and justifies the applicable GMEAIA consequence profile `CP0`–`CP3` without redefining the profile locally.

## 4.3 Test Sequence

### Step 1: Define the condition

State the actual condition requiring response. Do not define the problem as non-compliance with the proposed solution, insufficient integration, low transaction volume, or refusal to adopt a preferred tool.

### Step 2: Map affected parties and boundaries

Identify affected BAZs, sub-BAZ communities, non-members, mobile and unrepresented populations, affected Indigenous peoples, ecological systems, future generations, shared infrastructure, workers, users, and rights holders.

### Step 3: Map causal control and responsibility

Identify:

- principal causal variables;
- legal controller of each variable;
- practical controller of each variable;
- actors benefiting from the present condition;
- actors bearing its costs;
- authority, resources, and information needed to change it;
- uncertainty and alternative causal explanations.

Affected-party standing creates rights of participation, consent where applicable, challenge, and remedy. It does not assign responsibility to the affected party.

### Step 4: Test capability and resource alignment

Assess whether the present authority can address the condition with:

- existing powers;
- additional resources;
- shared technical support;
- temporary personnel;
- mutual aid;
- administrative simplification;
- local institutional redesign;
- lawful delegation.

Where the actor has jurisdiction but lacks capacity, the GMEAIA state is `capacity_limited`, not `constitutional_gap`.

### Step 5: Test voluntary coordination

Before compulsory transfer, consider bilateral agreement, multilateral compact, shared service, mutual recognition, procurement, federated administration, reciprocal support, bounded arbitration, or common infrastructure with material exit.

### Step 6: Determine authority formation

Classify the authority through GMEAIA as:

- `retained`;
- `delegated`;
- `transferred`;
- `concurrent`;
- `jointly_constituted`;
- `absent`.

A relational condition should not be disguised as an upward transfer where the authority must instead be created by the parties governing the relationship.

### Step 7: Identify the minimum necessary authority

Separate information access, convening, standard setting, funding, administration, adjudication, infrastructure operation, and enforcement. Need for one does not establish need for all.

### Step 8: Assess distribution, bargaining power, and burden transfer

Examine whether the arrangement:

- burdens lower-capacity BAZs;
- excludes weakly represented populations;
- erases Indigenous or local categories;
- creates financial or technical dependency;
- concentrates gateway or infrastructure control;
- rewards holdout or free riding;
- makes exit prohibitively costly;
- assigns documentation mainly to those suffering the harm;
- shifts risk without representation, compensation, or control.

### Step 9: Establish response, refusal, and escalation

For consequential requests or handoffs, record through GMEAIA:

- response-duty source and effect;
- acknowledgment and decision clocks;
- accepted, partially accepted, capacity-limited, refused, deferred, referred, timed-out, misrouted, or constitutional-gap status;
- interim protection;
- post-acceptance action clocks;
- continuing sender and recipient duties.

Timeout does not transfer jurisdiction automatically.

### Step 10: Establish review, sunset, return, and material exit

State:

- start and effective dates;
- review cadence;
- success and failure conditions;
- independent outcomes;
- authority-return or lawful reconstitution condition;
- data return or deletion;
- service and worker continuity;
- exit pathway and dependency reduction;
- restoration where the arrangement caused harm.

## 4.4 Subsidiarity and Causal Responsibility Record

Each consequential decision publishes or references:

- condition and decision class;
- selected consequence profile;
- affected parties;
- causal variables and legal/practical controllers;
- current and proposed authority formation;
- capability and resource gaps;
- voluntary and jointly constituted alternatives;
- minimum power required;
- retained local and sub-local powers;
- bargaining and dependency effects;
- response-duty and clock sources;
- uncertainty and dissent;
- duration, review, exit, closure, and unresolved duties.

The operational authority and causal-responsibility profile is governed by GMEAIA-20.

Where evidence does not establish that transfer is necessary, the finding is:

> **Centralization not justified—support, voluntary coordination, joint constitution, or a narrower remedy is required.**

## 4.5 Impossible Mandate and Constitutional Gap

A BAZ, compact, or coordination body may issue an **Impossible Mandate Notice** where it is assigned an outcome but lacks the lawful authority, practical control, resources, or cooperation necessary to produce it.

The notice identifies:

- required outcome;
- missing authority or controlled variable;
- actor retaining control;
- support requested;
- expected harm;
- response status;
- what should not be attributed to the burdened actor.

A `constitutional_gap` exists only where no identified authority possesses sufficient lawful jurisdiction. It is diagnostic and must route toward lawful constitution; it does not create provisional sovereignty in Meta-Governance, a compact secretariat, or a technical operator.

## 4.6 Authority Return

Delegated or transferred authority returns or narrows when the trigger ends, capability is restored, a less intrusive mechanism becomes available, the arrangement exceeds mandate, independent outcomes fail, the compact expires, participants validly withdraw, or the transfer creates greater harm.

Jointly constituted authority may instead be renewed, narrowed, reconstituted, or dissolved according to its compact. Improved administrative speed, conformance, or transaction growth alone does not justify continuation.

## 5.2 Decision Separation

The following functions should remain institutionally distinguishable where consequence is material:

- proposing a change;
- supplying observations;
- estimating the condition;
- authorizing action;
- administering action;
- auditing outcomes;
- adjudicating disputes.

One small institution may perform several functions during early piloting only where conflicts are declared, secondary review exists, and no fundamental right depends on a single unreviewed judgment.

## 5.3 Standing

Standing to submit evidence or challenge a cross-scale decision extends to:

- member BAZs;
- materially affected non-member BAZs;
- transition-stage BAZs;
- Indigenous nations and knowledge authorities;
- mobile, nomadic, displaced, and stateless populations;
- affected providers and participants;
- shared-commons guardians;
- relevant Offices of the Adversary and independent auditors.

Voting rights may differ by compact, but lack of formal membership does not erase affected-party standing.


## 5.4 Duty Attached to Retained Jurisdiction

A BAZ, compact, fund, infrastructure body, or global authority that retains jurisdiction over a consequential request shall disclose whether a response duty exists and its legal, contractual, charter-internal, voluntary, advisory, or absent effect.

Where a duty exists, the response follows GMEAIA-08 or GMEAIA-59. Refusal may be lawful. Silence, delay, capacity limitation, and constitutional absence remain distinguishable.

## 5.5 Affected Non-Members and Remedy

Affected non-members may have standing without membership or voting rights. A compact must provide a proportionate route for:

- notice;
- evidence;
- independent advice;
- challenge;
- interim protection;
- correction;
- restitution or compensation;
- review of continuing external effects.

Standing without an effective remedy is not sufficient protection against externalization.

# 6. Cross-Scale Interface Contracts

## 6.1 Purpose

A **Cross-Scale Interface Contract** defines how institutions coordinate without assuming that all value, data, identities, practices, or responsibilities are directly comparable.

It applies whenever a material object, claim, duty, service, or decision crosses a BAZ or framework boundary, including financial settlement, provider recognition, credentials, ecological records, solidarity claims, shared infrastructure access, governance decisions, and public reporting.

## 6.2 Interface Operations

Each interface identifies which operations it performs:

1. recognition;
2. authentication;
3. verification;
4. settlement;
5. conversion;
6. portability;
7. aggregation;
8. publication;
9. handoff or referral;
10. continuity or fallback.

Authorization for one operation does not authorize the others.

## 6.3 Required Contract Fields

Every material interface records:

1. participating jurisdictions and institutions;
2. purpose and consequence profile;
3. authority formation and source;
4. object, claim, or duty crossing the boundary;
5. permitted and prohibited operations;
6. causal responsibility and continuing duties;
7. required, prohibited, and protected data;
8. local semantic fields preserved;
9. mapping, settlement, or transformation method;
10. uncertainty, information loss, and disputed interpretations;
11. provenance, owner, steward, custody, and access;
12. retention, return, deletion, and correction;
13. accessibility and offline fallback;
14. service levels, response duties, clocks, and handoff status;
15. failure, rollback, safe state, and continuity;
16. essential-infrastructure and non-discrimination rules;
17. dependency, concentration, switching cost, and exit;
18. protocol version, compatibility, and change governance;
19. administrative and documentation burden;
20. audit, independent outcomes, and falsification conditions;
21. dispute, remedy, and affected non-member standing;
22. closure and unresolved-duty custody.

## 6.4 Semantic Preservation

An interface discloses what local meaning is lost during translation. Where no faithful equivalent exists, it may use a locally preserved field, bounded attestation, non-comparable category, narrative appendix, protected exception, or no translation.

The absence of a standardized field does not imply absence of value, legitimacy, or capability.

## 6.5 Data Minimization and Protected Non-Transfer

Interfaces transfer only information required for their declared operation. Cross-BAZ settlement does not ordinarily require a person's complete care history, Love Ledger narratives, health or disability details, cultural identity records, generalized reputation, unrelated location history, or sacred knowledge.

A refusal to disclose protected material is not evidence that the underlying right, claim, or community is invalid.

## 6.6 Common-Mode Dependency and Source Independence

Interfaces disclose whether nominally distinct channels share observers, platforms, algorithms, AI providers, funding bodies, governance authorities, source records, settlement rails, vendors, or professional networks.

Shared sources must not be represented as independent corroboration. High-consequence interfaces require operational fallback or explicit acceptance of the correlation risk.

## 6.7 Essential Infrastructure and Holdout

A body controlling essential shared infrastructure shall not use access, compatibility, settlement, identity, or data custody to obtain unrelated political concessions.

Contracts specify:

- continuity minima;
- non-discrimination;
- emergency access where lawfully required;
- proportionate suspension;
- independent review;
- migration and fork support;
- response to strategic holdout or free riding.

## 6.8 Interface Change Governance

Changes to mappings, required fields, conversion methods, eligibility rules, aggregation, retention, publication, reliability flags, AI roles, service minima, or human review are governance changes.

Material financial changes follow Financial Systems NARP. Material compact changes require affected-party amendment. Compatibility changes follow GMEAIA-53 and must distinguish technical incompatibility from political legitimacy.

# 7. Information Classes and Protected Non-Convertibility

## 7.1 Class I — Required Institutional Information

Information may be required across boundaries where necessary for public accountability or shared obligations, including:

- aggregate reserve compliance;
- settlement status;
- institutional audit records;
- compact finances;
- shared-infrastructure performance;
- rights and appeal performance;
- shared-commons impacts.

Institutional transparency does not authorize unnecessary individual exposure.

## 7.2 Class II — Aggregate Operational Telemetry

Operational telemetry may include:

- trade flows;
- settlement success;
- provider capacity;
- waiting times;
- infrastructure load;
- failed transactions;
- access gaps;
- administrative backlog;
- compact participation.

Operational telemetry may trigger review. It is not LMCI-M and does not prove flourishing, sovereignty, or equity merely because activity rises.

## 7.3 Class III — Voluntary Narrative and Contextual Evidence

This class includes:

- Love Ledger narratives;
- voluntary community accounts;
- qualitative experience;
- contextual interpretations;
- locally authorized cultural reports.

These records may contextualize decisions or generate hypotheses. They are not automatically monetized, scored, aggregated, or published.

## 7.4 Class IV — Protected Local, Sacred, Personal, or Ecological Knowledge

Protected information may remain:

- local;
- non-digital;
- non-comparable;
- held by a knowledge authority;
- available only as a bounded conclusion;
- represented through a veto, condition, or attestation;
- unavailable to general audit.

The system must support **protected non-transfer** as an interface outcome.

## 7.5 Protected Non-Convertibility

The following are not presumed convertible into Hearts, Leaves, market price, LMCI, credit, or portable reputation:

- human dignity;
- sacred knowledge;
- spiritual sincerity;
- informal affection or gratitude;
- cultural identity;
- community membership;
- political loyalty;
- private relationship quality;
- Treaty rights;
- AUBI Layer 1 entitlement;
- Indigenous sovereignty;
- ecological existence value.

A specific contribution or ecological outcome may be recognized under an authorized protocol without converting the underlying person, relationship, culture, or living system into a financial unit.

# 8. Value Recognition, Validation, and Settlement

## 8.1 Separation of Functions

Nested Economies distinguishes:

- **local recognition** of a practice or contribution;
- **validation** under AUBI, Work in Liberation, FLP, or ecological standards;
- **financial settlement** under Financial Systems;
- **cross-BAZ recognition** through a compact;
- **population-level evaluation** through independent LMCI-M and other outcome systems.

No one framework or data stream owns all five functions.

## 8.2 Care Contributions

Care contributions are validated under AUBI LMCI-A and relevant Work in Liberation protocols. Cross-BAZ recognition may accept a valid contribution credential without importing all underlying personal evidence.

An interface must preserve distinctions among:

- occurrence;
- recipient acceptance where appropriate;
- duration or scope;
- role;
- additionality;
- safeguarding;
- outcome evidence;
- uncertainty;
- participant preference for non-monetized recognition.

Nested Economies does not define a universal “Proof of Care score × multiplier” conversion.

## 8.3 Ecological Contributions

Ecological work and Leaves are governed by ecological verification, AUBI, FLP standards, Financial Systems settlement, and slow-variable safeguards.

Cross-BAZ recognition must disclose:

- baseline;
- additionality;
- permanence;
- leakage;
- uncertainty;
- land and knowledge authority;
- co-benefits and co-harms;
- reversal conditions.

A compact may recognize an ecological record without making it freely convertible.

## 8.4 Time Banks and Local Credits

A time bank or local credit may interoperate through:

- mutual recognition;
- reciprocal access;
- compact-specific settlement;
- bounded conversion;
- non-convertible credential recognition.

There is no universal formula based on hours, regional wages, and a global Hearts index.

Any conversion requires:

- participant consent;
- a stated purpose;
- local authorization;
- distributional review;
- transparent method;
- exit and fallback;
- no compulsory monetization of previously non-monetized practices.

## 8.5 Fiat and Heartstarter

Fiat contributions and Heartstarter capitalization follow Financial Systems §5. Nested Economies may coordinate applications, local matching, and cross-BAZ learning but does not redefine reserve, ceiling, or release rules.

## 8.6 Carbon and Other Ecological Credits

Carbon or biodiversity credits are not translated through a simple tonnage multiplier. Any interface defers to the relevant ecological framework and must address rights, additionality, permanence, leakage, uncertainty, and affected communities.

## 8.7 Interface Authority Table

| Object | Local recognition | Validation authority | Settlement authority | Cross-scale governance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Care contribution | BAZ/provider/participant | AUBI LMCI-A and Work in Liberation | Financial Systems where paid in Hearts | Compact may recognize credential; protected evidence remains local |
| Ecological contribution | BAZ/ecological authority | Ecological framework, FLP, AUBI | Financial Systems for Leaves/Hearts settlement | SVDC and Indigenous safeguards |
| Time-bank hour | Local time bank | Local rules | Compact-specific | No automatic global conversion |
| Fiat contribution | Local Treasury | Financial Systems/Heartstarter | Hearts Treasury | Reserve and capitalization rules govern |
| Love Ledger recognition | Participant/community | Voluntary local protocol | None | Narrative may remain local and non-transferable |
| Provider charter | BAZ Council | Work in Liberation/FLP standards | N/A | Mutual recognition through compact |
| AUBI entitlement | AUBI authority | AUBI | AUBI/Financial Systems delivery | Portable rights; local interface may not reduce entitlement |

# 9. Voluntary BAZ Compacts and Coordination Layers

## 9.1 Compact Purposes

BAZs may form compacts for trade, shared infrastructure, energy, food systems, transport, mutual aid, ecological stewardship, technical services, provider recognition, emergency response, solidarity, research, and learning.

A compact must not become a general authority merely because several functions are convenient to combine.

## 9.2 Compact Requirements

Every compact specifies:

1. purpose, scope, and shared governance object;
2. members and affected non-members;
3. authority formation and constituting source;
4. delegated or jointly constituted powers;
5. prohibited and retained powers;
6. decision rule and representation;
7. bargaining-power and small-BAZ safeguards;
8. funding, contribution, and administrative burden;
9. institutional host and conflicts;
10. data and interface contracts;
11. response duties, clocks, refusal, and escalation;
12. audit, common-mode dependency, and objective-reproduction review;
13. dispute, remedy, and affected non-member standing;
14. emergency authority;
15. amendment and compatibility change;
16. legal and material exit;
17. asset, liability, service, worker, and data allocation on exit;
18. sunset, renewal, dissolution, and authority return;
19. framework and compact falsification conditions.

## 9.3 Delegation and Joint Constitution

Delegated functions may include administering a shared service, infrastructure, procurement, technical nodes, bounded funds, or dispute support.

Jointly constituted functions govern a relational object that no single BAZ possesses alone.

Neither form transfers residual sovereignty. Silence is interpreted in favor of retained authority.

## 9.4 Representation and Bargaining Power

Compact governance should reflect participating BAZs, population where appropriate, affected Indigenous peoples, small-BAZ protection, mobile populations, users, workers, and ecological guardians.

A **Dependency, Holdout, and Bargaining Power Assessment** examines:

- control of essential gateways and assets;
- alternatives and switching costs;
- dependence on one buyer, supplier, funder, or settlement rail;
- ability to delay without bearing the resulting harm;
- risks of free riding or strategic exit;
- agenda, evidence, and legal-resource asymmetry;
- participation support and interim protection.

No universal representation formula is required, but asymmetry must be visible and addressed.

## 9.5 Response to Holdout and Non-Response

Voluntary coordination does not require mandatory agreement. It does require clear handling of consequential requests.

Where a response duty exists, the recipient must use the GMEAIA response lifecycle. A compact may require good-faith, time-bounded consideration without requiring acceptance.

Timeout does not establish substantive fault or transfer jurisdiction. It may trigger mediation, interim protection, adjudication where jurisdiction exists, or a constitutional-gap notice.

## 9.6 Material Exit

A BAZ may exit according to the compact without losing AUBI rights, institutional identity, its own data, undisputed balances, or essential continuity.

Exit is assessed across six dimensions:

1. **legal exit** — the right to withdraw;
2. **technical exit** — data portability, forkability, credentials, and migration;
3. **financial exit** — settlement of liabilities without punitive lock-in;
4. **service exit** — continuity of essential functions;
5. **political exit** — absence of retaliation or unrelated rights loss;
6. **material exit** — practical capacity to operate or join an alternative.

An exit right that only wealthy or highly capable BAZs can exercise is not generally meaningful.

## 9.7 Strategic Exit and Surviving Duties

Exit does not erase liabilities, reparative obligations, ecological duties, valid contracts, or duties created by prior harm. Restrictions must target surviving obligations rather than preserving membership for its own sake.

## 9.8 Forking and Alternative Standards

A BAZ or group may fork a coordination service or adopt an alternative compatible implementation where settlement and rights duties remain met, shared assets are handled fairly, data portability exists, affected parties receive notice, and the fork does not evade ecological or human-rights duties.

Compatibility determines whether shared operations remain safe; it does not determine legitimacy or worth.

# 10. Solidarity and Resource Justice

## 10.1 Purpose

Solidarity addresses unequal capacity, shocks, inherited injustice, ecological burdens, and shared obligations without turning every difference into a demand for uniformity or permanent allocator power.

It may take the form of grants, shared services, liquidity, technical assistance, infrastructure, debt relief, reparative transfer, provider support, mutual aid, equalization, or recognition of ecological stewardship burden.

## 10.2 Trigger Classes

A Solidarity Protocol may be activated for:

1. acute shock;
2. structural capability gap;
3. shared infrastructure;
4. ecological stewardship burden;
5. reparative obligation;
6. transition cost;
7. cross-boundary externality;
8. essential-service equalization.

## 10.3 Solidarity State and Causal Responsibility Estimate

Before a non-emergency transfer, the responsible body publishes:

- trigger class;
- affected populations and BAZs;
- current condition;
- causal variables and controllers;
- plausible alternative explanations;
- evidence, uncertainty, and source dependency;
- local and affected-party accounts;
- role of trade, interface, funding, and infrastructure rules;
- distributional pattern and informal provision;
- alternative interventions;
- whether restitution, rule change, capability support, or transfer is appropriate;
- expected independent outcomes;
- duration, conversion, and exit conditions.

Where the need is established but the instrument is unresolved, the official state remains unresolved rather than forcing one transfer model.

## 10.4 Contribution Bases

A solidarity arrangement may use negotiated contribution, compact fees, settlement-fee shares, GCF allocation, commons dividends, fiscal capacity, responsibility for harm, voluntary mutual aid, or resource-tax allocation.

The previous 10% rule remains illustrative only. No universal levy applies.

## 10.5 Recipient Logic

Recipient assessment may consider essential-service gaps, shock severity, capacity, infrastructure, stewardship, reparative claim, population, dependency, local contribution capacity, administrative burden, and missing populations.

Low formal revenue alone does not establish need.

## 10.6 Safeguards

Every compulsory or shared-fund arrangement includes:

- affected-party participation;
- public method and causal assumptions;
- contributor hardship protection;
- no unnecessary disclosure;
- distributional and bargaining-power review;
- appeal and correction;
- administrative support;
- anti-capture and common-mode review;
- sunset and reassessment;
- capability and dependency trajectory;
- response-duty and omission handling;
- material exit or conversion path.

## 10.7 Restitution versus Solidarity

Where one actor caused identifiable harm, the response may be restitution or reparation rather than generalized solidarity. A pool must not socialize responsibility for extraction, pollution, dispossession, or exploitative trade.

## 10.8 Capability without Dependency

A transfer intended to build capability must identify:

- the capacity to be created;
- the local owner;
- maintenance funding;
- technology and staffing transfer;
- dependency-reduction milestones;
- conditions for support withdrawal;
- safeguards against abrupt abandonment.

## 10.9 End and Conversion Conditions

A transfer changes, ends, or converts when the shock ends, capability closes, diagnosis is falsified, dependency becomes harmful, contributors enter hardship, infrastructure replaces recurring support, a rule change is preferable, or affected BAZs adopt a new compact.

Transfer volume, record completeness, or continued demand for support is not evidence of success.

# 11. Shared Infrastructure, Knowledge, and Technology Commons

## 11.1 Shared Infrastructure

BAZs may jointly govern:

- payment and settlement nodes;
- transport;
- energy;
- communications;
- data portability services;
- emergency logistics;
- training institutions;
- shared procurement;
- repositories and technical platforms.

Shared infrastructure governance must disclose:

- ownership;
- control;
- access conditions;
- fees;
- maintenance duties;
- security;
- data flows;
- fallback;
- exit and asset division;
- monopoly risk.

## 11.2 Knowledge Commons

Open-source software, templates, training material, and implementation knowledge should be shared through accessible repositories and local support networks.

A knowledge commons may include:

- Hearts interface software;
- compact templates;
- accessible wallet tools;
- offline ledgers;
- cooperative templates;
- participatory-budgeting tools;
- training curricula;
- audit methods;
- failure reports.

Open-source publication does not authorize publication of Indigenous, personal, sacred, or community-restricted knowledge.

## 11.3 Technology Autonomy

A BAZ should be able to:

- export its records in documented formats;
- operate essential functions offline;
- migrate between vendors or platforms;
- inspect public algorithms;
- maintain a local node where feasible;
- use accessible non-smartphone channels;
- retain local language and semantic fields;
- continue basic operations during network failure.

## 11.4 Public-Private Partnerships

Private partners may support infrastructure but may not acquire control over:

- AUBI entitlement;
- Hearts issuance authority;
- BAZ identity;
- Love Ledger narratives;
- sovereign community data;
- compulsory standards;
- exit rights.

Contracts must include:

- public-interest purpose;
- data-use restrictions;
- open standards;
- audit access;
- continuity obligations;
- termination;
- transfer of essential operations;
- no lock-in.


## 11.5 Infrastructure Dependency and Continuity Review

Shared infrastructure shall be reviewed for:

- operator concentration;
- common-mode failure;
- funding and vendor dependency;
- legal and technical portability;
- continuity during dispute or exit;
- ability of smaller BAZs to maintain alternatives;
- whether control of the infrastructure has become practical governance authority.

Where a common platform becomes unavoidable for essential participation, its constitutional obligations increase even if its charter remains formally technical.

# 12. Governance, NARP, and Slow Variables

## 12.1 Social Resilience Council

The **Social Resilience Council (SRC)**:

- maintains global economic coordination standards within its mandate;
- manages relevant GCF interfaces;
- coordinates with Financial Systems and AUBI;
- supports BAZ and compact capacity;
- receives cross-scale evidence;
- cannot use common infrastructure to acquire residual local authority;
- remains subject to AUBI governance, Accountable Coordination Audit, BAZ reformation triggers, Office of the Adversary review, Earth Council safeguards, and Meta-Governance oversight.

Financial supply, reserve, Treasury, ICTL, and NARP authorities are governed by Financial Systems.

## 12.2 Fractal Labor Parliament

The **Fractal Labor Parliament (FLP)** defines work-valuation and occupational standards under its governing frameworks. Nested Economies may facilitate cross-BAZ recognition but does not create independent wage, care, or Green Job Score formulas.

## 12.3 Earth Council

The **Earth Council** may intervene where cross-scale economic arrangements threaten:

- Indigenous sovereignty;
- ecological integrity;
- sacred sites;
- Treaty rights;
- long-term commons conditions.

Earth Council authority does not convert protected knowledge into a general data obligation.

## 12.4 NARP

Material changes to ICTL, financial interface standards, settlement, or financial observation methods follow **Financial Systems §12, Notice and Adaptive Response Protocol**.

Nested Economies extends the same principles to non-financial cross-scale changes:

- publication;
- plain-language explanation;
- affected-BAZ analysis;
- alternative designs;
- local counter-models;
- uncertainty;
- objection rights;
- amendment;
- sunset and reversal.

## 12.5 Slow-Variable Decision Protocol

Changes qualify for AUBI's **Slow-Variable Decision Category (SVDC)** where they materially affect long-horizon ecological conditions observed primarily through multigenerational or place-embedded knowledge.

Examples include:

- ecological exchange standards;
- carbon or biodiversity mappings;
- shared watershed extraction rules;
- long-term infrastructure affecting habitat;
- Leaves multipliers tied to slow ecological outcomes;
- authority transfers affecting Indigenous stewardship.

Such decisions require the applicable LEKH quorum and community authority.

## 12.6 Cross-Level Trust Protocol

The inherited **Cross-Level Trust Protocol** is operationalized through:

- Causal, Economic, and Constitutional Subsidiarity Test;
- Cross-Scale Interface Contracts;
- decision-rights matrix;
- institutional and compact audits;
- Observation Independence and Common-Mode Maps;
- participant and BAZ correction rights;
- outcome review;
- authority-return tests.

A biannual review is the default pilot cadence, with higher frequency for high-risk or crisis arrangements.


## 12.7 Response, Refusal, and Omission

Consequential cross-scale referrals use GMEAIA-08 or GMEAIA-59. Nested Economies does not create alternative response states.

The record distinguishes response omission from performance omission. Acceptance does not close the matter where the recipient then misses an action, remedy, review, or continuity clock.

## 12.8 Objective-Reproduction Review

Long-lived compacts, coordination bodies, funds, and infrastructure operators shall periodically examine the pressures shaping their behavior, including:

- funding and contribution incentives;
- appointment and staffing;
- prestige and professional continuation;
- benefits from mandate expansion;
- costs of devolution, fork, or closure;
- incentives to preserve dependency;
- whether affected BAZs bear more documentation than the coordinating body;
- what would make the body unnecessary.

The review follows the applicable GMEAIA-45 and GMEAIA-51/53 profiles. It does not infer bad faith from institutional continuation.

# 13. Monitoring and the Nested Economy Condition Dashboard

## 13.1 No Composite Sovereignty, Integration, or Success Score

Sovereignty, equity, interoperability, resilience, and solidarity remain multidimensional. No composite score may automatically determine authority, funding, compact membership, legitimacy, sanctions, or exit.

Conformance to CIS, GMEAIA, contracts, or interfaces demonstrates traceability and compatibility—not substantive success.

## 13.2 Information-System Separation

| Information product | Purpose | Prohibited interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **LMCI-M** | Independent population-level flourishing estimate | Not derived from transaction volume, logged care, or interoperability |
| **LMCI-A** | Contribution-level allocation | Not a community wellbeing or sovereignty score |
| **Operational telemetry** | Trade, settlement, providers, infrastructure, access, administration, latency, and failure | More activity is not proof of flourishing |
| **Love Ledger Narrative Feed** | Voluntary relational recognition and context | Not mechanically monetized, scored, or aggregated |

## 13.3 Local and Internal Autonomy Dimension

Monitor:

- decisions retained at BAZ and sub-BAZ levels;
- delegated and jointly constituted functions;
- concentration of external and internal authority;
- local exception preservation;
- data portability;
- authority-return performance;
- practical ability of affected subunits to challenge the BAZ.

## 13.4 Material Access Dimension

Monitor access to essentials, unmet need, affordability, geographic and disability access, digital and offline access, external-supply vulnerability, and missing populations.

## 13.5 Interoperability and Latency Dimension

Monitor:

- settlement and service success;
- interface uptime;
- sensing, response, decision, action, and correction latency;
- dispute and correction rates;
- portability and fallback;
- semantic loss;
- locally preserved fields;
- gateway concentration;
- unaccepted and timed-out handoffs.

High compatibility does not establish legitimate interoperability if exit, semantic integrity, privacy, timely response, or local control are absent.

## 13.6 Solidarity and Dependency Dimension

Monitor diagnosed need, adequacy, distribution, duration, administrative burden, capability development, dependency trajectory, contributor hardship, outcomes, unresolved causal disagreement, and transition after support withdrawal.

## 13.7 Bargaining and Holdout Dimension

Monitor:

- concentration of essential assets;
- switching costs;
- free riding and strategic exit;
- ability to delay without bearing harm;
- legal and evidentiary asymmetry;
- affected non-member remedy;
- participation support;
- non-discrimination in essential infrastructure.

## 13.8 Material Exit Dimension

Monitor legal, technical, financial, service, political, and material exit separately. Record failed exits, prohibitive costs, loss of essential continuity, and whether only high-capacity BAZs can exercise the right.

## 13.9 Local Economic Resilience Dimension

Monitor provider and supply-chain diversity, reserve status, shock recovery, administrative capacity, mutual aid, informal-care burden, external buyer/supplier concentration, technical continuity, and locally maintainable alternatives.

## 13.10 Rights and Legitimacy Dimension

Monitor participation, consent, correction, appeal, Indigenous authority, privacy, exit, retaliation, trust, representation, capture warnings, and unresolved remedies.

## 13.11 Common-Mode Dependency Dimension

Monitor shared funders, settlement rails, AI systems, vendors, platforms, data sources, auditors, professional networks, and methods. Several BAZs connected to one substrate do not constitute independent resilience.

## 13.12 LMCI-M Context

LMCI-M remains separate. Its movement does not by itself prove that a particular interface, compact, or solidarity instrument caused the change.

## 13.13 Uncertainty and Partial Identification

Dashboard publication includes data age, missingness, subgroup coverage, source dependencies, uncertainty, contested interpretations, method versions, and known blind spots.

Where multiple conditions fit the observations, the dashboard records unresolved causation rather than forcing one diagnosis.

## 13.14 Sectoral Concentration Dimension

Monitor sector shares of employment and income with rate of change, wage and land-cost differentials between sectors, revenue concentration on single funders or buyers, non-formation or contraction of other sectors, displacement of non-monetized provision, and the practical availability of a decision to slow or decline further scaling. Reported as a set. No composite index.

# 14. Missing Populations, Independent Observation, and Community Weavers

## 14.1 Missing-Population Sentinel

Each BAZ and major compact maintains privacy-preserving channels to estimate people who are unenrolled, isolated, digitally or linguistically excluded, disabled, stateless, displaced, mobile, nomadic, distrustful, dependent on informal provision, or excluded by local power.

Absence from the ledger is not evidence of absence of need, standing, or harm.

## 14.2 Community Weaver Role

Community Weavers may train participants, support accessible and offline participation, help articulate needs, relay interpretations, submit evidence, identify interface friction, and facilitate dialogue.

## 14.3 Prohibited Sole-Source Roles

A Community Weaver may not alone determine fraud, adjudicate appeal, certify LMCI-M, validate their own programme, represent an Indigenous people without mandate, determine compact legitimacy, control primary data and audit, or judge cultural sincerity.

## 14.4 Observation Independence and Common-Mode Map

Each BAZ and long-lived coordination arrangement identifies shared:

- observers and assessors;
- data sources and classifications;
- platforms, settlement rails, and AI providers;
- algorithms and models;
- funders and appointment authorities;
- vendors and professional networks;
- governance bodies and incentives;
- fallback dependencies.

Minimum safeguards include secondary review, independent sampling, participant correction, confidential complaint, anti-retaliation, conflict declarations, rotating audit, no single-source adverse decision, and operational fallback where feasible.

## 14.5 Compensation and Incentive Disclosure

Where Weavers, validators, providers, auditors, or coordination bodies are compensated through the systems they observe, the relationship is disclosed. Their evidence remains admissible but is not independent corroboration without another channel.

## 14.6 Adversarial Explanation Portfolio

High-consequence assessments should test competing explanations, including:

- local incapacity;
- upstream rule or infrastructure failure;
- measurement error;
- deliberate non-participation;
- extractive trade or settlement conditions;
- local capture;
- compact holdout;
- external shock;
- wrong jurisdiction.

Plural review should expose disagreement rather than average it away.

## 14.7 Sectoral Concentration and Crowding-Out Sentinel

A successful economic subsystem can degrade the diversity, productive capacity, affordability, informal provision, autonomy, or exit options of the economy around it, without any actor failing to conform and without any transaction being improper.

The hypothesis this sentinel tests is:

> A sufficiently resourced sector may generate wage, land, talent, or procurement differentials capable of displacing other forms of provisioning, and dependence on that sector may then constrain the community's practical choices.

The hypothesis is not assumed. It is a candidate explanation to be tested against observation, and its absence is as reportable as its presence.

### 14.7.1 Scope

The sentinel is not specific to finance. Applicable cases include resource extraction booms, tourism dependence, external aid dependence, a single dominant employer, defence or public-institution dependence, and a rapidly scaling Hearts, Leaves, or GGF-linked sector within a BAZ.

A GGF-linked sector is explicitly in scope. A BAZ in which a large share of skilled labour, income, or enterprise revenue derives from Hearts administration, validation, Community Provider contracting, or Global Commons Fund flows is exhibiting the condition this sentinel exists to detect, whatever the merits of the underlying activity.

### 14.7.2 Observation

Observation should distinguish, and not combine into a score:

- sector share of employment, and rate of change;
- sector share of local income or output;
- wage differential relative to other local sectors;
- rent and land-cost differential in areas concentrated on the sector;
- share of enterprise revenue derived from one funder, buyer, or procurement channel;
- contraction, exit, or non-formation of other productive sectors;
- displacement of unpaid, informal, mutual, or non-monetized provision;
- reliance on the sector for services with no realistic local substitute;
- the extent to which a decision to slow, decline, or reverse sector growth remains practically available.

No composite sectoral concentration or financialization index shall be constructed. Section 13.1 applies.

### 14.7.3 Interpretation

A high sector share is not itself a finding. Small economies are frequently and legitimately concentrated. The finding is a **displacement claim**: that growth in one sector caused decline elsewhere.

An adversarial explanation portfolio under §14.6 should be prepared, including at minimum:

- the sector grew and others declined for unrelated reasons;
- the displaced activity was voluntarily abandoned as conditions improved;
- measurement is capturing formalization of previously informal activity rather than displacement;
- the differential is temporary and transitional;
- the affected population disagrees that displacement is a loss.

Displacement of non-monetized provision is particularly susceptible to measurement artefact, because formalization and displacement look identical in most instruments. Where they cannot be distinguished, that shall be recorded as unresolved rather than resolved toward either reading.

### 14.7.4 Response

The sentinel authorizes diagnosis and deliberation. It authorizes no intervention, sanction, funding change, or scaling requirement.

Where a displacement claim is sustained, the competent BAZ authority determines the response, which may include declining to formalize or further scale a local Hearts economy under Financial Systems §7.3, diversification support, procurement adjustment, protection of informal provision under §2.5, or a decision to accept the concentration with reasons recorded.

No GGF body acquires authority over the composition of a local economy by operating this sentinel.

# 15. Implementation Stages and Proportional Record Profiles

The stages organize development; they do not form a maturity ranking or automatic path to greater integration. Each consequential action selects a GMEAIA consequence profile through the relevant GMEAIA record.

## 15.1 Stage 1 — Convening and Boundary Mapping

A community maps recurring needs, formal and informal economies, external dependencies, practices that remain non-monetized, excluded populations, neighbouring BAZs, ecological systems, and causal controllers.

No BAZ Council or cross-scale authority is created. Most activity is `CP0` or `CP1`.

## 15.2 Stage 2 — Proto-Economy

The community establishes authorized basic financial and provider functions, accessible formal and low-tech records, correction and complaint routes, and supported Community Weavers.

## 15.3 Stage 3 — Local Operating Capacity

The community demonstrates provider diversity, reserve compliance, access and waiting-time monitoring, missing-population channels, administrative-capacity monitoring, correction, information-system separation, and independent review.

Low activity caused by sufficiency, seasonality, or voluntary non-monetized provision does not force expansion.

## 15.4 Stage 4 — BAZ Governance Formalization

Before formalization, the BAZ establishes:

- internal and cross-scale decision rights;
- Observation Contracts and State Estimate Records where required;
- causal-responsibility and authority-formation records;
- individual and provider epistemic rights;
- common-mode dependency review;
- exit and fork provisions;
- data sovereignty and protected unobservability;
- administrative and appeal capacity;
- clear framework interfaces.

## 15.5 Stage 5 — Bilateral Interface

The BAZ tests a bounded interface with another BAZ. The design must be no larger than necessary for safety and no smaller than necessary to expose the claimed settlement, recognition, service, ecological, or dependency mechanism.

Each interface uses a Cross-Scale Interface Contract, response route, fallback, dispute process, material-exit test, and closure.

## 15.6 Stage 6 — Networked Coordination

Several BAZs may establish shared infrastructure, procurement, solidarity, technical services, or a compact. The arrangement records delegation or joint constitution, bargaining asymmetry, response duties, objective-reproduction risks, and dissolution.

## 15.7 Stage 7 — Global Integration

A mature BAZ may connect to global settlement, funding, standards, compacts, and commons systems only where local authority, rights, portability, protected difference, counter-model capacity, fallback, material exit, and common-mode resilience remain credible.

## 15.8 Core-Plus-Trigger Documentation

Documentation must follow GMEAIA proportionality:

- `CP0`: compact non-operative record for exploration or simulation;
- `CP1`: voluntary bounded-practice core plus triggered records;
- `CP2`: authority, affected parties, causal responsibility, activation, implementation, monitoring, challenge, closure, and triggered dependencies;
- `CP3`: `CP2` plus the controlling protected-domain interface.

Existing valid records should be reused. Translation and conformance burden should fall primarily on the actor proposing the cross-scale arrangement, not on the lower-capacity BAZ.

## 15.9 Minimum Viable and Minimum Causally Adequate Implementation

A minimum implementation must be both:

- small enough to preserve rights, reversibility, and local capacity;
- large and connected enough to expose the causal mechanism it claims to test.

An underpowered pilot may establish acceptability or feasibility but must not be represented as evidence of system effectiveness or transferability.

# 16. Crisis, Failure, Exit, and Graceful Degradation

## 16.1 Crisis Coordination

A crisis may justify temporary mutual aid, logistics, provider support, technical fallback, solidarity, or accelerated interface review under the controlling emergency interfaces.

Crisis authority does not automatically permit diversion of protected AUBI, permanent centralization, compulsory narrative reporting, permanent sensor changes, suspension of Indigenous or personal rights, or indefinite prevention of exit.

## 16.2 Crisis Constraints

Emergency arrangements state the trigger, decision class, affected groups, protected minima, authority, response clocks, preserved evidence, expiry, renewal limits, challenge, independent outcomes, after-action review, and authority/data restoration.

## 16.3 Interface Failure

Interfaces maintain fallback for outage, settlement failure, corrupted data, compromised platforms, disputed mappings, institutional capture, and inability to verify protected claims.

Fallback may include local operation, offline queues, manual verification, bilateral clearing, temporary recognition without conversion, or suspension of new transactions while preserving existing rights.

## 16.4 Compact Failure

If a compact fails:

- essential services continue during transition;
- liabilities and unresolved duties remain visible;
- records return or transfer lawfully;
- shared assets follow the compact;
- workers and affected users are protected;
- AUBI and undisputed balances remain protected;
- member BAZs may form a narrower replacement;
- the failure does not manufacture a single causal diagnosis.

## 16.5 Centralization or Joint-Authority Failure

Where an authority arrangement fails:

- scope expansion stops;
- independent review begins;
- affected parties receive interim protection and restoration options;
- delegated or transferred power returns where lawful;
- jointly constituted power is narrowed, reconstituted, or dissolved;
- data and infrastructure custody are resolved;
- continuing duties remain with named actors.

## 16.6 Local Capture or Rights Failure

Subsidiarity does not protect local abuse. Affected people retain direct standing, independent review may bypass captured local bodies where lawfully authorized, external protection remains minimum and temporary, and restoration and return remain mandatory once protection can safely localize.

## 16.7 Holdout, Refusal, and Failed Handoff

Lawful refusal remains possible. Strategic delay, `capacity_limited`, `timed_out`, `misrouted`, and `constitutional_gap` states remain distinct under GMEAIA.

A failed handoff does not erase sender duties, create recipient acceptance, or authorize Meta-Governance to act substantively. It triggers the applicable mediation, adjudication, support, interim protection, or lawful constituting route.

## 16.8 Exit without Abandonment

Exit balances freedom to leave with liabilities, essential-service continuity, vulnerable populations, environmental duties, shared assets, worker rights, and prior harm.

The exit plan must demonstrate legal, technical, financial, service, political, and material feasibility. A paper right to leave is not sufficient.

## 16.9 Closure and Unresolved Duties

Closure requires authority return, credential and access termination, data disposition, asset and liability settlement, worker and service continuity, public reasons, unresolved-duty custody, remedy, and reopening where claims remain.

The GMEAIA closure state may remain `closed_with_unresolved_duties` where the arrangement has ended but obligations survive.

# 17. Framework Interfaces

## 17.1 Constitutional Interface Specification

CIS governs plural legitimacy, causal subsidiarity, jointly constituted authority, response duties, protected continuity, minimum necessary authority, proportional process, and constitutional-gap doctrine.

Nested Economies applies those principles to economic relations; it does not redefine them.

## 17.2 GMEAIA

GMEAIA controls:

- authority-effect and normative-force classification;
- `CP0`–`CP3` consequence profiles;
- authority formation;
- causal-responsibility records;
- response and handoff states;
- clocks, activation, challenge, correction, closure, and unresolved duties;
- compatibility, versioning, and framework falsification.

Nested Economies supplies the economic profile only.

## 17.3 AUBI Framework

AUBI governs material-security layers, Hearts/fiat safeguards, LMCI, low-velocity diagnostics, individual epistemic rights, and relevant implementation. Nested Economies governs portability and cross-BAZ coordination without altering AUBI rights.

## 17.4 Financial Systems Framework

Financial Systems governs Hearts and Leaves mechanics, Love Ledger separation, Bridge Protocol, reserve hierarchy, issuance, demurrage, Treasury resolution, fraud, ICTL, NARP, and financial stress testing.

Nested Economies governs subsidiarity, compact, political standing, causal responsibility, interface, bargaining, and exit around those systems.

## 17.5 Work in Liberation Framework

Work in Liberation governs labour standards, Community Provider and Weaver professions, work valuation, occupational governance, and worker rights. Nested Economies governs cross-BAZ recognition and shared-service placement.

## 17.6 Indigenous Sovereignty Interfaces

Affected Indigenous peoples determine their representatives, law, territorial authority, consent, protected knowledge, and non-engagement. No generic GGF representative substitutes for the affected people.

Nested Economies records its own duties of non-interference, consent, restitution, and protected non-transfer.

## 17.7 Integrated Meta-Governance

Meta-Governance supplies bounded coordination, response and omission visibility, constitutional-gap routing, interface review, and closure support. It does not acquire economic jurisdiction from a failed compact, delayed response, dependency map, or causal diagnosis.

## 17.8 Treaty for Our Only Home

The Treaty supplies lawfully constituted rights floors, commons obligations, Earth Council authority, enforcement jurisdiction, and planetary crisis architecture.

## 17.9 Technology Governance

Technology Governance supplies security, algorithm governance, privacy engineering, portability, infrastructure audit, and accessibility. Technical compatibility does not determine political legitimacy or subsidiarity.

# 18. Irreducible Design Tensions

## 18.1 Autonomy versus Interdependence

Local freedom can externalize harm; shared coordination can erase agency. The framework manages this through causal responsibility, joint constitution, limited authority, affected standing, and return.

## 18.2 Interoperability versus Local Meaning

Common interfaces compress context. Semantic loss, protected fields, non-comparable categories, and non-transfer remain legitimate outcomes.

## 18.3 Solidarity versus Dependency

Transfers can protect capability or entrench allocator power. Causal review, local ownership, maintenance, support-withdrawal testing, sunset, and instrument change remain necessary.

## 18.4 Transparency versus Protected Unobservability

Accountability needs evidence, but total legibility chills relational and cultural life. Transparency focuses on authority, methods, institutions, and aggregate effects.

## 18.5 Standardization versus Innovation

Standards enable coordination but may freeze one design. Forkability, local variants, versioning, and compatibility-power review protect innovation.

## 18.6 Exit versus Shared Obligation

Meaningful exit prevents capture, but cannot erase prior harm, shared assets, or rights duties. Exit must be materially possible and orderly.

## 18.7 Capacity versus Authority

Low capacity needs support, yet support can become control. Capacity limitation must not be misclassified as absence of jurisdiction.

## 18.8 Voluntary Coordination versus Holdout

Voluntary agreement protects autonomy, but powerful actors may delay while others bear the harm. Response duties, interim protection, affected standing, and lawful adjudication address the gap without making agreement compulsory.

## 18.9 Two Tiers versus Durable Regional Institutions

The two-tier model avoids automatic regional sovereignty. Some relational systems may nevertheless require durable jointly constituted authority. The architecture must remain open to that evidence without treating every regional interdependence as a new tier.

# 19. Framework Assurance and Falsification

## 19.1 Theory of Change

The framework expects that causal subsidiarity, voluntary or jointly constituted coordination, protected difference, material exit, and bounded global standards can manage interdependence with less domination and externalization than either isolated localism or generalized central planning.

## 19.2 Framework Hypotheses

The framework places the following claims at risk:

1. causal-subsidiarity review reduces responsibility dumping without causing unnecessary centralization;
2. voluntary and jointly constituted compacts solve a sufficient share of cross-boundary problems;
3. optional coordination layers do not regularly become covert governments;
4. interface contracts preserve local meaning without excluding low-capacity BAZs;
5. material exit is usable beyond wealthy or technically capable BAZs;
6. solidarity builds capability without durable dependency;
7. protected non-convertibility preserves value without shielding abuse;
8. the two-tier architecture performs better than a default fixed regional tier;
9. response and omission records reduce unresolved holdout without creating residual sovereignty;
10. proportional documentation lowers burden while preserving rights and traceability.

## 19.3 Disconfirming Evidence

Evidence that should trigger narrowing, redesign, or replacement includes:

- repeated externalities unresolved by voluntary or jointly constituted arrangements;
- systematic holdout by powerful BAZs;
- regional functions becoming permanent without adequate representation;
- exit rights usable only by high-capacity actors;
- common infrastructure making autonomy nominal;
- solidarity increasing dependency or allocator power;
- interface administration excluding low-capacity communities;
- causal-responsibility mapping rarely changing allocation of duties;
- response clocks adding delay without improving accountability;
- conformance costs exceeding the harms prevented;
- a durable regional constitutional tier consistently outperforming optional compacts.

## 19.4 Narrowing, Suspension, and Replacement

A mechanism should be narrowed or suspended where it adds burden, delay, dependency, or exclusion without credible independent benefit. The framework may be replaced in part where another architecture better preserves rights, causal accountability, material autonomy, and timely coordination.

No finding automatically dissolves a BAZ or compact. Consequential change follows the competent CIS and GMEAIA route.

## 19.5 Review

Framework review shall include:

- successful and failed compacts;
- abandoned and non-started processes;
- failed exits;
- affected non-member claims;
- common-mode failures;
- administrative burden;
- coordination avoided, simplified, devolved, or ended;
- evidence from non-GGF comparators where feasible.

# Appendix H — Glossary

- **AUBI:** Adaptive Universal Basic Income.
- **Authority formation:** GMEAIA classification as retained, delegated, transferred, concurrent, jointly constituted, or absent.
- **BAZ:** Bioregional Autonomous Zone.
- **Causal responsibility:** Alignment of responsibility with legal and practical control over causes, jurisdiction, capacity, and resources.
- **Consequence profile:** GMEAIA `CP0`–`CP3` proportional record profile.
- **Constitutional gap:** No identified authority possesses sufficient lawful jurisdiction; not merely lack of capacity.
- **Coordination layer:** Function-specific arrangement among BAZs; not a governing tier by default.
- **Cross-Scale Interface Contract:** Protocol defining what crosses a boundary, how, with which duties, dependencies, and exit conditions.
- **Economic or causal subsidiarity:** Rule that authority and responsibility remain at the least intrusive capable arrangement controlling the relevant causes.
- **Hearts:** Formal regenerative currency governed by AUBI and Financial Systems.
- **ICTL:** Inter-Currency Translation Layer governed by Financial Systems.
- **Jointly constituted authority:** Authority created by participating jurisdictions to govern a relational object no party possesses alone.
- **Leaves:** Non-fungible ecological records and reward instruments.
- **LMCI-A:** Contribution-level allocation system for AUBI Layer 2.
- **LMCI-M:** Independent population-level Love, Meaning, and Connection measurement.
- **Love Ledger:** Voluntary, non-monetized recognition system.
- **Material exit:** Practical ability to leave across legal, technical, financial, service, political, and operational dimensions.
- **NARP:** Notice and Adaptive Response Protocol.
- **Protected non-convertibility:** Rule that some values, rights, knowledge, and relationships are not presumed translatable into currency or score.
- **Right Relationship:** Balanced, regenerative relations among people, communities, cultures, ecosystems, and generations.
- **Solidarity Protocol:** Governed process for diagnosing, authorizing, reviewing, converting, and ending cross-BAZ support.
