## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Purpose

The Digital Commons Framework proposes to govern the **shared knowledge infrastructure** of the GGF ecosystem — the protocols, institutions, and practices through which communities create, curate, contest, preserve, retire, and transmit knowledge across bioregional, cultural, and generational boundaries.

It addresses a specific gap: the Aurora Accord (Tier 1) governs *data* — its sovereignty, portability, fiduciary handling, and the rights attached to data as a category. The Digital Commons Framework governs *knowledge* — the social and institutional processes by which data becomes meaning, meaning becomes transmissible, and transmission becomes collective inheritance.

### 1.2 The Data/Knowledge Boundary

This distinction does significant architectural work and should be stated clearly:

- A **temperature reading** from a sensor is *data* (Aurora Accord).
- A community's **interpretation** of that temperature for planting decisions is *knowledge* (Digital Commons).
- An Indigenous song is *knowledge* (Digital Commons), but the recording file is *data* (Aurora Accord), and the IP stewardship transition is governed by the **Hearthstone Protocol**.

Where both apply — for instance, a dataset that is also Traditional Knowledge — the Digital Commons Framework calls on the Aurora Accord for consent lineage and sovereignty classification, then applies its own access and stewardship protocols.

This boundary is conceptually useful but practically blurry. Some knowledge artifacts are inseparable from their data substrate. The framework proposes coordination interfaces rather than clean separation, and acknowledges that boundary cases will require ongoing MGCC arbitration.

### 1.3 Scope

The framework aims to steward:

- **Cultural knowledge** — oral histories, narratives, community practices, artistic traditions
- **Collective memory** — contested histories, intergenerational transmission, memorial archives
- **Educational knowledge** — curricula, pedagogical resources, learning materials
- **Technical knowledge** — open-source blueprints, repair guides, agricultural practices
- **Wisdom traditions** — spiritual teachings, philosophical resources, contemplative practices
- **Epistemic infrastructure** — standards for how knowledge is verified, contested, and evolved

The framework does **not** govern:
- Data sovereignty, consent, or fiduciary obligations (Aurora Accord)
- Digital infrastructure, mesh networks, or cybersecurity (Conduit Protocol / Aurora Accord)
- Technology governance or AI ethics (TGIF / Global Technology Council)
- Intellectual property transition to stewardship (Hearthstone Protocol)

### 1.4 Core Objectives

- Propose stewardship infrastructure for knowledge as a living, contested commons
- Maintain the distinction between knowledge preservation and knowledge evolution without collapsing either
- Provide mechanisms for epistemic pluralism that do not degrade into epistemic paralysis
- Establish governance for knowledge retirement and forgetting, not only retention
- Enable downstream frameworks (Cultural Heritage, Religious Dialogue, Arts Flourishing, Consciousness Development) with shared knowledge infrastructure
- Integrate with the GGF economic layer (Hearts, Love Ledger, AUBI) to recognize knowledge stewardship as valued work

### 1.5 What This Framework Does Not Claim

This is a governance architecture draft. It proposes structures that would need validation through participatory design with Indigenous communities, pilot implementation in diverse BAZs, and reconciliation with existing GGF framework designers. Language throughout uses "proposes," "aims to enable," and "would need validation" rather than "provides," "ensures," or "guarantees."

## 3. Core Governance Architecture

### 3.1 Digital Commons Council (DCC)

A distributed governance body — not a centralized authority, but a standards-setting and conflict-arbitration council.

**Representation requirements (proposed):**
- Strong participation from Local Citizen Nodes / BAZ-level knowledge stewards and users of the commons
- Meaningful Indigenous and marginalized-community participation through those communities' own authorized processes where their knowledge or rights are implicated
- Enabling-cluster participation from adjacent knowledge/cultural/education frameworks where coordination is genuinely required
- Technical stewardship and infrastructure literacy sufficient for protocol interoperability and auditability
- Anti-capture safeguards so no single constituency, technical guild, or GGF cluster dominates the council

**Composition status:**
- The former 40% / 30% / 20% / 10% distribution is illustrative historical design material, not a self-executing constitutional quota.
- The DCC's valid constituting charter determines selectors, nomination routes, terms, vacancies, recall/removal, voting/consensus rules, conflict rules, and challenge routes.
- Indigenous participation must follow the affected peoples' own governance pathways. Neither Earth Council liaison nor any GGF body may appoint proxies on their behalf unless separately authorized by those peoples and by applicable constitutional instruments.

**Mandate:**
- Set interoperability standards for knowledge commons across BAZs
- Arbitrate boundary cases between Heritage and Living knowledge classifications (see Section 4)
- Produce annual **Commons Health Report** assessing knowledge ecosystem vitality
- Coordinate with MGCC on cross-framework governance questions

**Decision protocols:**
- Operational decisions by consent-based process (proposals pass unless a reasoned, articulable objection is raised)
- Changes to TEK-related protocols require the consent, concurrence, or review process specified by the competent Indigenous-governance authority and the DCC's valid charter; Earth Council participates only if separately authorized
- Changes to knowledge classification standards require 90-day BAZ objection window, mirroring Nested Economies' Slow-Variable Constraint
- DCC has no enforcement power — it flags violations to Digital Justice Tribunal or Earth Council

**Meetings:** Hybrid quarterly sessions, with emergency sessions triggered by BAZ Council or Digital Justice Tribunal referral.

**Accountability:** Annual citizen-assembly or equivalent public reviews. Any recall/removal rule — including a 60% constituency-recall threshold — must be enacted by the DCC's valid charter rather than assumed by this framework.

**Open question:** Whether the DCC should be a new body or a committee within an existing council (e.g., MGCC or GDGC). Creating new councils in complex governance ecosystems produces coordination overhead. This needs GGF-level architectural review.

### 3.2 Local Citizen Nodes

BAZ-level knowledge stewardship hubs — physical-digital presences, not purely online.

**Core functions:**
- Curate BAZ-level contributions to the knowledge commons
- Administer local Knowledge Stewardship License registrations
- Validate knowledge contributions for Proof of Care / Love Ledger recognition
- Maintain local knowledge repositories that sync to global commons under DCC protocols
- Host deliberative processes when local knowledge traditions conflict with commons standards
- Digitize and steward hyper-local non-web knowledge (oral histories, community meeting records, folklore, local ecological knowledge)

**Staffing:**
- **Knowledge Stewards** — trained role analogous to Community Weavers in Nested Economies (6-month training program covering knowledge curation, attribution ethics, TEK sensitivity protocols, classification administration). Compensated via AUBI Layer 2.

**Operational requirements:**
- Offline-first design (can function with tablet + solar uplink + SMS interface)
- Multilingual metadata support
- Aurora Accord-compliant consent interfaces for all contributions

**Integration note:** The Nested Economies framework already trains Community Weavers across BAZs. Whether Knowledge Stewards are a distinct role or a specialization within Community Weavers needs piloting. The skill sets differ (economic facilitation vs. epistemic curation), and collapsing them risks producing people who do both poorly. But maintaining parallel training pipelines is expensive.

## 4. Knowledge Classification and Versioning

### 4.1 The Heritage/Living Distinction

All knowledge artifacts in the commons carry a classification that governs how they may evolve:

**Heritage Knowledge** — Frozen in the form authorized by its originating stewards. Cannot be modified without FPIC from those stewards. Heritage artifacts are preserved exactly as authorized. Attempts to create derivative works that claim to represent Heritage knowledge without authorization are flagged as **Unverified Echoes** (see 4.3) and cannot serve as governance references.

**Living Knowledge** — Open to evolution through forking. Any registered participant may propose a divergent version. Living Knowledge evolves through parallel lineages with full provenance tracking, not through overwriting.

**Mixed** — Knowledge that straddles both categories. The originating community designates a rotating group of Living Stewards who may propose changes, but every change leaves a visible trace accessible to the original stewards. Requires a governance covenant specifying boundaries.

**Classification authority:** Knowledge originators choose their classification at contribution. Reclassification requires DCC review with originating community concurrence.

### 4.2 Fork/Merge Governance

The framework proposes an asymmetry: **forks are easy; merges are hard.**

- Any registered participant may fork any Living Knowledge artifact without permission. The fork inherits the parent's provenance chain but is marked as divergent.
- Forks that claim to correct or supersede a Heritage artifact must display a prominent notice: *"This diverges from a protected Heritage artifact. View the provenance chain for context."*
- Merges (synthesizing divergent forks into a shared version) require documented reconciliation and DCC review, or a determination that differences are non-substantive.

This asymmetry biases *toward preservation* for contested knowledge while allowing evolution through parallel lineages. It treats disagreement as a structural feature, not a bug.

**Provenance tracking:** Every fork, merge, annotation, and reclassification is recorded in the commons provenance registry — an immutable log (using Aurora Accord-compatible decentralized identifiers) that tracks who, when, why, and from what parent. This is a provenance registry, not a content store.

### 4.3 Unverified Echoes

An Echo is a derivative work that claims to represent or speak for a Heritage artifact without authorization from its stewards. Echoes are:

- Allowed to exist (the framework does not censor)
- Flagged in the provenance registry with a visible marker
- Excluded from governance use (cannot serve as basis for policy decisions, AUBI qualification, legal proceedings, or Earth Council assessments)
- Challengeable through Digital Justice Tribunal if they cause demonstrable harm

The distinction between a legitimate fork and a malicious echo is adjudicated by the DCC with a presumption of good faith. Burden of proof for malice is high — clear intent to deceive plus demonstrable harm.

## 5. The Central Tension: Epistemic Pluralism vs. Epistemic Integrity

The Digital Commons Framework must simultaneously serve:

1. **The Synoptic Protocol's** commitment to information integrity — the position that there are better and worse claims about reality, and that knowledge commons should not become "everything is equally valid" relativism.

2. **The Indigenous Framework's** insistence that some knowledge systems operate with fundamentally different epistemologies that cannot be adjudicated by Western scientific standards without doing violence to them.

3. **The MOS's** principle of Cognitive Pluralism — that diverse cognitive and reasoning styles are a resource, not a deficiency.

This tension cannot be resolved. It can only be held structurally.

### 5.1 Proposed Structural Approach

The framework proposes that epistemic integrity standards apply **within** knowledge traditions, not **between** them. Claims made within a scientific framework are evaluated for integrity by scientific standards. Claims made within an Indigenous knowledge system are evaluated by that system's own integrity standards (which the Indigenous Framework governs). The Digital Commons provides meta-infrastructure that keeps these systems legible to each other without forcing commensurability.

### 5.2 Epistemic Conflict Governance

When knowledge systems make competing claims about the same material reality — for instance, when an Indigenous ecological knowledge system and a quantitative ecology model disagree about land management — the framework proposes a deliberation process rather than an adjudication:

**Weave Forums:** Structured deliberation for synthesizing or mapping incompatible knowledge claims.

**Process:**
1. Any registered participant may petition a BAZ Council to convene a Weave Forum for a set of conflicting knowledge artifacts (minimum two forks, ten signatures)
2. Panel selected through fractal representation (stratified random sampling): five knowledge holders from each affected community, three subject matter practitioners, two facilitators trained in conflict transformation (from Peace & Conflict framework). If Indigenous knowledge is involved, panel selection follows Indigenous Framework protocols.
3. Deliberation period: 60–90 days. Panel reviews provenance chains, hears testimony, and identifies areas of agreement, irreducible disagreements (with rationales), and hybrid possibilities.
4. Output: A **Weave Document** — not a truth claim, but a map of the contested terrain. The Weave becomes a new artifact linked to all parent forks.
5. Weave Documents are non-binding. However, any GGF body relying on knowledge for governance must either use a stable reference (see 5.3) or publish a justification for why the Weave Document's mapping of disagreement does not affect their decision.

**What this does not resolve:** Communities with profound epistemic disagreement will not find common ground through this process. The framework does not attempt to force synthesis. It ensures both perspectives can coexist as artifacts, that governance bodies cannot hide the existence of disagreement, and that no one is forced to accept a false reconciliation.

### 5.3 Stable References (Anchors)

For governance purposes (legal definitions, AUBI eligibility criteria, ecological assessments), the Digital Commons may designate specific knowledge artifacts as **Anchors** — stable reference points used for coordination.

- Anchor designation requires DCC supermajority (7/9 or equivalent)
- All Anchors carry a mandatory sunset clause (maximum 10 years)
- Before renewal, a Weave Forum must assess whether new knowledge or contested interpretations warrant a revised Anchor
- Earth Council may override Anchor designations that affect Indigenous sovereignty

This prevents permanent freezing of governance-relevant knowledge while providing necessary stability.

## 6. Knowledge Stewardship License

The Digital Commons proposes a licensing framework for knowledge artifacts that moves beyond the open/closed binary. Four tiers, assigned by knowledge originators at contribution with DCC standards support:

### 6.1 Tiers

**Open Commons** — Fully open. Attribution required. Non-enclosure clause: knowledge can be used, built upon, and redistributed, but cannot be enclosed (patented, paywalled, or made proprietary). Derivatives must carry provenance chains.

**Contextual Commons** — Open with contextual requirements. Knowledge is accessible but comes with mandatory contextual metadata: where it came from, what epistemic tradition it belongs to, known limitations, and competing perspectives. The requirement is context, not restriction. Aims to prevent decontextualized extraction.

**Community-Gated** — Knowledge registered in the commons but access governed by the originating community's protocols. The Digital Commons provides the interface (discoverability, metadata, request mechanisms) but the community controls the gate.

**Heritage/Sacred** — Access governed entirely by originating stewards, with Earth Council oversight for Indigenous knowledge. Sacred Data Protocol governs access rules. The Digital Commons hosts but does not control.

### 6.2 Reciprocity

When knowledge from the commons generates economic value (e.g., a bioregional business builds on commons agricultural knowledge), a percentage flows through the Hearts system back to originating stewards and the maintaining community. Percentage and formula would need DCC standard-setting and integration with the Nested Economies' Inter-Currency Translation Layer.

### 6.3 Enforcement

License violations are adjudicable through the Digital Justice Tribunal. Remedies may include Hearts-based redistribution requirements, temporary access suspension, or provenance-chain correction orders.

**Open question:** The Knowledge Stewardship License interacts with existing legal IP regimes globally. How it interfaces with copyright, patent law, and trade agreements is a multi-year legal design challenge requiring international law expertise.

## 7. Governed Forgetting

Digital commons default to permanent retention. The Digital Commons Framework proposes that some knowledge *should* be retired from active circulation — when it becomes harmful, obsolete in ways that cause damage, or when its continued availability violates dignity.

### 7.1 Retirement Tiers

| Tier | Name | What Happens | Reversibility |
|------|------|-------------|---------------|
| R0 | **Shelved** | Removed from active circulation; metadata remains visible (title, steward, reason for shelving) | DCC vote (66%) |
| R1 | **Composted** | Metadata remains but access requires DCC petition demonstrating changed circumstances. Content annotated with reason for retirement, cross-references to superseding knowledge, original preserved intact. | DCC supermajority (80%) + harm review |
| R2 | **Sealed** | Content cryptographically sealed; accessible only through Digital Justice Tribunal petition for historical research | Tribunal review only |

### 7.2 Safeguards

- **Two-witness rule:** For R0-R1, two independent stewards must separately confirm the knowledge existed (prevents silent erasure)
- **Sunset reviews:** R0 decisions expire after 10 years unless renewed; R1 after 25 years
- **Appeal path:** Any participant may petition to restore retired knowledge, citing changed circumstances or original decision errors
- **Transparency:** All retirement decisions are public with documented reasoning
- **Censorship guard:** The DCC's consent-based process means any reasoned objection to politically motivated retirement blocks the decision. Retiring knowledge should be harder than preserving it.

### 7.3 Integration

Aurora Accord's Right to Be Forgotten operates at the individual data level. The Digital Commons' governed forgetting operates at the knowledge commons level. These are complementary mechanisms with different scopes and different governance processes.

**What this does not resolve:** "Harmful" and "obsolete" are not neutral categories — they are sites of power. The safeguards proposed here (challenge mechanisms, transparency, sunset clauses, preservation of original content at R1) are necessary but may be insufficient. This mechanism needs adversarial stress-testing before deployment.

## 8. Knowledge Maintenance as Care Work

Open-source ecosystems and knowledge commons historically fail through the "Tragedy of the Maintainer" — creators are celebrated, but the ongoing work of maintaining, updating, translating, contextualizing, and curating knowledge goes unrecognized and unrewarded.

### 8.1 Recognition through the Economic Layer

The Digital Commons proposes to route knowledge maintenance through the existing GGF economic infrastructure:

- **Love Ledger** tracks informal knowledge contributions: annotation, contextualizing, mentoring, oral history recording, translation, community curation
- **Proof of Care** validation applies to knowledge stewardship contributions, scored with a **knowledge maintenance multiplier** (proposed 1.0x–1.3x, calibrated against LMCI principles)
- **AUBI Layer 2** compensates Knowledge Stewards and other formal knowledge maintenance roles
- **Hearts** flow to knowledge workers whose contributions are validated: educators, translators, archivists, annotators, Weave Forum facilitators

### 8.2 Maintenance/Creation Balance

Healthy knowledge commons should invest more in maintaining existing knowledge than producing new knowledge. The DCC proposes to track a **Maintenance-to-Creation Ratio** across BAZ commons, targeting 1.5:1 or above. This metric is an indicator, not a binding target — it surfaces whether commons are being sustainably maintained or merely accumulated.

**Open question:** Hearts valuation for knowledge contributions may create gaming incentives similar to the metric failures the MOS warns against. A knowledge maintenance multiplier could incentivize low-quality annotation for Hearts extraction. Anti-gaming mechanisms from the Synoptic Protocol and MOS Institutional Integrity Protocols would need to be integrated.

## 9. AI-Generated Knowledge

The Aurora Accord covers AI training data. The Digital Commons needs a position on whether AI-generated knowledge can be contributed to the commons, and under what conditions.

### 9.1 Proposed Rules

- AI-generated knowledge artifacts are marked as **synthetic** in the provenance registry
- Synthetic artifacts require a **human sponsor** who takes fiduciary responsibility under Aurora Accord standards
- Synthetic artifacts cannot be classified as Heritage Knowledge
- Synthetic artifacts cannot serve as governance Anchors without Weave Forum review
- AI-generated derivatives of Heritage Knowledge require the same FPIC as human-created derivatives

### 9.2 Unresolved Questions

- How does the commons handle AI-generated knowledge that blends multiple source traditions in ways that obscure provenance?
- At what point does AI synthesis cross from "useful tool" to "epistemic colonialism by algorithm"?
- Should AI systems be permitted as participants in Weave Forums, and if so, under what constraints?

These questions intersect with the Technology OS and with increasingly urgent questions about epistemic provenance. The framework flags them rather than resolving them prematurely.

## 10. Implementation Pathway

### 10.1 Phased Rollout

**Phase 1 — Foundation (Years 1–3):**
- Establish DCC with interim composition (expandable as BAZ network grows)
- Pilot Knowledge Steward training in 5–10 culturally diverse BAZs (proposed: one Indigenous-led, one urban tech hub, one agrarian, one post-conflict, one small-island developing state)
- Deploy provenance registry prototype (minimal viable infrastructure, tested at 10,000 artifacts)
- Draft Knowledge Stewardship License v1.0 with legal review
- Conduct first Weave Forum pilot on a low-stakes contested knowledge topic

**Phase 2 — Scaling (Years 3–7):**
- Expand to 50+ BAZ nodes
- Integrate Maintenance-to-Creation tracking into DCC reporting
- Operationalize governed forgetting (R0 tier only in Phase 2)
- Launch Reciprocity mechanism for commons-generated economic value
- First round of Anchor designations with sunset clauses

**Phase 3 — Maturation (Years 7+):**
- Full four-tier Knowledge Stewardship License operational
- Governed forgetting expanded to R1-R2 tiers with track record
- AI-generated knowledge governance operationalized
- Cross-BAZ knowledge exchange protocols stabilized
- First Anchor sunset reviews completed

### 10.2 Funding

Knowledge Steward compensation flows through AUBI Layer 2. Infrastructure costs (provenance registry, hosting) flow through the Global Commons Fund. DCC operations funded through Meta-Governance budget. No new taxation mechanism proposed — the Digital Commons rides on existing GGF economic infrastructure.

## 12. Honest Uncertainty

### 12.1 Structural Uncertainties

1. **The data/knowledge boundary** is conceptually useful but practically messy. Knowledge is encoded in data, and data governance decisions constrain knowledge governance possibilities. This framework proposes coordination rather than clean separation, but the boundary will require ongoing negotiation with Aurora Accord governance.

2. **DCC as new institution vs. committee** of existing governance. Governance proliferation is a real GGF risk. Whether the DCC justifies its coordination overhead needs architectural review.

3. **Knowledge Steward vs. Community Weaver** role integration. Both are 6-month trained BAZ roles compensated through AUBI Layer 2. Running parallel training pipelines is expensive; collapsing them risks producing people who do both poorly. Needs pilot evidence.

4. **Scalability of provenance tracking.** Immutable provenance registries with rich metadata across potentially millions of artifacts, multilingual, with complex forking histories — the technical feasibility at GGF scale is genuinely uncertain and needs infrastructure assessment.

5. **Legal status of Knowledge Stewardship License.** It proposes to operate alongside existing IP regimes without specifying how. This is a multi-year legal design challenge.

### 12.2 What Might Be Wrong

6. **Governed forgetting has censorship risk** despite safeguards. The framework's own safeguards (challenge mechanisms, sunset clauses, transparency) may be insufficient against determined political weaponization.

7. **The Weave Forum process may be too slow** for rapidly evolving knowledge domains and too expensive in facilitator time. It may work for high-stakes disputes but be impractical as a routine governance mechanism.

8. **The framework assumes all knowledge *can* be governed through commons mechanisms.** Some knowledge systems — particularly oral, embodied, and initiatory traditions — may be fundamentally incompatible with any archival framework, even a respectful one. The opt-out provisions (communities can decline participation) attempt to address this, but may be insufficient.

9. **Hearts integration may create gaming incentives.** Rewarding knowledge contributions with Hearts could incentivize volume over quality, or create artificial knowledge production for currency extraction.

10. **Material conditions are unaddressed.** The framework says nothing about server locations, energy consumption, rare earth minerals for storage infrastructure, or digital colonialism in hosting decisions. A complete framework would need material governance, not just informational governance.

### 12.3 Expert Input Needed

- Indigenous data sovereignty practitioners (to validate Heritage/Living/Mixed classification adequacy)
- Information retrieval and distributed systems designers (provenance registry scalability)
- International IP lawyers (Knowledge Stewardship License legal standing)
- Library science and archival professionals (curation standards, governed forgetting precedents)
- Community memory practitioners — oral historians, ritual custodians (to ground classifications in real practice)
- Cognitive pluralism researchers (Weave Forum process design)
- GGF legal working group (governed forgetting's relationship to existing right-to-be-forgotten laws)

| **Cultural Heritage Preservation** | Uses Digital Commons archives as primary infrastructure for intangible heritage. Cultural Stewardship Teams interact with Knowledge Stewards at the BAZ level. |
| **Religious & Spiritual Dialogue** | The Wisdom Repository is hosted on Digital Commons infrastructure. Sacred Data Protocol governs access to sacred knowledge within the commons. |
| **Education Framework** | Primary demand-side partner. Educational curricula draw from the commons; educational output feeds back into it. Knowledge literacy is a prerequisite for meaningful commons participation. |
| **MOS** | Cognitive Pluralism → epistemic governance design. Conscientious Non-Participation → opt-out protections. Institutional Integrity Protocols → anti-capture mechanisms for DCC. Perceptual Integrity → validates protective withholding as sometimes legitimate. |
| **Conduit Protocol** | Inherits v2.2's infrastructure content (mesh networks, cybersecurity, backup nodes). Provides the physical and digital infrastructure on which the knowledge commons runs. |

## Appendix B: Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Heritage Knowledge** | Knowledge frozen in the form authorized by its originating stewards. Modification requires FPIC. |
| **Living Knowledge** | Knowledge open to evolution through forking, with full provenance tracking. |
| **Fork** | A divergent version of a Living Knowledge artifact, inheriting the parent's provenance chain. |
| **Weave** | A synthesis or mapping document produced by a Weave Forum, linking competing knowledge claims. |
| **Anchor** | A stable knowledge reference designated for governance purposes, with mandatory sunset clause. |
| **Unverified Echo** | A derivative that claims to represent Heritage Knowledge without steward authorization. Allowed but flagged; excluded from governance use. |
| **Knowledge Steward** | Trained BAZ-level role for knowledge curation, classification, and commons maintenance. |
| **Provenance Chain** | Immutable record of a knowledge artifact's creation, modification, forking, and classification history. |
| **Weave Forum** | Structured deliberation process for mapping contested knowledge without forcing false consensus. |
| **Commons Health Report** | Annual DCC assessment of knowledge ecosystem vitality. |
| **Maintenance-to-Creation Ratio** | Metric tracking resource allocation between maintaining existing and producing new knowledge. |
| **Knowledge Stewardship License** | Four-tier licensing framework (Open, Contextual, Community-Gated, Heritage/Sacred) governing knowledge artifact access and use. |
