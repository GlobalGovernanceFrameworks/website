# Preamble

We, the Parties to this Treaty,

recognizing that climate disruption, biodiversity loss, pandemics, conflict, financial contagion, technological risk, displacement, inequality, and information disorder interact across borders and institutions;

acknowledging that no state, institution, model, technology, knowledge system, or social group possesses complete understanding or sufficient authority to govern the whole;

affirming the dignity and fundamental rights of every person, the equal standing of peoples, the inherent sovereignty of Indigenous peoples, the rights and responsibilities of present and future generations, and the need to protect the living systems upon which all communities depend;

recognizing that planetary interdependence creates legitimate duties beyond borders while not erasing local autonomy, cultural difference, democratic self-government, or Indigenous law;

resolved to replace unmanaged competition and fragmented response with bounded, polycentric, lawful, and accountable cooperation;

committed to preserving uncertainty, disagreement, dissent, local interpretation, protected knowledge, and the right to refuse participation where authority has not been validly constituted;

determined that crisis response, enforcement, institutional reform, and economic transition must protect material continuity, essential services, due process, and the rights of people not responsible for the alleged harm;

agree to the following Treaty for Our Only Home.

# Article 1 — Purpose, status, and legal character

## 1.1 Purpose

The purpose of this Treaty is to establish a legitimate constitutional basis for cooperation on conditions that are genuinely shared across jurisdictions, including:

- planetary and transboundary ecological harms;
- pandemics and other cross-border health emergencies;
- international crimes and severe rights violations within valid jurisdiction;
- global public goods and shared infrastructure;
- cross-border financial, technological, and security risks;
- climate adaptation, displacement, and reparative obligations;
- institutional coordination where isolated action is demonstrably insufficient.

The Treaty seeks to move from a system that repeatedly generates and externalizes crisis toward one capable of prevention, resilience, restoration, and peaceful adaptation.

## 1.2 Constitutional draft status

Version 1.3 is a design draft. It does not itself create public authority, amend the United Nations Charter, bind any state or people, establish criminal jurisdiction, levy a tax, or authorize deployment.

Such powers arise only after valid ratification, domestic or international legal implementation where required, and satisfaction of the activation conditions in this Treaty.

## 1.3 No implied authority

The moral urgency of a planetary condition does not itself prove that a proposed institution has jurisdiction over it.

No body may infer authority from:

- expertise alone;
- superior data or modelling capacity;
- a declaration that existing institutions have failed;
- participation in the Global Stewardship Alliance;
- funding or technical dependence;
- an emergency alert generated by an algorithm;
- the absence or refusal of affected communities.

## 1.4 Relationship to other law

The Treaty shall be interpreted consistently with applicable international law, fundamental rights, valid Indigenous law and treaty relationships, and the constitutional requirements of each Party.

Where a conflict exists, the responsible institution shall publish the conflict, identify the claimed sources of authority, preserve rights and essential continuity, and seek adjudication rather than silently selecting the interpretation that expands its own power.

# Article 2 — Definitions

For this Treaty:

- **Affected authority** means a public, Indigenous, commons, or other institution possessing legitimate standing in relation to the territory, people, right, resource, or practice affected.
- **Assembly of Parties** means the state-party chamber established by Article 8.
- **Bounded coordination** means coordination under partial knowledge and limited scope authority, without a claim to system-wide visibility or control.
- **Class I–VI decision** means a decision classified under Article 6.
- **Commons institution** means a lawfully constituted body with fiduciary responsibilities for an ecological or other shared commons.
- **Constitutional measure** means a Treaty act altering institutional powers, rights, decision procedures, competences, or amendment rules.
- **Emergency** means the time-bounded condition defined in Article 17.
- **Indigenous people or nation** means a people that identifies and governs itself through its own laws, authorities, and processes; the Treaty does not determine Indigenous identity or representation.
- **Local sensemaking** means the required process through which affected local and Indigenous authorities interpret, adapt, contest, or refuse the local application of a measure within their lawful authority.
- **Material cross-boundary externality** means a significant harm or risk imposed outside the originating jurisdiction that cannot be adequately addressed through the originating jurisdiction acting alone.
- **Observation Contract** means the public record required by Article 12 for a consequential decision.
- **Party** means a state for which this Treaty is in force.
- **Peoples’ Chamber** means the representative and deliberative chamber established by Article 9.
- **Protected continuity** means preservation of fundamental rights, essential services, personal balances, lawful compensation, access to remedy, and other protected interests during institutional or emergency change.
- **Protected non-transfer** means a lawful interface outcome in which knowledge, data, deliberation, or meaning remains undisclosed while an authorization, refusal, condition, or bounded attestation may be communicated.
- **Shared planetary commons** means ecological or material systems beyond or across national jurisdiction whose integrity is materially affected by collective action, including the atmosphere, oceans, polar systems, orbital environment, and globally significant biodiversity systems as defined by applicable law and protocols.
- **State Estimate Record** means the plural causal and evidentiary assessment required by Article 12.
- **Treaty institution** means a body created under this Treaty and possessing only the authority expressly assigned to it.

# Article 3 — Parties, accession, participation, and non-member status

## 3.1 State Parties

The Treaty is open for signature and ratification by states through their valid constitutional procedures.

Ratification delegates only the competences expressly stated in the Treaty. General political support does not constitute an unlimited delegation.

## 3.2 Indigenous peoples and nations

Indigenous sovereignty is not created, granted, absorbed, or exhausted by this Treaty.

Indigenous peoples and nations may, through authorities and procedures they determine:

- enter nation-to-nation agreements with Parties or Treaty institutions;
- participate in selected Treaty processes;
- recognize or decline specific interfaces;
- create their own coordination bodies;
- maintain complete non-participation;
- withdraw from a compact under its agreed terms.

No universal Indigenous chamber, Earth Council, selection procedure, representation percentage, or proxy authority is created by this Treaty.

## 3.3 Cities, regions, BAZs, and public networks

Cities, regions, Bioregional Autonomous Zones, and other lawful public authorities may become **Associate Public Participants** through compacts authorized under applicable law.

Associate Public Participants may:

- join pilots;
- submit proposals and evidence;
- participate in deliberative bodies;
- enter cross-boundary commons and public-goods compacts;
- receive or administer authorized funds.

They do not acquire state-party voting rights or authority beyond the law and compact under which they participate.

## 3.4 Civil society, science, labour, business, and spiritual traditions

Civil-society organizations, research institutions, labour and professional bodies, businesses, faith and humanist communities, and other non-state actors may receive defined consultative, deliberative, monitoring, or implementation standing.

Corporate participation is not a source of public sovereignty. Financial contribution does not purchase legislative, adjudicative, or veto authority.

## 3.5 Non-members

Non-Parties are not bound by Treaty legislation, taxation, administrative standards, or institutional decisions merely because a condition has global significance.

The Treaty may affect non-Parties only through:

- obligations recognized under otherwise applicable international law;
- jurisdiction validly accepted by the non-Party;
- direct regulation of persons, transactions, assets, or conduct within Party jurisdiction;
- lawful conditions on access to Treaty benefits, procurement, funds, or voluntary trade arrangements;
- narrowly established duties concerning direct transboundary harm.

Non-members retain notice, evidence, response, and adjudication rights where Treaty action materially affects them.

## 3.6 Reservations

A Party may enter a reservation unless the reservation:

- defeats the Treaty’s object and purpose;
- removes fundamental rights or due-process protections;
- claims authority over Indigenous peoples without valid consent or law;
- shifts prohibited harm onto other Parties or shared commons;
- eliminates financial obligations while retaining the corresponding benefits.

Reservations and objections shall be public and periodically reviewed.

## 3.7 Withdrawal

A Party may withdraw by written notice. Withdrawal takes effect after twelve months unless a longer period is agreed.

Withdrawal does not extinguish:

- liabilities for prior violations;
- outstanding judicial orders;
- lawful restitution or reparation duties;
- obligations concerning protected data or knowledge;
- accrued financial commitments;
- emergency responsibilities already activated for a defined operation;
- duties to prevent immediate transboundary harm.

Withdrawal shall include an institutional and material continuity plan.

# Article 4 — Constitutional principles

Treaty institutions and Parties acting under the Treaty shall observe the following principles.

## 4.1 Subsidiarity and polycentrism

Authority remains at the lowest capable and legitimate level unless a material externality, shared commons, fundamental right, or validly delegated common function requires broader action.

Higher scale is not presumed superior. Lower scale is not presumed harmless.

## 4.2 Democratic legitimacy and inclusion

Public authority shall be traceable to ratification, democratic mandate, lawful delegation, treaty consent, adjudicative jurisdiction, or another recognized constituting process.

Participation systems shall address structural exclusion affecting the Global South, Indigenous peoples, women, youth, persons with disabilities, displaced people, marginalized minorities, and people excluded by digital or administrative systems.

Inclusion inside a Treaty body does not substitute for affected-party consent or sovereign authority.

## 4.3 Accountability and rule of law

Powers shall be defined, reviewable, challengeable, and subject to remedy. No institution is exempt from the rules it applies to others.

## 4.4 Ecological integrity and intergenerational responsibility

Treaty action shall protect the regenerative capacity of living systems and consider long-term and irreversible effects.

Future-generations standing does not authorize present institutions to invent the preferences of future people without transparent assumptions and plural challenge.

## 4.5 Epistemic plurality

No single model, institution, AI system, scientific discipline, culture, or knowledge tradition shall monopolize interpretation of a consequential planetary decision.

Disagreement shall be preserved as information where it cannot honestly be resolved.

## 4.6 Bounded coordination

No Treaty institution shall be designed to achieve total visibility or control over the governance system.

Coordination bodies may connect jurisdictions; they do not thereby become sovereign over them.

## 4.7 Local sensemaking

Global measures shall be interpreted through affected local contexts before durable implementation, subject to the decision classes and rights in this Treaty.

Local sensemaking may result in adaptation, implementation sequencing, a request for support, lawful refusal, or a jurisdictional challenge. It is not merely a reporting ritual.

## 4.8 Minimum necessary authority

Every exercise of Treaty power shall use the least extensive authority reasonably capable of addressing the valid shared condition.

## 4.9 Protected continuity

Emergency, sanction, veto, institutional succession, or withdrawal shall not casually destroy material security, essential services, personal rights, protected knowledge, or access to remedy.

## 4.10 Reversible containment before irreversible consequence

Where evidence supports immediate concern but not a complete diagnosis, institutions shall prefer temporary, narrow, reversible containment followed by investigation and adjudication.

# Article 5 — Competence and reserved powers

## 5.1 Conferred competences

Treaty institutions may act only in competences expressly conferred by this Treaty or a protocol adopted under it.

Conferred competences may include:

- shared planetary commons;
- transboundary ecological harm;
- pandemic and biosafety coordination;
- defined international crimes and rights protection;
- global public goods;
- cross-border cyber and technological security;
- Treaty finance and administration;
- adjudication of Treaty obligations;
- voluntary common standards and mutual recognition;
- emergency coordination within Article 17.

## 5.2 Reserved powers

Unless expressly delegated, the Treaty does not control:

- ordinary local land use and public services;
- domestic social, cultural, educational, labour, or economic policy;
- Indigenous internal governance, law, identity, ceremony, or knowledge;
- AUBI eligibility or personal entitlement;
- Hearts or Leaves issuance and settlement outside Financial Systems authority;
- professional standards outside lawful labour and practice institutions;
- private, family, spiritual, or relational life;
- constitutional powers reserved by a Party.

## 5.3 Necessary and proper measures

A measure incidental to a conferred competence is valid only where it is:

- necessary to exercise that competence;
- proportionate;
- unable to be achieved through a materially less intrusive means;
- time-bounded where appropriate;
- reviewable;
- consistent with rights and Indigenous obligations.

## 5.4 No competence through institutional failure

A Treaty or Meta-Governance institution does not acquire another institution’s competence because it considers that institution slow, captured, unobservable, or ineffective.

Authority transfer requires the process in Article 22.

# Article 6 — Decision classes and jurisdiction

Every consequential measure shall be assigned a decision class before authorization.

## 6.1 Class I — Primarily local matters

A matter whose material effects remain substantially within one lawful jurisdiction is governed locally.

Treaty institutions may offer resources, standards, or technical support but may not compel a uniform solution absent another valid source of jurisdiction.

## 6.2 Class II — Voluntary cross-boundary coordination

Two or more authorities may create a compact for shared infrastructure, trade, mutual aid, research, standards, or services.

Authority is limited to the compact. Exit, amendment, cost allocation, data use, dispute resolution, and continuity shall be specified.

## 6.3 Class III — Material cross-boundary externalities

Broader action may be justified where an originating jurisdiction causes or credibly threatens serious harm outside its territory and voluntary resolution has failed or is inadequate.

The action shall target the externality, not use it as a pretext for general governance of the originating jurisdiction.

## 6.4 Class IV — Shared planetary commons

The Treaty may coordinate stewardship of shared planetary commons within Party jurisdiction and applicable international law.

The existence of a commons does not eliminate differentiated responsibility, affected-community standing, Indigenous rights, or the need to define the object and limits of stewardship.

## 6.5 Class V — Fundamental rights and international crimes

Treaty institutions may act only where the relevant right, crime, jurisdiction, procedure, and remedy have been validly established.

Political bodies may refer or investigate under law. They may not determine guilt.

## 6.6 Class VI — Indigenous territory, knowledge, culture, and sacred relations

Where a decision affects Indigenous territory, rights, knowledge, community data, cultural continuity, or sacred relations, the Indigenous interfaces in Article 11 apply.

Global or pan-Indigenous participation does not substitute for the affected people or nation.

## 6.7 Emergency overlay

Emergency status may shorten procedure but does not change the underlying decision class or create permanent jurisdiction.

# Article 7 — Subsidiarity, capability, and local sensemaking

## 7.1 Subsidiarity record

Before moving authority upward or sideways, the proposing institution shall state:

1. the condition to be addressed;
2. the decision class;
3. the authorities currently possessing jurisdiction;
4. the cross-boundary harm or shared obligation;
5. why the existing scale cannot adequately act;
6. whether capacity support could resolve the problem;
7. the minimum additional authority requested;
8. duration and review;
9. protected local discretion;
10. challenge, return, and withdrawal conditions.

## 7.2 Capacity-first rule

Insufficient administrative, financial, scientific, or technical capacity shall not automatically justify removal of authority.

Before centralization, institutions shall consider:

- grants;
- shared services;
- technical assistance;
- mutual aid;
- temporary delegated administration;
- slower implementation;
- federated coordination;
- independent facilitation.

## 7.3 Failure-to-act standard

A broader authority may intervene for failure to act only where:

- the duty to act is legally established;
- the harm is material and within valid jurisdiction;
- the lower authority received notice and a practical opportunity to respond;
- capacity support was considered;
- the intervention is narrow and time-bounded;
- adjudication is available.

## 7.4 Local Sensemaking Record

Before durable local implementation, the affected authority shall have a meaningful opportunity to state:

- local conditions and affected populations;
- relevant law and governance;
- translation and cultural issues;
- likely distributional and ecological effects;
- proposed adaptation;
- requested resources;
- refusal, consent, or jurisdictional objection where applicable;
- unresolved disagreement.

## 7.5 Limits on adaptation

Local adaptation may not remove fundamental rights, export serious harm, falsify reporting, or claim consent from another people.

Conversely, global uniformity may not be imposed merely for administrative convenience, comparability, or dashboard simplicity.

# Article 8 — Assembly of Parties

## 8.1 Composition

Each Party appoints or elects its delegation under domestic constitutional law and holds one vote in the Assembly of Parties.

Delegations shall disclose relevant conflicts, funding interests, and instructions.

## 8.2 Functions

The Assembly may:

- adopt Treaty measures within conferred competence;
- approve budgets and assessed contributions;
- authorize protocols and institutions;
- elect or confirm officers as specified;
- refer matters for adjudication;
- authorize emergency continuation under Article 17;
- propose amendments;
- review implementation and reservations.

## 8.3 Ordinary decision rule

The Assembly shall seek consensus. Where consensus is unavailable, an ordinary binding measure requires two-thirds of Parties present and voting, subject to any required approval by the Peoples’ Chamber.

## 8.4 Enhanced decisions

The following require at least three-quarters of Parties present and voting and the corresponding Peoples’ Chamber threshold:

- constitutional amendments;
- creation of a new coercive power;
- armed deployment;
- permanent institutional succession;
- suspension of a Party’s voting rights;
- a new class of compulsory levy.

## 8.5 Small-state and affected-party protection

Population size does not eliminate a Party’s formal equality. Where a measure imposes concentrated burdens on a small number of Parties, the Assembly shall publish a burden and compensation assessment and hear the directly affected Parties before voting.

# Article 9 — Peoples’ Chamber and civic participation

## 9.1 Purpose

The Peoples’ Chamber supplies a democratic check not reducible to interstate voting.

It is the Treaty’s transitional pathway toward the United Nations Parliamentary Assembly proposed in v1.2, without presuming that the United Nations has already adopted such a chamber.

## 9.2 Transitional composition

During the initial period, members may be selected through a mixed system of:

- delegates from national and regional parliaments;
- directly elected members where participating jurisdictions authorize elections;
- stratified civic sortition for a bounded portion of seats;
- accessible representation arrangements for marginalized and displaced populations.

The composition shall be periodically reviewed with the aim of greater direct democratic legitimacy while preserving accessibility and geographic plurality.

## 9.3 Powers

The Peoples’ Chamber may:

- approve or reject measures requiring dual authorization;
- initiate legislative proposals;
- conduct public hearings;
- require institutional responses;
- approve the Treaty budget;
- initiate review by the Office of the Adversary, Ombudsperson, or Tribunal;
- participate in amendment and emergency decisions.

## 9.4 Voting

Ordinary dual-authority measures require a majority of votes cast in the Peoples’ Chamber. Enhanced decisions require two-thirds.

Population representation shall be degressively proportional and shall not permit a small number of highly populated states to control the chamber alone.

## 9.5 Global Citizens’ Initiative

A verified initiative supported by at least ten million persons across at least fifty jurisdictions, including a distribution threshold preventing concentration in a few states, shall require:

- formal placement on the public agenda;
- an institutional response;
- a public hearing;
- a decision whether to initiate ordinary legislation or an advisory global vote.

An initiative does not bypass constitutional competence, rights, or Indigenous consent.

## 9.6 Accessible and multi-modal deliberation

Treaty deliberation shall support synchronous, asynchronous, written, oral, visual, signed, translated, low-bandwidth, and accessible participation. Digital participation shall not be the sole route.

# Article 10 — Advisory, integrity, and coordinating institutions

## 10.1 Global Council for the Future

The Global Council for the Future is an advisory foresight body representing relevant scientific, civic, city, labour, youth, ecological, and other long-term perspectives.

It may:

- publish threat and opportunity assessments;
- propose long-term measures;
- request reconsideration;
- initiate a future-generations review;
- recommend an emergency convening.

It does not independently legislate, deploy, tax, sanction, or determine Indigenous standing.

## 10.2 Council of Guardians

The Council of Guardians consists of nine to twelve members serving one non-renewable term of up to fifteen years under an open nomination, conflict screening, public hearing, and dual-chamber confirmation process.

Its functions are:

- annual Principle Impact Reviews;
- intergenerational and constitutional scrutiny;
- the right, by supermajority, to require one reconsideration of a measure posing a credible threat to planetary survival, fundamental rights, or the Treaty’s constitutional integrity.

The Council has no executive power and cannot permanently veto legislation.

## 10.3 Office of the Adversary

The Office is independent and may:

- red-team proposals and operations;
- inspect assumptions, uncertainty, incentives, and distributional effects;
- publish dissent alongside major decisions;
- request information subject to protected knowledge and security rules;
- seek interim judicial relief;
- protect internal dissenters and whistleblowers.

It does not authorize the measure it audits.

## 10.4 Global Equity Ombudsperson

The Ombudsperson investigates structural exclusion, unequal burdens, procedural inaccessibility, retaliation, and failures of remedy.

The Ombudsperson may recommend correction, initiate review, and support affected claimants but does not determine criminal guilt.

## 10.5 Meta-Governance Coordination Council

The Council coordinates interfaces among domain institutions. It may:

- map interdependence;
- convene legitimate authorities;
- facilitate compact formation;
- maintain interface standards;
- support plural observation;
- publish advisory coordination audits;
- incubate authorized pilots.

It possesses no inherent power to legislate, tax, sanction, dissolve, transfer functions, or create a successor authority.

## 10.6 Global Arbitration and Mediation Panel

The Panel provides voluntary or treaty-mandated mediation for jurisdictional and institutional disputes before adjudication where appropriate.

Mediation does not replace the affected authority’s consent or a Tribunal’s jurisdiction.

## 10.7 Institutional independence

The same appointment authority shall not control all integrity bodies. Budgets, appointments, removals, and information access shall be distributed to reduce correlated capture.

# Article 11 — Indigenous sovereignty and Treaty interfaces

## 11.1 Recognition, not creation

The Treaty recognizes inherent Indigenous sovereignty. It does not create, appoint, rank, certify, or consolidate Indigenous authorities.

Any Indigenous coordination body engaging with the Treaty exists through Indigenous-determined processes and may limit its scope or decline participation.

## 11.2 Affected-nation standing

Where a measure affects Indigenous territory, treaty rights, land or water relations, cultural continuity, community data, Traditional Knowledge, or sacred relations, standing belongs first to the affected people or peoples through authorities they identify.

Representation of Indigenous persons in a Treaty body does not replace this standing.

## 11.3 Consent and FPIC

Free, prior, and informed consent is required where applicable under international law, Indigenous law, a treaty relationship, or an Indigenous–Treaty compact.

The Consent Record shall specify:

- the people and authority;
- territory and subject;
- permitted and prohibited actions;
- knowledge and data boundaries;
- duration;
- compensation, benefit sharing, or restitution;
- amendment and withdrawal;
- continuity obligations;
- unresolved internal plurality.

Consent in one domain does not imply consent in another.

## 11.4 Internal plurality

When Indigenous standing is contested, Treaty institutions shall not select the speaker most convenient to the proposed action.

They shall disclose the contest, pause irreversible measures where possible, support an Indigenous-determined process, preserve individual and minority rights, and record unresolved disagreement.

## 11.5 Protected knowledge

No Treaty duty of transparency, open science, institutional memory, or adversarial audit compels disclosure of sacred, restricted, community-controlled, or intentionally unrecorded knowledge.

A community may provide a bounded attestation, condition, refusal, or authorization without disclosing its basis.

## 11.6 Treaty of the Circle and other covenants

A Party, BAZ, commons institution, or Treaty body may enter a covenant with affected Indigenous authorities concerning territorial use, ecological limits, restitution, shared stewardship, or economic authorization.

A covenant may authorize an injunction, suspension, remediation, charter review, or eventual revocation of delegated authority. It shall not automatically invalidate personal balances, AUBI Layer 1, wages, essential services, or unrelated third-party rights.

## 11.7 Non-participation

Non-participation by an Indigenous people shall not be interpreted as consent, lack of capacity, hostility to science, or authorization for a proxy institution.

# Article 12 — Decision observability and epistemic governance

## 12.1 Consequential decisions

A decision is consequential when it may materially affect rights, territory, public finance, sanctions, deployment, institutional authority, shared commons, Indigenous interests, protected knowledge, or long-term ecological conditions.

Every consequential decision shall have an Observation Contract and State Estimate Record proportionate to urgency and impact.

## 12.2 Observation Contract

The Observation Contract shall state:

1. decision and legal authority;
2. decision class;
3. observations and sources;
4. groups and territories observed;
5. missing and unseen populations;
6. update frequency and latency;
7. source dependencies;
8. estimator or interpretive method;
9. uncertainty and alternative explanations;
10. alert thresholds;
11. actions an indicator may and may not authorize;
12. privacy, sovereignty, and knowledge restrictions;
13. challenge, review, and correction;
14. independent outcomes;
15. sunset and reassessment.

## 12.3 State Estimate Record

The Record shall present:

- the condition as currently understood;
- materially plausible causal explanations;
- supporting and conflicting evidence;
- uncertainty and partial identification;
- affected authorities’ accounts;
- distributional effects;
- immediate containment if any;
- the selected action and alternatives;
- expected independent outcomes;
- reversal and escalation conditions.

## 12.4 Epistemic Plurality Protocol

For constitutional, Class IV, major Class III, major Class V, and full emergency decisions, the authorizing body shall seek at least three materially independent modelling or interpretive systems where feasible.

Independence concerns data, assumptions, institutions, incentives, and methods—not merely different software implementations of the same model.

The systems may include scientific, economic, legal, social, ecological, local, and Indigenous-governed evidence, subject to consent and protected non-transfer.

Where three systems are not feasible, the authority shall explain the limitation and strengthen adversarial and local review.

## 12.5 Indicators are alerts, not judgments

A threshold, ranking, model output, trust score, resilience index, cultural-alignment score, or AI prediction may trigger attention or review. It does not by itself establish:

- guilt;
- capture;
- consent;
- jurisdiction;
- cultural legitimacy;
- a planetary emergency;
- institutional failure;
- the correct intervention.

## 12.6 Source independence

Where several observation channels share the same platform, observer, funder, model, or governance authority, the dependence shall be visible in an Observation Independence Map.

## 12.7 Public reasons and protected limits

Institutional reasons, legal authority, public finance, model assumptions, votes, and implementation outcomes shall be public by default.

Personal, security-sensitive, Indigenous-restricted, and other protected information shall be minimized and purpose-limited.

# Article 13 — Legislative and policy procedure

## 13.1 Proposal

A proposal shall identify:

- the competence and decision class;
- the problem and evidence;
- the authorities and populations affected;
- costs and funding;
- rights and Indigenous interfaces;
- local implementation space;
- implementation and enforcement body;
- outcomes, review, and sunset.

## 13.2 Public and local review

Except in provisional emergency action, proposals shall undergo public notice, accessible deliberation, local sensemaking, and affected-authority review for a period proportionate to impact.

## 13.3 Cross-pillar impact assessment

A measure shall assess significant effects across:

- ecology;
- rights and equity;
- peace and security;
- public finance;
- work and livelihoods;
- technology and data;
- Indigenous sovereignty;
- institutional capacity;
- future generations.

The assessment shall preserve conflicts rather than collapse them into one score.

## 13.4 Dual authorization

A binding measure of general application requires approval by the Assembly of Parties and Peoples’ Chamber unless this Treaty expressly provides otherwise.

## 13.5 Reconsideration and review

The Council of Guardians, Office of the Adversary, Ombudsperson, an affected Indigenous authority, a specified minority of Parties, or a qualifying civic initiative may trigger reconsideration or judicial review under the applicable rules.

## 13.6 Pilots

Novel mechanisms should begin as bounded pilots where the risk of irreversible harm is low enough to permit experimentation.

Every pilot shall define:

- consent and legal authority;
- scope;
- comparison or baseline;
- rights and protected minimums;
- failure and stop conditions;
- data and knowledge boundaries;
- independent evaluation;
- exit and continuity;
- whether scaling requires new authorization.

# Article 14 — Planetary commons, ecology, and future generations

## 14.1 Charter for Planetary Rights and Responsibilities

The Parties shall negotiate protocols defining:

- rights of nature or ecosystem legal standing where adopted;
- planetary-boundary duties;
- responsibilities of states and corporations;
- differentiated responsibilities and capabilities;
- future-generations standing;
- affected-community and Indigenous authority;
- remedies, restoration, and restitution.

No numerical planetary boundary becomes legally self-executing without an adopted protocol specifying evidence, jurisdiction, uncertainty, and procedure.

## 14.2 Cross-boundary Commons Trusts

Parties and affected authorities may establish Commons Trusts for watersheds, forests, aquifers, migratory species, coastal systems, and other cross-boundary commons.

A Trust charter shall define:

- the commons and territory;
- fiduciary duty;
- affected authorities;
- guardian and ecological expertise;
- Indigenous relationships;
- decision powers;
- veto or injunction scope;
- funding;
- evidence and review;
- appeal;
- exit and succession.

## 14.3 Guardian authority

Guardians may represent the integrity of an ecosystem within a valid charter. Their default powers are to observe, warn, propose conditions, request precautionary containment, and seek an injunction.

A binding veto must be expressly authorized, limited to the relevant commons harm, reasoned, reviewable, and subject to protected continuity.

## 14.4 Precaution

Where a credible threat of serious or irreversible ecological harm exists, scientific uncertainty does not require inaction.

Precaution shall not eliminate the duty to state uncertainty, consider livelihood and distributional effects, seek less harmful alternatives, or reassess as evidence changes.

## 14.5 Global Commons Authority proposal

The v1.2 proposal for a Global Commons Authority is retained as a possible future institution, not activated by v1.3.

Creation requires a separate protocol defining its commons, parties, powers, relation to existing law, Indigenous interface, finance, adjudication, and limits.

## 14.6 Space and orbital commons

The Parties shall pursue updated rules for orbital debris, spectrum and orbital use, celestial resource activity, scientific access, and equitable benefit sharing.

A temporary moratorium on specified extraction activities may be adopted through the ordinary Treaty process where evidence and equity require it.

---

# Article 15 — International law, adjudication, and accountability

## 15.1 Judicial institutions

The Treaty supports reform and strengthening of competent international courts and may establish a Digital Justice Tribunal or other specialized adjudicative bodies through protocol.

No court acquires jurisdiction solely by being named in this draft.

## 15.2 International crimes and ecocide

The Parties may adopt a protocol defining ecocide and other Treaty crimes, including:

- prohibited conduct;
- mental and material elements;
- individual and corporate liability;
- state responsibility;
- jurisdiction;
- evidence;
- defenses;
- penalties;
- restorative and ecological remedies;
- protections against selective or geopolitical misuse.

## 15.3 Independent oversight

Investigation, prosecution, adjudication, sanction implementation, and outcome review shall be institutionally separated.

## 15.4 Global Forensic Corps

A Forensic Corps may gather and preserve evidence under lawful authorization.

It shall maintain:

- chain of custody;
- source provenance;
- sensor and model reliability records;
- human review;
- uncertainty;
- affected-party rights;
- protected knowledge and privacy boundaries;
- judicial admissibility standards.

AI systems may assist triage and verification but cannot determine guilt or admissibility.

## 15.5 Whistleblowers and defenders

The Parties shall protect persons reporting corruption, grave rights violations, ecocide, cybercrime, or other Treaty violations.

Measures may include confidentiality, anti-retaliation remedies, legal support, relocation, and Planetary Guardian Visas under a separate asylum and protection protocol.

## 15.6 Fundamental rights

No Treaty objective, including ecological protection, public health, security, information integrity, or institutional adaptation, authorizes arbitrary detention, discrimination, torture, collective punishment, forced labour, or denial of due process.

---

# Article 16 — Compliance and enforcement

## 16.1 Graduated compliance

The normal compliance sequence is:

1. notice and disclosure;
2. affected-authority response;
3. technical or capacity support;
4. negotiated compliance plan;
5. mediation;
6. interim injunction where necessary;
7. adjudication;
8. proportionate sanction or remedy;
9. restoration and outcome review.

Urgency may change the order only as authorized by Article 17.

## 16.2 Sanctions

Sanctions shall be:

- legally authorized;
- based on adjudicated or otherwise valid findings;
- targeted;
- proportionate;
- reviewable;
- designed to protect uninvolved populations and essential services;
- accompanied by clear release and restoration conditions.

Automatic blacklists, generalized reputation penalties, and collective economic collapse are prohibited.

## 16.3 Treaty benefits

A Party may be temporarily suspended from specified discretionary benefits after due process. Suspension of benefits shall not extinguish individual rights, humanitarian support, or access to adjudication.

## 16.4 Specialized service corps

The Parties may establish separately chartered corps, including:

- a Health Emergency Corps;
- an Environmental Crimes Investigation Unit;
- a Cyber Defense and Recovery Service;
- a Humanitarian Stabilization and Reconstruction Service.

Each charter shall define command, jurisdiction, civilian control, consent, use-of-force rules, data handling, worker protections, financing, oversight, and sunset.

## 16.5 Deployment classes

- **Technical assistance and rescue** require the consent of the receiving authority except where immediate rescue of life is lawfully permitted.
- **Evidence gathering** requires judicial, treaty, or host-state authorization.
- **Defensive cyber assistance** requires a defined request or lawful collective-defense basis.
- **Unarmed stabilization** requires host or competent international authorization.
- **Armed deployment** requires the enhanced dual-majority procedure, a valid legal basis, necessity, proportionality, civilian protection, time limit, and independent review.

## 16.6 No AI deployment authority

An AI-detected threshold may automatically convene an emergency session or preserve evidence. It may not authorize deployment, classify a state as an aggressor, or determine that force is necessary.

---

# Article 17 — Emergency constitutionalism

## 17.1 Emergency definition

An emergency is a time-bounded condition presenting a credible and serious threat to life, fundamental rights, essential systems, international peace, or irreversible ecological integrity, where ordinary procedure cannot respond within the necessary time.

Political inconvenience, institutional resistance, declining trust, slow reform, or failure to meet a performance target is not an emergency.

## 17.2 Provisional action

A body expressly authorized by protocol or prior law may take narrow provisional action for no more than seventy-two hours where delay would cause serious harm.

The provisional record shall state:

- evidence and uncertainty;
- legal authority;
- decision class;
- geographic and subject scope;
- action;
- affected rights;
- protected minimums;
- why ordinary procedure was insufficient;
- review time.

## 17.3 Continuation

Continuation beyond seventy-two hours requires dual authorization or the emergency procedure specified by a ratified protocol.

The authorization shall state:

- powers activated and prohibited;
- duration;
- oversight;
- affected local and Indigenous interfaces;
- work-mobilisation rules;
- financial authority;
- termination and restoration.

An authorization shall not exceed thirty days without renewal. Cumulative renewal beyond ninety days requires extraordinary independent review and transition to ordinary law where the condition has become persistent.

## 17.4 Protected powers and interests

Emergency authority does not automatically permit:

- suspension of AUBI Layer 1;
- confiscation of personal balances;
- compulsory disclosure of sacred or protected knowledge;
- permanent territorial or constitutional transfer;
- indefinite detention or censorship;
- automatic guilt or blacklist findings;
- permanent institutional dissolution;
- forced work through Community Providers;
- irreversible extraction from Indigenous territory;
- waiver of non-derogable rights.

## 17.5 Local and Indigenous interface

Local sensemaking may be compressed only to the minimum required by urgency.

Every emergency shall maintain affected-area liaison, local evidence channels, Indigenous notification and authority where affected, accessible public reasons, and post-emergency restoration.

Immediate rescue of life without prior consent shall be narrow, non-extractive, temporary, and immediately reviewable. It creates no precedent for permanent use.

## 17.6 Emergency work

Mobilization of workers or Community Providers shall follow Work in Liberation’s Emergency Work Mobilisation Record, including voluntariness, safety, compensation, accommodation, withdrawal, and independent monitoring.

Compulsory service requires a separate valid constitutional basis and cannot be inferred from this Treaty.

## 17.7 Financial emergency

Emergency financial measures shall follow Financial Systems v3.4. Layer 1 and essential-provider continuity remain protected except under a separately authorized constitutional standard.

## 17.8 After-action duties

Within ninety days after termination, the responsible body shall publish:

- actions and evidence;
- rights affected;
- local and Indigenous accounts;
- outcomes and harms;
- financial flows;
- unresolved claims;
- restoration and restitution;
- proposed legal changes.

# Article 18 — Public finance and the Global Commons Fund

## 18.1 No self-authorizing global tax

This Treaty authorizes the Parties to negotiate and implement common revenue instruments. It does not levy a tax merely by naming one.

A compulsory levy requires:

- a conferred competence;
- dual authorization;
- implementation through applicable law;
- a defined base, rate, collection authority, use, exemptions, audit, and review;
- distributional and cross-border assessment.

## 18.2 Authorized revenue classes

Protocols may establish or coordinate:

- carbon and pollution charges;
- aviation and shipping levies;
- financial transaction levies;
- digital-services contributions;
- extractive-resource and commons-use charges;
- assessed Party contributions;
- voluntary contributions and bonds;
- recovery of penalties and restitution.

Exact rates in v1.2 remain policy proposals rather than constitutional rules.

## 18.3 Assessed contributions

The contribution formula may consider:

- income and fiscal capacity;
- wealth and inequality;
- historic and current emissions;
- resource consumption;
- benefits received from shared systems;
- crisis and development circumstances.

The formula shall be public, periodically reviewed, and challengeable.

## 18.4 Global Commons Fund

The Global Commons Fund finances authorized global public goods, ecological restoration, climate adaptation, health, crisis readiness, participation equity, and institutional operations.

Its governance shall separate:

- allocation proposals;
- authorization;
- Treasury custody;
- implementation;
- audit;
- outcome evaluation.

The board shall include Parties, public and civic representation, affected communities, and independent fiduciary members under conflict and rotation rules.

## 18.5 Currencies and settlement

The Fund may accept and disburse multiple lawful currencies and instruments. Hearts and Leaves may be used only under Financial Systems v3.4 and the relevant domain protocols.

No contribution, ecological outcome, or care activity is automatically convertible into debt service, market price, Hearts, or Leaves.

## 18.6 Commons dividends

A protocol may distribute part of revenue from authorized commons use directly or collectively to people and affected communities.

The protocol shall address ownership, stewardship, Indigenous rights, ecological limits, distribution, non-members, and future generations.

## 18.7 Financial transparency and privacy

Budgets, contracts, allocations, institutional beneficiaries, and audits shall be public. Personal and protected community information shall be minimized.

---

# Article 19 — Reparations, restitution, and debt justice

## 19.1 Reparations

The Parties recognize that colonial extraction, slavery, dispossession, environmental destruction, and unequal climate burdens may create duties of reparations and restitution.

A Reparations Protocol shall define:

- claimants and responsible actors;
- historical and legal basis;
- affected-community authority;
- forms of land, financial, institutional, cultural, and ecological repair;
- funding;
- non-repetition guarantees;
- adjudication;
- long-term outcome review.

## 19.2 Indigenous restitution

Reparations affecting Indigenous peoples shall be developed through affected-nation processes and shall not substitute global representation for territorial and people-specific standing.

## 19.3 Sovereign Debt Transformation

The sovereign-debt mechanism proposed in v1.2 is deferred.

No nation’s debt is converted into ecological restoration, care infrastructure, Hearts, Leaves, or planetary-health milestones without a dedicated protocol addressing:

- legal authority;
- debtor consent and democratic authorization;
- affected-community and Indigenous consent;
- creditor treatment;
- baseline and additionality;
- ecological permanence and leakage;
- labour and distributional effects;
- currency and settlement risk;
- verification and challenge;
- restructuring, default, and insolvency law.

## 19.4 Emergency debt relief

Parties may establish time-limited debt-service pauses, liquidity facilities, or restructuring procedures for pandemics, disasters, or other shocks through valid financial and legal protocols.

An alert does not automatically rewrite contractual or sovereign obligations.

---

# Article 20 — Knowledge, technology, and information integrity

## 20.1 Federated institutional memory

Treaty institutions shall maintain durable records of public authority, decisions, versions, evidence, dissent, financial flows, and outcomes.

Institutional memory shall be federated rather than placed by default in one comprehensive global database.

No blockchain or distributed ledger is constitutionally required.

## 20.2 Open-source boundary

Publicly funded modelling and software should be open, auditable, and reusable by default.

Exceptions may protect:

- personal data;
- Indigenous and community-controlled knowledge;
- sacred or restricted knowledge;
- active security operations;
- legitimate vulnerability disclosure;
- narrowly protected confidential information.

An exception shall not conceal legal authority, public spending, model purpose, or material rights effects.

## 20.3 Global Knowledge Stewardship Council proposal

A Knowledge Stewardship Council may be created by protocol to support open science, education, independent media, multilingual knowledge access, and research integrity.

It shall not become a ministry of truth or acquire general authority to determine acceptable belief.

## 20.4 Epistemic security

Measures against coordinated disinformation, deepfakes, cyber manipulation, and information attacks shall distinguish harmful conduct from lawful disagreement, journalism, scholarship, satire, political opposition, spiritual belief, and minority interpretation.

AI monitoring may generate alerts. Restriction, sanction, or content removal requires lawful authority, evidence, proportionality, and appeal.

## 20.5 Digital governance

A Global Digital Governance Body may be established only through a separate protocol defining:

- cybersecurity and AI competence;
- human-rights limits;
- community and Indigenous veto over intrusive local deployment where applicable;
- data sovereignty;
- audit and redress;
- relationship to national and technical standards bodies;
- no general surveillance mandate.

## 20.6 Civic learning

The Parties may support civic education in systems thinking, digital literacy, misinformation analysis, rights, ecological interdependence, and deliberation.

Curricula shall be locally adapted, plural, non-coercive, and open to challenge. A global competency framework shall not rank cultures or impose one developmental model.

---

# Article 21 — Institutional review, sunset, and amendment

## 21.1 Periodic review

Every Treaty institution and coercive protocol shall undergo independent review at least every five years, examining:

- mission and competence;
- rights and equity;
- capture and conflicts;
- costs and administrative burden;
- local and Indigenous effects;
- epistemic independence;
- outcomes and unintended harm;
- whether continuation, reform, federation, succession, or dissolution is appropriate.

## 21.2 Sunset clauses

Novel institutions and exceptional powers should have explicit sunset dates. Expiration does not automatically destroy essential services; a continuity plan is required.

## 21.3 Amendment

An amendment may be proposed by:

- one-third of Parties;
- the Peoples’ Chamber;
- a constitutional convention convened by dual majority;
- a qualifying Global Citizens’ Initiative followed by ordinary institutional sponsorship.

Adoption requires three-quarters of the Assembly of Parties and two-thirds of the Peoples’ Chamber, followed by ratification according to the amendment’s terms.

## 21.4 Entrenched protections

No amendment may abolish:

- fundamental human dignity and non-derogable rights;
- Indigenous sovereignty by Treaty fiat;
- due process and access to remedy;
- the limitation of Treaty institutions to conferred competence;
- the prohibition on automatic constitutional consequence from a metric or AI output;
- the right of a Party to withdraw subject to surviving obligations.

## 21.5 Constitutional convention

A comprehensive review shall be convened no later than fifteen years after entry into force and thereafter at intervals not exceeding twenty years.

The convention shall include Parties, the Peoples’ Chamber, affected public authorities, civil society, youth, disability representation, and Indigenous participation only through Indigenous-determined processes.

---

# Article 22 — Bypass, authority transfer, succession, and dissolution

## 22.1 Bounded experiments

Where a core institution cannot address a valid shared problem, Parties may authorize a temporary bypass or experimental institution.

Its charter shall define:

- problem and evidence;
- competence;
- participating jurisdictions;
- funding;
- rights and Indigenous interfaces;
- data and knowledge boundaries;
- relationship to the existing institution;
- duration;
- independent evaluation;
- coexistence, reintegration, succession, and dissolution options.

## 22.2 No presumption of reintegration

A successful bypass may:

- transfer learning to the original institution;
- federate with it;
- coexist where jurisdictions remain distinct;
- replace a function through lawful transfer;
- close when its mission ends.

Reintegration is not automatically superior to stable polycentric coexistence.

## 22.3 Authority transfer

Permanent transfer of a public function requires:

- valid legal and constitutional authority;
- notice and participation;
- an independent assessment;
- rights and distributional review;
- affected-authority and Indigenous interfaces;
- asset, staff, record, and service continuity;
- adjudication;
- the enhanced dual-majority procedure.

The auditing or coordinating institution may not authorize its own acquisition of the function.

## 22.4 Dissolution

Institutional failure, mission completion, capture alerts, or loss of trust may trigger review. They do not automatically dissolve an institution.

Dissolution requires an independent authorizing body, evidence, response, continuity plan, appeal, and lawful decision.

## 22.5 Protocol preservation

Useful public protocols and knowledge may be preserved after dissolution, subject to protected data and knowledge rules. Preservation does not preserve the dissolved institution’s authority.

---

# Article 23 — Transition, the United Nations, and the Global Stewardship Alliance

## 23.1 Voluntary coalition phase

Before entry into force, willing states, cities, Indigenous partners, civil-society organizations, and other actors may form a Global Stewardship Alliance to:

- develop the Treaty;
- conduct lawful pilots;
- coordinate voluntary public goods;
- propose reforms in existing institutions;
- build technical and deliberative capacity;
- demonstrate safeguards and outcomes.

Alliance participation creates no coercive authority over members or non-members beyond explicit compacts.

## 23.2 Relationship to the United Nations

The Treaty’s institutional proposals may be implemented through:

- amendment or reform of existing UN bodies;
- agreements between willing states;
- new treaty institutions operating alongside the UN;
- eventual functional integration where lawfully agreed.

The Treaty does not presume that the UNGA, UNSC, ICJ, ICC, IMF, World Bank, WTO, or WHO has already accepted the reforms proposed in v1.2.

## 23.3 UNSC and UNGA reform agenda

The Parties may jointly advocate:

- limits and overrides on veto use;
- broader regional representation;
- rapid but lawful emergency sessions;
- stronger democratic and parliamentary participation;
- reformed voting and development priorities in Bretton Woods institutions;
- restoration of effective trade adjudication with climate and labour safeguards.

These remain external reform objectives until adopted through the applicable institutions’ legal procedures.

## 23.4 Coexistence and non-predatory transition

Treaty institutions shall not manufacture failure or undermine essential legacy functions to accelerate succession.

They may demonstrate alternatives, support voluntary integration, publish comparative evidence, and negotiate lawful functional transfers.

## 23.5 Separate strategy documents

Campaign narratives, diplomatic bargaining, media strategy, pilot recruitment, institutional off-ramps, reform indexes, certifications, and geopolitical playbooks shall be maintained outside the constitutional Treaty text and remain subject to its rights and integrity rules.

---

# Article 24 — Entry into force and activation of powers

## 24.1 Entry into force

The Treaty enters into force ninety days after ratification by at least thirty states representing:

- at least five world regions; and
- at least twenty percent of the world population; and
- at least twenty percent of assessed global economic capacity under the provisional contribution formula.

These thresholds are design proposals for legal review and may be revised before signature.

## 24.2 Limited initial activation

Entry into force activates only:

- the Assembly of Parties;
- transitional Peoples’ Chamber;
- administrative Secretariat;
- Office of the Adversary;
- Global Equity Ombudsperson;
- voluntary coordination and funding functions;
- procedures for adopting protocols.

Coercive taxation, criminal jurisdiction, compulsory sanctions, armed deployment, and permanent authority transfer require separate protocols and their stated activation thresholds.

## 24.3 Regional and issue-specific protocols

A protocol may enter into force among a subset of Parties where:

- the Treaty permits differentiated integration;
- non-participants are not improperly bound;
- externalities and shared-commons effects are addressed;
- rights and continuity are protected.

## 24.4 Provisional application

A signatory may provisionally apply specified non-coercive provisions under its domestic law. Provisional application shall be public, time-bounded, and terminable.

## 24.5 Depositary and authentic texts

The final Treaty shall designate a depositary and authentic language versions. Translation differences shall be resolved through a public multilingual interpretation process that preserves local and legal meaning rather than silently privileging one language.

---

# Article 25 — Final governing rules

## 25.1 Constitutional allocation within the GGF

The GGF framework family does not form one total hierarchy. Authority is allocated by subject and legitimate source:

- this Treaty governs Treaty institutions and conferred planetary powers;
- the Constitutional Interface Specification governs shared authority and interface design;
- Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing v1.1 governs GGF obligations toward Indigenous sovereignty, subject to Indigenous-led revision;
- domain frameworks govern their defined subjects;
- Meta-Governance coordinates but does not override valid domain or sovereign authority.

Where a domain framework conflicts with this Treaty, the conflict shall be disclosed and resolved through amendment, protocol, or adjudication rather than silently harmonized.

## 25.2 Interpretation

The Treaty shall be interpreted in good faith according to its text, purpose, rights, plural legitimacy, subsidiarity, protected continuity, and the principle that no institution may turn its own observation into unchecked power.

## 25.3 Governing maxim

> **Coordinate globally where the condition is genuinely shared; preserve authority where knowledge, rights, and responsibility are local; recognize Indigenous sovereignty rather than administrating it; and never permit an observation system to convert its own diagnosis into unchecked power.**

# Conclusion

The Treaty for Our Only Home remains a response to the polycrisis, but v1.3 changes the meaning of response.

The answer to fragmented governance is not a planetary institution that presumes its own legitimacy, sees the whole system, and acts before it has distinguished urgency from jurisdiction. The answer is a constitutional structure capable of acting across borders while remaining explicit about who authorized it, whom it binds, what it knows, what it does not know, which powers it lacks, how it can be challenged, and when authority must return.

The Treaty therefore joins ambition to constraint:

- global coordination without global omniscience;
- enforceable law without automatic judgment;
- ecological protection without unbounded administration;
- crisis response without permanent emergency;
- institutional innovation without self-authorized succession;
- public knowledge without compulsory legibility;
- inclusion without manufactured consent;
- Indigenous recognition without Indigenous administration by the GGF;
- shared finance without automatic conversion of life into price;
- transition without sacrificing the people whom transition is meant to serve.

Its final test is not whether the Treaty can act everywhere. It is whether it can act where legitimate shared authority is necessary while preserving the plurality, dignity, knowledge, and autonomy that make a planetary civilization worth protecting.
