**Responsible:** Global Governance Frameworks initiative

# Preamble

Humanity requires coordination across territories, ecosystems, economies, cultures, generations, and domains of expertise. It also requires protection against the recurring failure in which coordination becomes a claim to superior knowledge, superior legitimacy, or permanent command.

This Specification defines the constitutional interfaces through which Global Governance Frameworks institutions may relate without collapsing their distinct sources of authority into one hierarchy.

It begins from five observations:

1. **Legitimacy is plural.** Indigenous peoples, persons, communities, democratic public authorities, treaty parties, rights-bearing ecosystems, professional communities, and shared-commons institutions do not derive all authority from one source.
2. **Interdependence is real.** Local autonomy cannot justify ungoverned harm to other peoples, territories, ecosystems, or shared commons.
3. **Observation is limited.** No indicator, model, dashboard, algorithm, council, or epistemic tradition possesses a complete view of the system it helps govern.
4. **Coordination is not sovereignty.** An institution that connects jurisdictions does not thereby acquire authority over them.
5. **Continuity matters.** Crisis action, veto, charter revocation, institutional transfer, or dissolution must stop harm without casually destroying rights, material security, essential services, or protected knowledge.

The Specification therefore establishes a polycentric constitutional order. Territorial councils govern place-based public functions. Commons institutions govern cross-boundary ecological relations. Practice and labour institutions govern professional and economic standards within their mandate. Indigenous peoples exercise inherent sovereignty through their own authorities and laws. Treaty institutions exercise only authority validly delegated or constituted under the Treaty. Meta-Governance coordinates interfaces but possesses no free-standing substantive jurisdiction.

The Polycentric Governance Architecture Framework contributes an important structural insight to this Specification: separation of powers must be horizontal as well as vertical. Territory, commons, economic practice, and Indigenous sovereignty create overlapping jurisdictions that can check one another. This Specification retains that topology while rejecting any claim that overlap makes capture impossible, that one GGF-designed Indigenous council can represent all Indigenous authority, or that automatic metrics, reputation systems, sortition juries, or economic sanctions may safely replace constitutional process.

# 1. Status, purpose, and scope

## 1.1 Purpose

This Specification supplies shared constitutional rules for:

- sources of legitimacy;
- jurisdiction and subsidiarity;
- Indigenous sovereignty and consent;
- polycentric separation of powers;
- cross-boundary commons;
- local adaptation, refusal, veto, and exit;
- observation and epistemic governance;
- automatic controls;
- deadlock and adjudication;
- emergency powers;
- institutional bypass, succession, and dissolution;
- protected material and informational continuity;
- interfaces among GGF frameworks.

## 1.2 Design status

This document is a **design specification**, not a self-executing treaty or source of public authority.

Within the GGF framework family, later documents should conform to it unless they explicitly identify and justify a constitutional divergence. Public institutions acquire authority only through valid law, treaty, democratic mandate, Indigenous law, compact, charter, or other legitimate constituting process.

## 1.3 Normative language

- **Must / shall** indicates a required design constraint.
- **Must not / shall not** indicates a prohibited design.
- **Should** indicates a strong presumption that may be departed from only with written justification.
- **May** indicates a permitted option.

## 1.4 Non-certification clause

This Specification can evaluate the internal coherence of GGF proposals concerning Indigenous sovereignty. It cannot certify that an institution, person, council, teaching, protocol, quotation, or interpretation is legitimate within any Indigenous people or nation.

Substantive legitimacy requires Indigenous-determined processes. The GGF may bind itself to obligations of non-interference, consent, restitution, support, and respect. It may not manufacture the authority from which it seeks legitimacy.

## 1.5 Scope boundaries

This Specification does not fully define:

- the substantive law of international crimes;
- the jurisdiction and procedure of the Digital Justice Tribunal;
- the scientific content of planetary boundaries;
- the internal law of any Indigenous people;
- Hearts, Leaves, reserves, or financial settlement;
- AUBI eligibility and payments;
- worker and provider standards;
- technical cybersecurity architecture.

Those belong to their domain frameworks. This Specification governs the interfaces and authority boundaries among them.

# 2. Constitutional commitments

All conforming GGF frameworks shall observe the following commitments.

## 2.1 Plural legitimacy

No single institution is presumed to originate all valid authority.

The constitutional order shall recognize distinct legitimacy sources, including:

- inherent Indigenous sovereignty;
- personal dignity and fundamental rights;
- democratic authorization;
- treaty consent;
- affected-community consent;
- delegated compact authority;
- fiduciary stewardship of shared commons;
- professional self-governance within rights and public-interest limits;
- lawful adjudicative authority.

Conflict among these sources requires a defined interface, not an assumed hierarchy.

## 2.2 Minimum necessary authority

A higher-scale, cross-boundary, or coordinating body may exercise only the least authority necessary to address a demonstrated:

- cross-boundary externality;
- shared-commons obligation;
- fundamental-rights violation;
- treaty duty;
- jurisdictionally valid crisis;
- coordination problem that lower-scale bodies cannot adequately resolve with support.

Authority shall be limited in subject, territory, duration, and remedy. It shall return when the justification ends.

## 2.3 Bounded coordination

No body shall be designed on the assumption that it can observe or optimize the whole constitutional system.

Every institution shall publish:

- its jurisdiction;
- known blind spots;
- dependencies;
- excluded matters;
- uncertainty;
- challenge routes;
- conditions for devolution or dissolution.

## 2.4 Non-creation of Indigenous sovereignty

Indigenous sovereignty is recognized, not delegated by the GGF.

The GGF shall not:

- decide who legitimately speaks for an Indigenous people;
- create a universal Indigenous council;
- define mandatory internal selection procedures;
- impose representation quotas as a substitute for affected-nation consent;
- compel disclosure of protected knowledge;
- make participation in GGF economic systems conditional on accepting a GGF-designed Indigenous institution.

## 2.5 Decision-chain separation

Consequential action shall distinguish:

1. observation;
2. interpretation;
3. proposal;
4. authorization;
5. implementation;
6. adjudication;
7. outcome evaluation;
8. reversal, restoration, or restitution.

No body should control the complete chain. A body that materially benefits from a diagnosis shall not be its sole interpreter or authorizer.

## 2.6 Indicators are not judgments

An institutional indicator, ecological threshold, crisis alert, model disagreement, public-trust measure, transaction pattern, variety-gap estimate, participation rate, or composite dashboard may justify attention or bounded containment.

It does not by itself establish:

- guilt;
- illegitimacy;
- capture;
- failure of sovereignty;
- authority transfer;
- sanction;
- dissolution;
- economic invalidation;
- suspension of rights.

## 2.7 Protected continuity

Every crisis intervention, veto, charter revocation, institutional succession, or dissolution shall protect, to the greatest extent compatible with stopping the harm:

- human rights;
- AUBI Layer 1;
- personal balances and valid claims;
- access to essential services;
- worker and participant rights;
- records needed for correction and appeal;
- Indigenous and community data control;
- ecological safeguards;
- restoration for erroneous action.

## 2.8 Protected non-transfer

Interoperability shall support an explicit outcome of **protected non-transfer**.

Knowledge, data, authority, or value may remain local, confidential, sacred, non-comparable, non-convertible, or available only through a bounded attestation. A right to audit institutional authority does not create a right to inspect all knowledge supporting that authority.

## 2.9 Refusal and non-participation

Individuals, communities, BAZs, states, and Indigenous peoples possess different forms of refusal and exit according to their legal and constitutional status.

The framework shall distinguish:

- refusal to participate;
- refusal to disclose;
- local adaptation;
- conscientious objection;
- jurisdictional objection;
- veto;
- withdrawal from a compact;
- withdrawal from a treaty;
- non-compliance with an unlawful act.

These shall not be collapsed into one category of resistance or system failure.

## 2.10 Reversible containment before irreversible consequence

Where immediate action is necessary under uncertainty, the default response shall be the narrowest reversible containment capable of preventing serious harm while preserving evidence and rights.

Permanent sanction, authority transfer, dissolution, or economic invalidation requires accountable authorization and review.

# 3. Constitutional actors and legitimacy sources

## 3.1 Persons

Persons possess dignity, rights, and claims that do not depend on institutional membership, economic contribution, citizenship, data visibility, or participation in GGF systems.

No institution may infer diminished standing from low participation, refusal, missing data, or non-use of a platform.

## 3.2 Indigenous peoples and nations

Indigenous peoples and nations possess inherent authority arising outside the GGF.

Their own processes determine:

- political representation;
- territorial authority;
- consent;
- knowledge stewardship;
- internal accountability;
- relations among nations, clans, communities, and authorities;
- whether and how to engage the GGF.

The GGF may recognize an Indigenous-convened Earth Council, confederation, council of nations, territorial authority, or other institution. It may not presume that one such body represents all Indigenous peoples or non-participating nations.

## 3.3 Democratic territorial authorities

Territorial councils, municipalities, BAZ assemblies, regional public bodies, states, and treaty organizations exercise place-based public authority only to the extent lawfully constituted.

Residence creates standing in territorial decisions but does not grant unilateral control over:

- cross-boundary commons;
- Indigenous territories or rights;
- professional self-governance;
- externalized harms;
- treaty obligations.

## 3.4 Commons institutions

Commons Trusts, watershed councils, ocean trusts, forest guardianships, atmospheric bodies, and equivalent institutions exercise fiduciary authority over defined ecological or shared systems.

Their legitimacy may derive from:

- affected jurisdictions;
- rights-of-nature law;
- treaty authority;
- Indigenous law and stewardship;
- public trust law;
- compact.

They shall state the commons governed, affected parties, Guardian roles, evidence methods, rights, limits, and appeal route.

## 3.5 Practice, labour, and professional institutions

Guilds, syndicates, Fractal Labor Parliament bodies, professional colleges, worker associations, and Community Provider governance bodies may set standards within their legitimate domain.

They shall not acquire unilateral authority over:

- currency issuance;
- public taxation;
- territorial land use;
- ecological limits outside their mandate;
- individual AUBI rights;
- closed-shop exclusion contrary to rights.

## 3.6 Treaty parties and Treaty institutions

Treaty institutions exercise only powers validly conferred by the Treaty after its entry into force for the relevant parties and jurisdiction.

Treaty authority shall identify:

- the parties bound;
- the territorial and subject scope;
- the decision rule;
- non-member effects;
- rights and reservations;
- review and amendment;
- withdrawal and surviving duties.

## 3.7 Meta-Governance institutions

Meta-Governance bodies are interface institutions. Their default authority is to:

- map interdependence;
- convene legitimate authorities;
- facilitate coordination;
- maintain interface protocols;
- support plural observation;
- publish advisory audits;
- incubate authorized experiments;
- preserve institutional learning.

They possess no inherent authority to tax, legislate, sanction, dissolve, transfer functions, create a successor government, or override a domain institution.

## 3.8 Tribunals and adjudicators

Tribunals decide only matters within valid jurisdiction.

They may adjudicate:

- treaty compliance;
- rights;
- procedure;
- jurisdiction;
- evidence admissibility;
- compact obligations;
- restitution.

They shall not claim authority to determine the validity of sacred knowledge or replace Indigenous law in matters reserved to Indigenous jurisdiction.

## 3.9 Guardians, auditors, and adversarial bodies

Guardians, ecological observers, the Office of the Adversary, ombuds institutions, auditors, and citizen review bodies strengthen challenge capacity.

Unless separately authorized, they may:

- inspect;
- warn;
- publish dissent;
- request reconsideration;
- initiate review;
- seek an injunction.

They do not acquire executive authority merely by identifying a risk.

# 4. Functional polycentrism

## 4.1 Constitutional topology

The GGF shall use overlapping functional jurisdictions rather than one nested chain of command.

The four principal constitutional functions are:

1. **Territorial authority — the Where:** place-based infrastructure, public services, land use, and local civic administration.
2. **Commons authority — the What:** ecological systems, shared resources, rights of nature, and cross-boundary regenerative limits.
3. **Practice and labour authority — the How:** professional standards, worker rights, occupational governance, and practice-specific integrity.
4. **Indigenous sovereign authority — the Root:** inherent territorial, cultural, legal, spiritual, and knowledge authority exercised through Indigenous-determined institutions.

The fourth is not a GGF chamber and is not reducible to a universal “Wisdom Council.” It is an interface with one or more sovereign authorities whose form is determined outside the GGF.

## 4.2 Horizontal and vertical checks

Polycentrism requires both:

- **vertical subsidiarity:** authority remains at the lowest capable scale; and
- **horizontal separation:** no territorial, commons, economic, epistemic, or coordinating body controls the others’ domains.

Overlap raises the cost of capture and creates alternative routes for challenge. It does not make tyranny, corruption, error, collusion, or capture impossible.

## 4.3 No unilateral cross-domain action

A body may act independently within its uncontested jurisdiction.

Where an action materially affects another domain, the initiating body shall:

- notify affected authorities;
- identify the cross-domain effect;
- seek required consent or review;
- publish an interface record;
- provide challenge and adjudication routes.

Consultation is insufficient where the other authority possesses a lawful veto, consent right, or exclusive jurisdiction.

## 4.4 Cross-boundary commons

Commons boundaries shall follow the relevant ecological or functional system rather than defaulting to political borders.

A cross-boundary commons institution shall include:

- upstream and downstream communities;
- directly affected jurisdictions;
- Indigenous peoples with territorial or stewardship rights;
- rights-of-nature or Guardian representation where lawfully constituted;
- relevant scientific and local knowledge;
- missing or weakly represented populations.

No affected party shall be made fully legible as the price of standing.

## 4.5 Guardian authority

Guardian roles shall be defined through fiduciary duty to the represented ecosystem or future interest.

A Guardian may possess a binding vote or injunction power where lawfully constituted. The scope shall be limited to the represented interest.

Disagreement among Guardians, Indigenous authorities, scientists, communities, and territorial bodies shall not be resolved by ranking knowledge systems in a universal hierarchy. The process shall surface:

- the claims;
- provenance;
- uncertainty;
- incompatible interpretations;
- urgency;
- reversibility;
- protected knowledge boundaries.

Where irreversible ecological harm is plausible and evidence remains unresolved, a precautionary temporary hold may apply while independent review continues.

## 4.6 Fractal adaptation

Small communities may combine institutional roles where separate bodies would be impractical.

Role combination shall be disclosed through an Observation Independence Map. A person or body acting as observer, proposer, implementer, and reviewer shall not be treated as independent merely because the functions have different names.

The logical distinction among jurisdictions must survive institutional compression.

# 5. Installation, accession, and non-member status

## 5.1 Four authority conditions

The constitutional stack shall distinguish:

### A. Voluntary coalition action

Participating actors coordinate only through consent and compact. Non-participants are not bound, except by generally applicable law or duties concerning direct harms.

### B. Treaty-party authority

Ratifying parties delegate defined powers to Treaty institutions. The institution may bind only within the conferred jurisdiction.

### C. Shared-commons authority

An institution may regulate a shared commons where validly constituted by affected authorities, treaty, law, or recognized rights-of-nature arrangement. The geographic scope follows the commons, not political convenience.

### D. Fundamental-rights and international-crime jurisdiction

Some duties may apply irrespective of consent under valid international or constitutional law. The source, threshold, jurisdiction, procedure, and remedies must be explicit.

No framework may move silently from A to D.

## 5.2 Entry into force

A future Treaty shall define:

- required ratifications;
- population, regional, or other thresholds;
- whether institutions activate incrementally;
- provisional institutions;
- authority before full entry into force;
- treatment of reservations;
- review of democratic legitimacy.

## 5.3 Non-members

Non-members may not be treated as members through interface design.

The Treaty may affect non-members only through valid:

- international law;
- border or trade measures within member jurisdiction;
- response to direct cross-boundary harm;
- shared-commons arrangements;
- emergency action lawfully protecting persons or ecosystems.

Every non-member effect shall state its legal basis, proportionality, and challenge route.

## 5.4 Accession and withdrawal

Accession shall be informed, public, and lawfully authorized.

Withdrawal shall define:

- notice;
- continuing rights and obligations;
- protection of residents and minorities;
- treatment of shared assets and records;
- unresolved claims;
- ecological and reparative obligations that survive;
- transition of essential services.

Withdrawal shall not erase responsibility for prior harm.

## 5.5 Indigenous participation

No Treaty accession by a state automatically constitutes Indigenous consent to measures affecting Indigenous territories, rights, knowledge, or institutions.

Affected Indigenous peoples retain their own standing and consent rights according to Indigenous law and applicable legal standards.

# 6. Decision classes and jurisdiction

Every consequential proposal shall be assigned a decision class before authorization.

## 6.1 Class I — Primarily local decision

**Examples:** ordinary local services, neighborhood infrastructure, internal provider operations, cultural programmes without material external effects.

**Default authority:** local territorial, community, provider, or Indigenous authority.

**Upward role:** support, interoperability, or rights review only.

**Presumption:** no higher-scale override.

## 6.2 Class II — Voluntary cross-boundary coordination

**Examples:** shared procurement, mutual aid, bilateral BAZ trade, shared technical services, voluntary regional compacts.

**Default authority:** participating parties through compact.

**Required safeguards:** scope, contribution, exit, dispute process, data limits, sunset, and treatment of non-parties.

**Presumption:** no binding effect on non-participants.

## 6.3 Class III — Material cross-boundary externality

**Examples:** upstream pollution, contagious disease, cross-border labour exploitation, harmful data or financial spillovers.

**Default authority:** affected jurisdictions and rights holders seek a compact or adjudicated remedy.

**Upward intervention condition:** credible evidence that local action or voluntary coordination cannot adequately prevent the material harm.

**Remedy:** targeted to the externality; local autonomy otherwise preserved.

## 6.4 Class IV — Shared planetary commons

**Examples:** atmosphere, high seas, migratory species, orbital environment, globally consequential biodiversity systems.

**Default authority:** validly constituted commons or Treaty institutions with affected-party and Indigenous standing.

**Required safeguards:** plural observation, planetary and local impact, distribution, historical responsibility, rights, and benefit sharing.

## 6.5 Class V — Fundamental rights and international crimes

**Examples:** genocide, slavery, torture, severe persecution, ecocide where legally defined, systematic denial of fundamental rights.

**Default authority:** competent courts and enforcement bodies under valid law.

**Required safeguards:** legality, jurisdiction, evidence, defence, independent adjudication, proportional remedy, and non-weaponization review.

## 6.6 Class VI — Indigenous territory, knowledge, culture, and sacred relations

**Examples:** development on Indigenous territory, access to sacred knowledge, research using Traditional Knowledge, representation of Indigenous authority, cultural heritage, data concerning Indigenous communities.

**Default authority:** affected Indigenous peoples through their own institutions.

**GGF role:** fulfill its own obligations, provide resources when invited, recognize bounded attestations, and coordinate with other valid jurisdictions.

**Prohibition:** state, Treaty, BAZ, Commons Trust, Meta-Governance, or GGF-defined council may substitute for affected-nation authority.

## 6.7 Emergency overlay

A crisis does not become a separate permanent jurisdiction. It temporarily modifies process within the limits of Section 13.

The emergency record shall identify the underlying decision class. A crisis label cannot convert a Class VI matter into unrestricted global jurisdiction.

# 7. Economic Subsidiarity and Authority Test

Before authority moves upward, sideways, or into a new body, the proposer shall complete an Economic and Constitutional Subsidiarity Test.

## 7.1 Required questions

1. What condition is being addressed?
2. Who is affected, including absent and future parties?
3. Which authority currently holds jurisdiction?
4. What cross-boundary harm, rights issue, or commons duty exists?
5. What evidence supports the condition?
6. What materially different explanations remain plausible?
7. Has the current authority received sufficient resources, time, and technical support?
8. Could voluntary compact, shared service, mutual aid, or narrower remedy suffice?
9. What is the least additional authority required?
10. What local discretion remains protected?
11. What rights, knowledge, and material minimums remain protected?
12. What is the duration?
13. Who may challenge the transfer?
14. What outcome would falsify the transfer’s necessity?
15. When and how does authority return?

## 7.2 Capacity-first rule

Administrative weakness shall not automatically justify centralization.

Before transferring authority, consider:

- capacity grants;
- shared services;
- technical assistance;
- temporary delegation;
- federated administration;
- independent observers;
- slower implementation;
- mutual aid.

Resource inequality must not become an automatic hierarchy of sovereignty.

## 7.3 Failure-to-act standard

A lower-scale authority “demonstrably fails to act” only where:

- it possesses jurisdiction and practical capability or has refused reasonable support;
- a defined material harm or rights duty exists;
- time and process are proportionate to urgency;
- evidence and uncertainty are published;
- the authority can respond and contest;
- an independent body authorizes the remedy.

Failure is not established solely by missing a performance target, rejecting a Meta-Governance recommendation, preserving a different ontology, or declining to adopt a preferred policy.

# 8. Refusal, consent, veto, and injunction

## 8.1 Distinct constitutional acts

The following shall remain distinct:

- **Non-participation:** declining to join an institution or process.
- **Withdrawal:** ending participation under defined terms.
- **Adaptation:** implementing a shared rule differently within allowed discretion.
- **Refusal:** declining an act, disclosure, or role.
- **Consent:** affirmative authorization where required.
- **Veto:** lawful power to prevent a decision within a defined jurisdiction.
- **Injunction:** temporary or permanent order stopping an act to protect rights or prevent harm.
- **Emergency hold:** short reversible containment pending review.
- **Reconsideration brake:** requirement that another body reconsider without predetermining the result.

## 8.2 Veto scope

A veto shall identify:

- holder;
- constituting authority;
- jurisdiction;
- decision stopped;
- legal or constitutional basis;
- evidence or bounded attestation;
- duration;
- review or renewal;
- remedy required for release.

No universal quota shall dictate how often a legitimate veto may be exercised. A numerical target for “healthy veto frequency” is not a constitutional standard.

## 8.3 Indigenous consent and veto

Where Indigenous consent is required, non-consent is not merely advice and cannot be overridden through a general popularity vote, sortition jury, global representation quota, or claim of system optimization.

The GGF may adjudicate whether its own institutions followed required procedure. It may not declare protected Indigenous reasons invalid because they were not fully disclosed or translated into a preferred evidentiary form.

## 8.4 Commons veto

A Commons Trust or Guardian veto shall be limited to material effects on the commons or represented ecological interest.

It may stop or condition the harmful activity. It shall not automatically acquire general territorial, economic, or criminal authority.

## 8.5 Consequence proportionality

A veto or charter revocation shall target the responsible act, permission, institution, or authority as closely as possible.

It shall not automatically:

- invalidate personal balances;
- suspend AUBI;
- terminate essential care;
- impose collective punishment;
- erase records;
- expose protected knowledge;
- collapse unrelated economic activity.

# 9. Decision observability and epistemic governance

## 9.1 Consequential decisions

A decision is consequential where it may materially affect:

- rights;
- sovereignty;
- territory;
- essential services;
- ecological integrity;
- economic access;
- institutional existence;
- protected knowledge;
- crisis powers;
- significant public resources.

Consequential decisions require the records in this section.

## 9.2 Observation Contract

An Observation Contract shall state:

- decision class;
- latent condition of concern;
- observations used;
- observations unavailable;
- sources and dependencies;
- population coverage;
- missing-population risk;
- protected knowledge boundaries;
- uncertainty;
- time scale and lag;
- model or interpretive method;
- alternative interpretations;
- sensor-change rules;
- independent outcomes;
- correction and challenge routes.

## 9.3 State Estimate Record

Before authorization, the responsible interpreter shall publish:

- leading state estimate;
- coexisting conditions;
- plausible alternatives;
- incompatible explanations;
- confidence and uncertainty;
- evidence for and against each;
- source-dependence concerns;
- consequences of acting under the wrong explanation;
- whether the cause remains unresolved.

“Cause unresolved” is a valid constitutional state.

## 9.4 Observation Independence Map

The decision record shall disclose whether observations share:

- personnel;
- funders;
- platforms;
- databases;
- incentives;
- algorithms;
- governance authorities;
- cultural or methodological assumptions.

Several reports generated by one institutional pipeline do not constitute independent evidence.

## 9.5 Epistemic plurality

Major planetary, intergenerational, rights, or institutional-replacement decisions should use several genuinely independent interpretive systems where feasible.

Plurality is not satisfied by running several models on the same data and ontology.

The process shall preserve disagreement rather than averaging it into one recommendation. Decision-makers may act under uncertainty but must state why the selected action remains justified across the unresolved interpretations.

## 9.6 No cultural scoring

Central bodies shall not assign a universal “cultural adaptation score,” “Indigenous alignment score,” “community sincerity score,” or equivalent metric.

Affected communities may provide narratives, conditions, consent decisions, or bounded attestations. These shall not be collapsed into a scalar of cultural compatibility.

## 9.7 Metrics and sentinels

Composite health indexes, trust scores, veto-balance targets, participation thresholds, learning-velocity ratios, and capture indicators may be exploratory dashboards.

They shall not directly trigger:

- chamber dissolution;
- exchange-rate penalties;
- trade isolation;
- authority transfer;
- Treaty enforcement;
- finding of capture;
- Indigenous intervention.

Indicators trigger inquiry. Constitutional consequences require diagnosis and authorization.

# 10. Knowledge, data, and institutional memory

## 10.1 Information classes

### Class A — Public constitutional and institutional information

Examples:

- legal authority;
- decisions;
- budgets;
- institutional rules;
- public reasons;
- conflicts of interest;
- aggregate performance.

Public institutions have a strong transparency duty.

### Class B — Purpose-limited operational information

Examples:

- system capacity;
- aggregate flows;
- case status;
- interface performance;
- ecological observations approved for sharing.

Access and retention shall follow purpose limitation.

### Class C — Personal and claimant-controlled information

Examples:

- identity;
- health;
- household circumstances;
- contribution evidence;
- location;
- migration status.

Individuals retain notice, access, correction, explanation, and appeal rights.

### Class D — Community-controlled and Indigenous-restricted knowledge

Examples:

- Traditional Knowledge;
- community research data;
- territorial observations;
- culturally restricted records;
- language resources;
- collective histories.

The relevant community or Indigenous authority governs access, use, storage, benefit sharing, and withdrawal.

### Class E — Sacred, non-transferable, or intentionally unrecorded knowledge

This information may remain outside GGF systems entirely.

A bounded conclusion, condition, veto, or attestation may substitute for disclosure.

## 10.2 Federated memory

The constitutional stack shall not require one blockchain-backed UN database or universal global ledger.

Institutional memory should be federated through:

- local custody;
- interoperable provenance;
- purpose-specific registries;
- retention limits;
- deletion or withdrawal where lawful;
- cryptographic verification where appropriate but not mandatory;
- offline and non-digital records;
- protected archives.

## 10.3 Open-source boundary

Publicly consequential algorithms, model logic, transformation rules, and institutional software should be open and auditable.

Open-source obligations do not require publication of:

- personal data;
- security-sensitive details;
- protected Indigenous knowledge;
- sacred knowledge;
- confidential witness material.

An auditable system may rely on bounded attestations and independent trusted review rather than universal disclosure.

## 10.4 Love Ledger boundary

Love Ledger narratives shall not serve as a default source for:

- institutional legitimacy;
- cultural scoring;
- participant reputation;
- labour matching;
- public sentiment measurement;
- Treaty enforcement;
- LMCI-M;
- economic sanctions.

## 10.5 Data-change governance

A consequential change in sensors, data sources, classifications, models, or retention shall be versioned and reviewed as a policy change.

The record shall explain how the change affects:

- who becomes visible;
- who disappears;
- historical comparability;
- incentives;
- privacy;
- Indigenous and community control;
- decision thresholds.

# 11. Automatic controls

## 11.1 Permitted automatic functions

Subject to versioning, audit, and human review, automation may:

- issue an alert;
- convene a meeting;
- preserve evidence;
- isolate a compromised technical node;
- pause a specific transaction or activity briefly;
- enforce a previously authorized hard safety limit;
- maintain a humanitarian minimum;
- route a case to independent review;
- publish uncertainty or data failure.

## 11.2 Prohibited automatic constitutional consequences

Automation shall not independently:

- determine guilt or capture;
- transfer jurisdiction;
- dissolve a chamber or institution;
- invalidate a BAZ economy;
- revoke Treaty membership;
- impose a permanent sanction;
- redirect protected AUBI;
- alter constitutional rights;
- rank Indigenous legitimacy;
- penalize exchange rates based on reputation;
- compel knowledge disclosure;
- authorize armed deployment;
- convert sovereign debt;
- create a successor government.

## 11.3 Containment requirements

An automatic hold shall be:

- narrow;
- time-limited;
- reversible;
- non-punitive;
- evidence-preserving;
- visible to affected parties unless immediate safety requires temporary confidentiality;
- reviewable by a human authority;
- accompanied by a protected minimum.

## 11.4 Self-validating controls

A control’s effect on its trigger indicator does not prove success.

Examples:

- dissolving a chamber reduces measured deadlock;
- adding sensors raises measured observational dimensionality;
- trade penalties lower interaction with a sanctioned BAZ;
- blocking an activity prevents further reported violations;
- changing a threshold restores formal compliance.

Independent human, institutional, and ecological outcomes are required.

# 12. Deadlock, mediation, and adjudication

## 12.1 Graduated process

A cross-domain deadlock should proceed through:

1. direct negotiation;
2. facilitated mediation;
3. publication of competing State Estimate Records;
4. affected-party deliberation;
5. independent adjudication where rights or jurisdiction are involved;
6. a sortition or citizen body where constitutionally authorized for the specific decision;
7. Treaty or court remedy where applicable.

Not every dispute requires every stage. Urgency and harm determine sequencing.

## 12.2 Timeout defaults

Timeouts may trigger escalation or temporary precautionary protection. They shall not establish which party is substantively correct.

The status quo is not always neutral. Where continuation creates irreversible harm, the interim default may be a narrow precautionary hold.

## 12.3 Sortition bodies

A sortition jury may decide a specific deadlock only where:

- its authority was established before the dispute;
- the decision is within its jurisdiction;
- affected rights and Indigenous consent are preserved;
- members receive balanced independent support;
- evidence and uncertainty are accessible;
- conflicts of interest are screened;
- appeal is available for jurisdiction, rights, or serious procedural error.

A sortition decision shall not automatically dissolve every deadlocked chamber or make the jury a temporary general government.

Institutional dissolution requires the separate process in Section 16.

## 12.4 Incentive limits

No Hearts, Leaves, Love Ledger recognition, reputation score, or political-survival threat should reward a particular settlement outcome.

Institutions may fund participation and compensate time. They should not pay a “consensus bonus” that makes agreement itself the objective regardless of justice or accuracy.

## 12.5 Jurisdictional conflicts

Where two authorities claim exclusive jurisdiction, the adjudicator shall first decide:

- the sources of authority;
- the decision class;
- territorial and subject scope;
- rights and consent;
- whether shared or concurrent jurisdiction exists.

Substantive merits follow only after jurisdiction is clarified.

# 13. Emergency Constitutionalism Protocol

## 13.1 Emergency definition

An emergency is a time-bounded condition presenting a credible and serious threat to life, fundamental rights, essential systems, or irreversible ecological integrity, where ordinary process cannot respond within the necessary time.

Political inconvenience, institutional resistance, slow reform, declining trust, or failure to meet a performance target is not an emergency.

## 13.2 Provisional action

A narrowly authorized body may take provisional action for no more than 72 hours where delay would cause serious harm.

The provisional record shall state:

- evidence;
- uncertainty;
- legal authority;
- action;
- affected rights;
- protected minimums;
- reason ordinary process was insufficient;
- review time.

## 13.3 Full emergency authorization

Continuation beyond 72 hours requires a formal authorization specifying:

- crisis class;
- geographic and subject scope;
- powers activated;
- powers prohibited;
- duration;
- oversight;
- local and Indigenous interfaces;
- financial authority;
- work mobilisation rules;
- review and termination.

An authorization should not exceed 30 days without renewal. Cumulative renewals beyond 90 days require extraordinary independent review and a transition to ordinary law where the condition has become persistent.

## 13.4 Powers that remain prohibited

Emergency authority shall not automatically permit:

- suspension of AUBI Layer 1;
- confiscation of personal balances;
- compulsory disclosure of sacred knowledge;
- permanent territorial transfer;
- permanent constitutional amendment;
- indefinite detention or censorship;
- automatic guilt or blacklist findings;
- permanent institution dissolution;
- forced labour through Community Providers;
- irreversible extraction from Indigenous territory.

## 13.5 Local sensemaking during emergency

Ordinary local interpretation may be compressed only to the minimum required by urgency.

Every emergency shall maintain:

- affected-area liaison;
- local evidence channels;
- Indigenous notification and authority where affected;
- accessible public reasons;
- post-emergency review;
- restoration of ordinary jurisdiction.

Where immediate rescue of life makes prior consent impossible, action shall be narrow, non-extractive, temporary, and immediately reviewable. It does not create precedent for later permanent use.

## 13.6 Emergency work

Any mobilisation of individuals or Community Providers shall comply with Work in Liberation’s Emergency Work Mobilisation Record, including voluntariness by default, safety, compensation, refusal, conscientious objection, disability and caregiving accommodation, time limits, and demobilisation.

## 13.7 Financial emergency

Financial measures shall comply with Financial Systems v3.4. Emergency authorities may deploy stabilization and essential-provider support within authorized limits but may not silently bypass protected AUBI obligations or redefine protected balances.

## 13.8 After-action obligations

Every emergency produces:

- an independent outcome review;
- rights and subgroup analysis;
- record of observation failures;
- account of emergency powers used;
- restoration and restitution decisions;
- sensor and rule changes proposed through ordinary process.

# 14. Indigenous covenant and economic continuity

## 14.1 Surviving Treaty of the Circle principle

Where a BAZ or economic institution operates on Indigenous territory or through an Indigenous covenant, Indigenous authority shall be structural rather than merely consultative.

The covenant may grant affected Indigenous authorities power to:

- refuse the arrangement;
- define prohibited activities;
- require conditions;
- suspend harmful activity;
- demand remediation;
- revoke the BAZ’s covenant or charter standing.

## 14.2 Unsafe coupling prohibited

The covenant shall not be implemented through one cryptographic token whose revocation automatically invalidates all Hearts, personal balances, essential services, or unrelated economic activity.

Indigenous authority should not be made politically responsible for uncontrolled economic collapse.

## 14.3 Consequence ladder

A covenant violation should proceed, subject to urgency, through:

1. warning or immediate activity-specific hold;
2. evidence preservation and independent observation;
3. Indigenous authority’s finding, condition, or bounded attestation;
4. suspension of the offending permit, project, flow, or institutional power;
5. remediation and restitution plan;
6. charter restriction or revocation;
7. Financial Systems resolution and continuity plan;
8. transfer or reconstitution of lawful functions;
9. restoration if the finding is reversed or obligations are met.

## 14.4 Protected continuity

Throughout the process:

- Layer 1 remains protected;
- innocent personal balances remain valid;
- essential services continue;
- workers retain rights and earned compensation;
- protected knowledge remains under Indigenous control;
- ecological harm is not allowed to continue merely to preserve financial stability.

## 14.5 Internal plurality

Where several Indigenous peoples, nations, clans, or authorities hold relevant standing, the GGF shall not select one as the universal representative.

The affected peoples determine whether they act through:

- separate consent decisions;
- confederation;
- joint protocol;
- territorial allocation;
- consensus;
- another Indigenous-determined mechanism.

Unresolved internal jurisdiction shall be acknowledged rather than solved through GGF sortition or quota design.

# 15. Meta-Governance limits and adaptive institutions

## 15.1 Connector rule

Meta-Governance may coordinate authority. It does not originate substantive jurisdiction.

A Meta-Governance body shall not acquire power over a domain merely because it:

- detects a problem;
- holds more data;
- responds faster;
- predicts failure;
- operates a successful pilot;
- publishes an Accountable Coordination Audit.

## 15.2 Learning and Adaptation Profile

The scalar Learning Velocity Index and automatic comparison of observational dimensionality to disturbance dimensionality shall be replaced by a plural profile.

The profile may include:

- detection latency;
- missing-population discovery;
- source independence;
- uncertainty calibration;
- challenge and correction;
- response latency;
- implementation capability;
- independent outcomes;
- model revision after falsification;
- ability to reopen decisions;
- surveillance burden;
- protected unresolved states;
- institutional capacity to devolve or dissolve.

No composite score shall directly authorize an Adaptive Surge, bypass, funding diversion, functional transfer, or dissolution.

## 15.3 Variety Gap Assessment

A Variety Gap Assessment is an advisory diagnosis produced through multiple independent perspectives.

It shall identify:

- environment dimensions considered;
- dimensions excluded;
- observation channels;
- authority blind spots;
- alternative interpretations;
- whether the apparent gap reflects missing capacity, wrong jurisdiction, missing trust, or deliberate non-observation;
- recommended responses from least to most intrusive.

A finding may convene review. It does not declare “constitutional unobservability” as a self-executing legal status.

## 15.4 Bypass Charter

A bypass may be created only by an authority competent over the affected function, with the standing of affected parties.

Every charter shall specify:

- problem and evidence;
- constituting authority;
- jurisdiction;
- participants and non-participants;
- rights floor;
- Indigenous and territorial interfaces;
- funding;
- data;
- experiment design;
- independent evaluator;
- sunset;
- challenge;
- terminal states.

Valid terminal states include:

- integration into an existing institution;
- federation;
- stable coexistence;
- succession through lawful transfer;
- dissolution;
- termination for harm or failure.

Permanent polycentric coexistence is not inherently fragmentation.

## 15.5 Reintegration pressure

A bypass may publish evidence and offer integration. It may not automatically divert funding, seize authority, or impose escalating pressure on a core institution solely because the core declined to adopt its design.

Any funding or authority change requires the ordinary constitutional process governing those resources or powers.

## 15.6 Functional transfer

Transfer of a public function requires:

- lawful authority;
- service and rights continuity;
- affected-party standing;
- independent evaluation;
- worker and record transition;
- financial settlement;
- challenge and appeal;
- review after transfer.

Performance superiority is evidence relevant to the decision, not a source of jurisdiction.

# 16. Institutional succession and dissolution

## 16.1 Dissolution triggers as alerts

Possible triggers include:

- mission completion;
- persistent rights violation;
- capture;
- inability to perform the lawful mandate;
- loss of valid constituting authority;
- obsolete function;
- community or party decision under the charter.

Metrics and audits identify cases for review. They do not dissolve institutions automatically.

## 16.2 Independent authorization

The body proposing or benefiting from dissolution shall not be the sole authorizer.

The process requires:

- notice;
- statement of authority;
- evidence and State Estimate Record;
- affected-party participation;
- Indigenous standing where relevant;
- response;
- independent decision;
- appeal for rights, jurisdiction, or serious procedure;
- transition plan.

## 16.3 Continuity plan

Before dissolution, specify:

- essential services;
- outstanding obligations;
- personal and institutional records;
- worker status;
- public assets;
- ongoing cases;
- data custody;
- knowledge archives;
- successor or devolution;
- restoration if the decision is reversed.

## 16.4 Chamber dissolution

Deadlock alone shall not automatically dissolve every participating chamber.

Dissolution may be justified where a chamber persistently lacks valid composition, violates rights, exceeds jurisdiction, or cannot perform its mandate. The remedy shall target the defective institution rather than treating disagreement itself as illegitimacy.

## 16.5 Protocol preservation

Useful protocols may survive institutional dissolution. Preservation does not authorize automatic re-instantiation.

A future body must receive its own valid mandate.

# 17. Capture resistance and accountability

## 17.1 Structural tests

Institutions should be audited for:

- self-justifying authority;
- asymmetric transparency;
- prohibitive exit costs;
- self-perpetuation as primary function;
- jurisdictional expansion;
- observer-beneficiary conflict;
- concentration of appointment and funding;
- inability to correct records;
- retaliation against refusal;
- control over essential resources.

## 17.2 No target veto frequency

A healthy system is not one in which every chamber vetoes 15–25 percent of decisions or makes a fixed share of critical decisions.

Low veto use may reflect trust and good design. High use may reflect genuine threat or institutional dysfunction. Frequency is contextual evidence, not a health target.

## 17.3 No generalized reputation enforcement

The constitutional stack shall not maintain universal BAZ, person, council, or community trust scores that automatically alter:

- exchange rates;
- trade terms;
- public standing;
- access to funds;
- treaty rights.

Specific defaults or breaches may produce proportionate contractual consequences through due process. They shall not become portable moral reputation.

## 17.4 Independent challenge routes

Every consequential institution shall provide at least one challenge route outside the original decision chain.

Depending on the domain, this may include:

- ombuds;
- Tribunal;
- affected-community review;
- Indigenous authority;
- worker advocate;
- Office of the Adversary;
- independent audit assembly;
- judicial review.

## 17.5 Refusal without retaliation

Conscientious refusers, whistleblowers, dissenting modelers, affected communities, and non-participants shall not lose unrelated rights or benefits merely because they challenge the process.

Protection does not immunize unlawful harm. It prevents disagreement from being treated as capture or disloyalty by default.

# 19. Conformance requirements

A framework conforms to this Specification only if it can answer the following questions for each consequential mechanism.

1. Who constituted the authority?
2. Whom does it bind?
3. What subject and territory does it govern?
4. Which decision class applies?
5. What observation supports action?
6. What alternative interpretations remain?
7. Who proposes, authorizes, implements, adjudicates, and evaluates?
8. What Indigenous authorities and rights are affected?
9. What may be refused, adapted, vetoed, or withheld?
10. What may happen automatically?
11. What rights and material systems remain protected?
12. What is the appeal route?
13. What is the sunset or return condition?
14. What independent outcome would falsify the intervention?
15. What happens if the observation or decision was wrong?

## 19.1 Non-conformance categories

- **P0 Constitutional conflict:** mechanism claims authority without a valid source, overrides Indigenous sovereignty, or creates irreversible consequence from an indicator.
- **P1 Interface gap:** authority is plausible but records, challenge, continuity, or decision separation are missing.
- **P2 Operational gap:** implementation, metrics, or technical details remain under-specified without immediate constitutional contradiction.

## 19.2 Required revision record

Every revised framework shall publish:

- specification version used;
- provisions adopted;
- intentional divergences;
- unresolved dependencies;
- compatibility tests performed.

# Conclusion

The constitutional stack should not be one pyramid with the Treaty at the top, Meta-Governance as its nervous system, and Indigenous authority as an integrated legitimacy module.

It should be a network of differently constituted authorities connected through explicit interfaces.

The Treaty may bind its parties within conferred jurisdiction. Indigenous peoples remain sovereign beyond and within those arrangements according to their own authority. Territorial institutions govern place. Commons institutions protect systems that cross boundaries. Labour and practice institutions govern how work is organized. Tribunals adjudicate valid law. Meta-Governance helps these institutions see and coordinate with one another without becoming their sovereign.

The governing rule is:

> **No observation creates jurisdiction, no coordination function creates sovereignty, no representation substitutes for consent, and no corrective mechanism is exempt from the constitutional limits it applies to others.**

The operational rule is:

> **For every consequential act, identify who constituted the authority, what condition is believed to exist, what uncertainty remains, who may refuse or veto, what may happen automatically, what remains protected, who adjudicates, and how power returns when the justification ends.**
