# Constitutional Stack Coherence, Sovereignty, and Observability Audit v0.1

## Do the Treaty, Indigenous Sovereignty framework, and Meta-Governance framework describe the same constitutional order?

**Status:** Internal bounded architectural audit  
**Date:** 2026-08-01  
**Primary corpus:**
- *Treaty for Our Only Home* v1.2
- *Invitational Framework for Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing* v1.0
- *Integrated Meta-Governance Framework* v1.4

**Secondary compatibility corpus:**
- AUBI Framework v2.5
- Financial Systems Framework v3.4
- Nested Economies Framework v3.2
- Work in Liberation Framework v2.0

**Scope:** Constitutional authority, legitimacy, jurisdiction, subsidiarity, Indigenous sovereignty, local sensemaking, knowledge and observation, crisis and enforcement, bypass and institutional replacement, dissolution, and interfaces with the revised economic frameworks.

**Out of scope:** External legal feasibility, empirical validation of claims and examples, current international law, verification of quotations or statistical claims, and substantive certification of Indigenous legitimacy. This is an internal GGF architecture audit. Indigenous peoples and institutions must determine whether the Indigenous-facing content is legitimate, useful, or appropriate.

# Executive summary

## Overall verdict

The three frameworks share a recognizable constitutional aspiration:

- power should be polycentric and subsidiary;
- no institution or model should monopolize reality interpretation;
- Indigenous sovereignty and knowledge must not be reduced to stakeholder consultation;
- global coordination should address genuinely planetary problems;
- institutions should remain contestable, reversible, and capable of dissolution;
- crisis action should be rapid but temporary;
- governance should learn from plural evidence and local interpretation.

That common aspiration is real and should be preserved.

The stack is nevertheless **not yet constitutionally coherent**. It combines plural legitimacy in principle with several single-chain command mechanisms in practice:

- the Treaty creates binding global decisions, universal jurisdiction, global taxation, enforcement corps, and emergency authority;
- Meta-Governance can classify institutions as unobservable, divert resources, incubate bypasses, pressure legacy bodies, transfer functions, and dissolve institutions;
- the Indigenous framework can condition BAZ economic legitimacy on an Indigenous covenant and revoke that legitimacy with immediate economic consequences.

No shared constitutional protocol determines what happens when these powers conflict.

The central audit finding is:

> **The stack has strong principles against centralized omniscience and unaccountable authority, but its most consequential mechanisms frequently move from observation to diagnosis, from diagnosis to authority transfer, and from authority transfer to irreversible institutional or economic consequences without a shared adjudication architecture.**

## Readiness verdict

**Amber-red, with a P0 constitutional-interface gate.**

None of the three should be revised in isolation before the shared authority and sovereignty relationships are fixed. A common Constitutional Interface Specification should be drafted from this audit, followed by bounded revisions of the Indigenous framework, Treaty, and Meta-Governance framework.

## Strongest surviving elements

1. **Invitational Indigenous framing.** The Indigenous framework acknowledges that it was produced by a non-Indigenous author with AI assistance, presents itself as an invitation rather than a prescription, and gives communities authority to modify, reject, or withdraw.
2. **Bounded coordination.** The Treaty explicitly rejects a system-wide omniscient controller and requires uncertainty, blind spots, and model disagreement to remain visible.
3. **Epistemic plurality.** Multiple modelling ecosystems and adversarial challenge are better than a single optimization model.
4. **Local sensemaking.** Global rules should not arrive locally as context-free commands.
5. **Conscious refusal.** Meta-Governance recognizes refusal as a legitimate integrity response rather than mere disengagement.
6. **Institutional impermanence.** Sunset, dissolution, and protocol preservation are valuable design goals.
7. **Coalition and coexistence pathways.** The Treaty and Meta-Governance both contain routes for willing actors to demonstrate alternatives without beginning from universal coercive authority.

## Critical findings

### P0-1 — The source of constitutional authority is undefined

The Treaty moves among four different legitimacy models without separating them:

1. reform of existing UN institutions;
2. a voluntary Global Stewardship Alliance of willing states;
3. a future world-charter or global constitution;
4. universal jurisdiction and binding planetary authority.

These stages may form a transition, but the document does not specify when voluntary participation becomes binding law, who is bound, what authority exists over non-participants, or how a coalition acquires legitimate planetary jurisdiction.

### P0-2 — Indigenous sovereignty is simultaneously inherent, invitational, represented, and administratively integrated

The Indigenous framework treats communities as sovereign authors of their own participation. The Treaty primarily treats Indigenous peoples through reserved representation and FPIC. Meta-Governance says it “provides” an Indigenous representation standard and plans to “deploy” Indigenous integration.

Those are not equivalent. Representation within GGF institutions cannot substitute for the inherent sovereignty of Indigenous nations, and GGF institutions cannot create or convene the Earth Council on their behalf.

### P0-3 — Local sensemaking, refusal, veto, and global jurisdiction conflict

The Treaty’s Local Sensemaking Requirement says local adaptation and refusal reports are required inputs but are not normally vetoes. The Indigenous framework grants communities complete withdrawal and proposes absolute ecological veto. The Treaty also claims absolute international-court jurisdiction in some domains and binding global planetary decisions in others.

The stack lacks a decision-class protocol explaining which form of standing applies to which decision.

### P0-4 — Automatic mechanisms carry constitutional consequences

The frameworks contain automatic or quasi-automatic mechanisms for:

- convening crisis sessions;
- changing funding and authority during an “adaptation deficit”;
- declaring an institution constitutionally unobservable;
- creating bypass zones;
- escalating reintegration pressure;
- transferring functions to successor institutions;
- reallocating captured funds;
- dissolving institutions;
- pausing sovereign debt;
- revoking a BAZ’s economic legitimacy.

Automatic alerting and narrow temporary containment may be legitimate. Automatic sanctions, authority transfer, institutional replacement, or economic collapse are not legitimate merely because an indicator crossed a threshold.

### P0-5 — Meta-Governance is not consistently limited to coordination

Meta-Governance describes itself as a connector, but its systemic-dynamics architecture can become an executive and constitutional authority. The same architecture can observe an institution, classify it as deficient, charter a bypass, divert resources, pressure reintegration, transfer its functions, and participate in dissolving it.

This creates a self-expansion pathway disguised as adaptive learning.

### P0-6 — Bounded coordination conflicts with central information infrastructure

The Treaty prohibits total visibility while proposing a blockchain-backed UN institutional-memory database, real-time dashboards, AI monitoring, global modelling, voting infrastructure, and reform trackers. Meta-Governance proposes a global variety-gap unit, reflexivity engines, real-time monitoring, and system-wide learning metrics.

The stack does not yet distinguish necessary shared evidence from the creation of a central observer.

### P0-7 — Knowledge sovereignty conflicts with open-source and permanent-memory requirements

The Treaty requires modelling systems to be open-source and auditable. The Indigenous framework requires community-controlled access, storage, use, and withdrawal. Meta-Governance archives institutional knowledge for future re-instantiation.

Some knowledge cannot be simultaneously open, permanently archived, globally auditable, culturally restricted, and revocable. The stack needs protected non-transfer and bounded-attestation rules.

### P0-8 — The Treaty of the Circle protects authority through an unsafe consequence chain

The Indigenous framework’s core insight is strong: Indigenous ecological authority should be structural rather than advisory. But the proposed chain—ecological violation, cryptographic legitimacy revocation, currency invalidation, economic collapse—can punish people who did not cause the violation, destabilize AUBI access, create key-capture incentives, and make custodians politically responsible for material crisis.

The veto should remain authoritative over the prohibited activity or covenant. The consequence chain should become staged, rights-protecting, and institutionally separable.

### P0-9 — The economic interfaces are stale

The Treaty operationalizes a Sovereign Debt Transformation Protocol that Financial Systems v3.4 explicitly deferred as too consequential and under-specified. It also uses the Love Ledger for verification and benefit tracking in ways incompatible with AUBI v2.5, and treats Hearts and Leaves as instruments whose meanings and authorities no longer match the revised economic frameworks.

### P0-10 — Crisis constitutionalism is distributed across three incompatible rule sets

The Treaty permits 24-hour votes, temporary global authority, deployment, and temporary waiver of local sensemaking. Meta-Governance permits 24-hour activation and 90-day emergency protocols. The Indigenous framework does not say whether FPIC, withdrawal, knowledge restrictions, or ecological veto can be suspended during crisis.

A single Emergency Constitutionalism Protocol is required.

# 1. Intended constitutional roles

## 1.1 Treaty for Our Only Home

The Treaty currently performs at least five functions:

1. moral and civilizational vision;
2. reform programme for the UN and Bretton Woods institutions;
3. proposed constitutional order for planetary governance;
4. implementation and geopolitical strategy;
5. public communications and campaign document.

These functions should ultimately be separated. A treaty core cannot safely mix constitutional authority with advocacy tactics, proposed AI tools, campaign corps, certifications, media labs, and illustrative budgets without making it unclear which provisions are law, policy, strategy, or aspiration.

## 1.2 Indigenous Sovereignty and Planetary Healing

The framework has two distinct and partly incompatible identities:

- an invitational space in which Indigenous peoples may consider, reject, or transform ideas;
- a detailed GGF institutional proposal defining BAZs, Earth Council composition, economic authority, AI, measurement, and cryptographic enforcement.

The first is appropriately humble. The second risks becoming a non-Indigenous specification of Indigenous governance.

The GGF can responsibly specify **its own obligations toward Indigenous peoples**. It cannot authoritatively specify what the Earth Council, Indigenous governance, Indigenous law, or Traditional Knowledge must be.

## 1.3 Integrated Meta-Governance

Meta-Governance should govern interfaces among legitimate domain authorities. It should not be an independent source of substantive authority.

Its valid functions include:

- convening;
- information translation;
- conflict routing;
- interface standards;
- identifying coordination gaps;
- facilitating voluntary experiments;
- preserving dissent;
- supporting sunset and devolution.

It becomes a governor rather than a connector when it can unilaterally:

- declare another institution constitutionally inadequate;
- divert its resources;
- create a competing authority;
- force reintegration;
- transfer functions;
- dissolve or replace the institution.

## 1.4 Correct dependency direction

The intended dependency should be:

- **Indigenous peoples and nations define their own authorities, protocols, representation, and knowledge boundaries.**
- **The Treaty recognizes those inherent authorities and establishes the obligations of Treaty institutions toward them.**
- **Meta-Governance coordinates only within the authority delegated by the Treaty, Indigenous authorities, BAZs, states, and domain frameworks.**

Meta-Governance must not “provide” the composition standard for Indigenous authority. The GGF must not convene the Earth Council. It may hold open an institutional interface, provide resources when invited, and recognize a council convened through Indigenous-led processes on Indigenous-determined timelines.

# 2. Legitimacy, accession, and the transition to binding authority

## 2.1 Four authority conditions must be separated

### A. Voluntary coalition action

Willing states, cities, civil-society bodies, BAZs, and Indigenous nations coordinate under explicit compacts. Their authority binds participants and governs shared resources they legitimately control.

### B. Treaty-party authority

Ratifying parties accept defined duties, institutions, taxation, adjudication, and enforcement. Accession, reservation, withdrawal, amendment, and dispute rules must be explicit.

### C. Shared-commons authority

Some conduct creates material effects beyond participating jurisdictions. The Treaty may claim authority over defined planetary commons, but must specify the jurisdictional basis, evidence threshold, affected-party standing, and limits.

### D. Universal rights and atrocity jurisdiction

The Treaty proposes absolute jurisdiction for fundamental rights and international crimes. This is a distinct claim requiring its own constitutional and legal basis, not an implication of joining the Global Stewardship Alliance.

## 2.2 Missing installation architecture

The stack needs a Tier 0 or Genesis installation protocol covering:

- convening authority;
- initial legitimacy sources;
- recognition of pre-existing sovereigns;
- accession and non-member status;
- provisional institutions;
- ratification thresholds;
- authority activation;
- time limits;
- transition from coalition to treaty organization;
- relationship with the UN;
- relationship with non-participating states;
- dissolution if legitimacy fails.

## 2.3 Plural legitimacy rather than one constitutional chain

The stack should not pretend that all legitimacy flows from one source. It should recognize at least:

- Indigenous inherent sovereignty;
- democratic consent of peoples and participating jurisdictions;
- treaty consent of states and other recognized parties;
- BAZ and local self-government;
- universal human-rights claims;
- ecological and intergenerational duties;
- delegated professional and domain authority.

Conflicts among those sources cannot be solved by a single global optimization or simple hierarchy. They require jurisdiction rules, protected domains, negotiated interfaces, and adjudication.

## 2.4 Sovereignty bargains

Offering leadership roles, finance, technology, or security guarantees in exchange for veto cession may be a pragmatic transition tactic. It may also become coercive where parties face crisis dependence or unequal bargaining power.

Any sovereignty bargain should disclose:

- exchanged powers;
- beneficiaries;
- dependency created;
- alternatives;
- duration;
- revocation;
- public ratification;
- distributional effects;
- whether the bargain affects non-consenting peoples or territories.

# 3. Indigenous sovereignty, Earth Council, and internal plurality

## 3.1 What is already sound

The Indigenous framework correctly states that:

- it is an invitation, not a prescription;
- it was not produced through an Indigenous mandate;
- communities may adapt, reject, or withdraw;
- no proposed Earth Council has authority over non-participating communities;
- knowledge and technology should remain under community control.

These are constitutional constraints, not introductory disclaimers.

## 3.2 Earth Council recognition rule

The stack should state:

> **The GGF does not establish, appoint, certify, or convene the Earth Council. It recognizes the sovereign right of Indigenous peoples and nations to convene one or more institutions, or none, through processes and timelines they determine. Treaty institutions hold open interfaces and resources without defining the body’s legitimacy, composition, or internal law.**

There may be several councils, confederations, territorial authorities, or issue-specific bodies. The framework should not assume one global Indigenous voice.

## 3.3 Representation is not sovereignty

Reserved seats and percentage quotas may improve institutional inclusion. They do not grant or exhaust Indigenous sovereignty.

The current 30% Meta-Governance representation target is problematic because it:

- is chosen by the GGF rather than Indigenous peoples;
- aggregates diverse peoples into one category;
- may invite non-sovereign representatives to speak for sovereign nations;
- can be met numerically while territorial authority is ignored;
- treats representation within a foreign institution as equivalent to jurisdiction over land, knowledge, and culture.

Representation should be one interface among several, alongside:

- nation-to-nation relations;
- territorial consent;
- Indigenous-law jurisdiction;
- knowledge veto;
- affected-nation standing;
- withdrawal;
- independent Indigenous adjudication.

## 3.4 Internal plurality and contested standing

The framework must not assume that “Indigenous Custodians” are a self-identifying, uncontested actor. A legitimate interface must be able to handle:

- overlapping traditional territories;
- several nations connected to one watershed;
- disagreements among hereditary, elected, elder, youth, women’s, and land-defender authorities;
- displaced and urban Indigenous populations;
- contested state recognition;
- communities that reject GGF participation;
- disputes about who may disclose knowledge;
- changes in community authorization.

The GGF should not resolve these questions through its own representation rules. It should accept community-defined authority records and Indigenous-led dispute processes.

## 3.5 Treaty of the Circle

### Surviving constitutional insight

A BAZ operating on Indigenous territories cannot obtain legitimate ecological or economic authority merely by consulting Indigenous peoples. The relationship may require a binding covenant and an Indigenous power to stop prohibited activity.

### Unsafe current mechanism

The current chain collapses several functions:

1. Indigenous ecological observation;
2. determination that a threshold was violated;
3. interpretation of Treaty breach;
4. revocation of authorization;
5. invalidation of currency legitimacy;
6. collapse of the economic charter.

These functions require separation even where the Indigenous authority has final substantive standing on ecological authorization.

### Recommended consequence ladder

1. **Alert and evidence preservation** — an Indigenous authority issues a warning or stop notice.
2. **Immediate activity-specific suspension** — the harmful activity stops where the evidence and Indigenous law justify it.
3. **Protected material minimums** — AUBI Layer 1, personal balances, essential services, and uninvolved providers remain protected.
4. **Covenant review** — the BAZ must demonstrate restoration, compliance, or lawful challenge limited to identity, scope, procedure, or factual error.
5. **Charter restriction or revocation** — the Indigenous authority may withdraw the covenant or specified permissions.
6. **Orderly financial resolution** — Financial Systems authorities implement a ring-fenced wind-down, not instantaneous currency collapse.
7. **Restoration and restitution** — harmed communities and ecosystems receive priority.

A colonial tribunal must not overrule valid Indigenous law on Indigenous territory. But procedural separation protects the authority from being blamed for uncontrolled harm to innocent residents and reduces incentives for cryptographic or political capture.

## 3.6 “Cryptographic sovereignty”

Cryptography can enforce a protocol. It cannot create sovereignty, establish rightful representation, resolve territorial legitimacy, or determine ecological truth.

The phrase should be replaced with **cryptographically enforced delegated authority**, explicitly subordinate to Indigenous law and community governance.

## 3.7 Romanticized safeguards

The claim that traditional governance has millennia-old accountability mechanisms is not an adequate anti-corruption protocol. It generalizes across peoples and risks romanticizing Indigenous institutions.

The framework should instead require that each participating nation define:

- authorized decision-makers;
- conflicts of interest;
- succession;
- internal review;
- revocation of representatives;
- handling of dissent;
- security and key custody;
- transparency and protected secrecy.

# 4. Subsidiarity, local sensemaking, and jurisdiction

## 4.1 Local sensemaking is currently consultative

The Treaty requires local interpretation but allows a policy to proceed despite local refusal unless a separate veto applies. This is useful as a minimum contextualization rule but insufficient as a constitutional allocation of authority.

## 4.2 Decision classes

The stack should distinguish at least six classes.

### Class I — Primarily local decisions

Local authorities govern unless rights or material externalities are implicated.

Examples:
- local administration;
- cultural practice;
- service design;
- local economic organization.

### Class II — Voluntary cross-boundary coordination

Affected jurisdictions negotiate a compact.

Examples:
- shared infrastructure;
- mutual aid;
- trade interfaces;
- watershed projects among consenting parties.

### Class III — Material cross-boundary externality

A higher-scale process may act when the originating jurisdiction cannot or will not contain significant harm to others.

It must prove:
- affected parties;
- causal pathway;
- severity;
- necessity;
- least-authority intervention;
- return condition.

### Class IV — Shared planetary commons

Treaty institutions may exercise defined authority over oceans, atmosphere, orbital commons, and other explicitly chartered domains. Jurisdiction must be purpose-limited and contestable.

### Class V — Fundamental rights and international crimes

Independent legal institutions may act under the applicable constitutional basis. Emergency or political bodies may not determine guilt.

### Class VI — Indigenous territory, knowledge, culture, and sacred relations

Indigenous law, territorial authority, FPIC, and Red Lines apply. Representation in global bodies does not replace affected-nation authorization.

## 4.3 Adaptation, refusal, and exit

These are different rights:

- **adaptation:** implement the shared objective through a local form;
- **exception:** receive a bounded exemption;
- **refusal:** decline participation in a process;
- **veto:** block a decision within a protected jurisdiction;
- **exit:** leave an institution or compact;
- **non-recognition:** reject the institution’s claimed authority.

The stack should specify which actors hold which right for each decision class.

## 4.4 Fractal Governance Bandwidth

The intuition that different scales are suited to different temporal bands is useful. The current specification is not sufficient for jurisdiction.

Latency alone does not determine the correct scale. A local action can create global fast-moving harm; a global slow-variable policy can impose immediate local injury. A higher level may have slower response but broader information, while a local level may have faster response and better context.

The assignment test should include:

- spatial extent;
- temporal frequency;
- irreversibility;
- rights implications;
- ecological scale;
- knowledge locality;
- response latency;
- local capability;
- cross-boundary effects;
- reversibility and exit.

## 4.5 “Demonstrable failure to act”

The Meta-Governance non-override rule allows intervention where a lower layer demonstrably fails. That phrase is currently a gateway to centralization.

Before upward intervention, require:

1. an Observation Contract;
2. a State Estimate Record;
3. the lower authority’s account;
4. alternative explanations such as capacity shortage or lawful refusal;
5. assistance-first options;
6. independent review;
7. the minimum necessary transfer;
8. a return date or condition.

# 5. Knowledge, observation, and epistemic authority

## 5.1 Epistemic Plurality Protocol

The EPP is one of the strongest parts of the Treaty. It correctly requires:

- more than one model;
- explicit assumptions;
- uncertainty;
- visible disagreement;
- adversarial testing.

But “three independent modelling ecosystems” is not enough to establish independence. Models may share:

- training data;
- sensor networks;
- funding;
- cloud infrastructure;
- institutional incentives;
- ontology;
- parameter assumptions;
- evaluators.

The stack needs an **Observation Independence Map** for major decisions.

## 5.2 Observation Contract

Each consequential global or meta-governance decision should publish:

1. decision and authority;
2. condition being assessed;
3. observed variables;
4. unobserved or protected variables;
5. data sources;
6. source dependencies;
7. sampling and missing populations;
8. model or interpretive processes;
9. alternative state estimates;
10. uncertainty and disagreement;
11. affected communities’ accounts;
12. permitted alert or containment;
13. prohibited inference;
14. decision rule;
15. rights and challenge process;
16. independent outcomes;
17. reassessment and sunset;
18. protocol version.

## 5.3 Model disagreement

Disagreement should not always be “resolved.” It may justify:

- collecting new evidence;
- preserving local variation;
- a reversible pilot;
- temporary containment;
- no action;
- several parallel approaches;
- explicit unresolved status.

A plural dashboard should not be followed by a hidden aggregation rule that produces one binding recommendation.

## 5.4 Cultural adaptation scoring

A global model should not assign a single “cultural alignment” score to a community. That converts local meaning and authority into a comparable administrative variable.

Use:

- community-authored interpretation;
- documented objections;
- protected red lines;
- bounded attestations;
- several non-aggregated dimensions;
- no ranking of cultures.

## 5.5 Protected non-transfer

The stack must recognize that some knowledge may be:

- retained entirely by the community;
- disclosed only to named persons;
- available only for a defined purpose;
- represented through a conclusion, condition, or veto;
- non-digitized;
- revocable;
- unavailable to general audit.

A global system may receive the statement “authorization denied” without receiving the sacred, relational, or ecological knowledge supporting it.

## 5.6 Institutional memory

A blockchain-backed UN database is incompatible with data minimization and revocability if it stores primary knowledge or personal/community records.

Institutional memory should use:

- federated storage;
- community-controlled custody;
- purpose limitation;
- protocol and decision provenance;
- minimal public records;
- revocable access where possible;
- protected local archives;
- deletion and correction rules;
- no presumption that permanence is always desirable.

## 5.7 Epistemic security

Monitoring misinformation, nationalist rhetoric, or narrative-reality gaps may help detect coordinated harm. It may also convert political dissent into a security signal.

The system must distinguish:

- factual correction;
- lawful political opposition;
- refusal of GGF legitimacy;
- propaganda;
- incitement;
- coordinated deception;
- imminent threat.

No actor should be sanctioned merely for rejecting the Treaty, Meta-Governance, globalism, or a model’s conclusions.

# 6. Automatic controls, enforcement, and emergency powers

## 6.1 Constitutional classification

### Safe or potentially safe automatic functions

- alert;
- convene a meeting;
- preserve evidence;
- initiate independent review;
- pause a narrowly defined technical process;
- apply a temporary, reversible safety limit.

### Functions that require accountable authorization

- transfer authority;
- divert institutional funding;
- impose sanctions;
- dissolve an institution;
- replace a government function;
- invalidate an economic charter;
- deprive persons of balances or services;
- declare guilt;
- override Indigenous or local jurisdiction;
- publish protected evidence.

## 6.2 Treaty mechanisms

### AI-triggered emergency sessions

Automatically convening a meeting is relatively low risk if the trigger is transparent, contestable, and does not itself establish a crisis diagnosis.

### Crisis Prevention Bonds

These may reward measurable prevention but are vulnerable to gaming, attribution error, selective crisis definition, and paying actors for conditions they did not cause. They require independent baselines and additionality.

### Fast-track binding votes

Speed may be necessary, but a 24- or 48-hour vote requires pre-existing authority, clear decision classes, minimum evidence, affected-party representation, and post-decision review.

### AI Auditors

AI may organize and flag evidence. It cannot establish legal admissibility, identity, intent, guilt, or sanction.

### Automatic debt pauses

A pre-authorized temporary debt-service pause during a formally defined shock may be a legitimate containment mechanism. Permanent conversion or creditor reallocation requires a dedicated debt framework.

## 6.3 Meta-Governance mechanisms

### Adaptive Surge

The current trigger automatically shifts resources and authority when a scalar learning-velocity relationship deteriorates. This is too consequential for an uncertain estimator.

A low-confidence indicator may trigger an **Adaptive Review**, not an automatic authority shift.

### Constitutional-unobservability declaration

No unit housed in the GIF-Council should automatically mandate another institution’s replacement based on its own variety-gap estimate.

The declaration should become a contestable audit finding requiring:

- independent plural assessment;
- affected-institution response;
- local and domain evidence;
- appeal;
- possible capacity support;
- constitutional authorization for any redesign.

### Automatic fund reallocation

A capture alert can pause new discretionary commitments. It cannot permanently reallocate funds without an accountable decision and protection of essential functions.

### Automatic dissolution

Dissolution should never be a purely automatic consequence of a metric. It requires:

- valid authority;
- evidence;
- notice;
- response;
- independent decision;
- continuity of rights and essential services;
- records and asset transfer;
- appeal;
- restoration where the diagnosis was wrong.

## 6.4 Emergency Constitutionalism Protocol

All three frameworks should share one protocol specifying:

1. enumerated crisis classes;
2. evidence threshold;
3. lawful declaring authority;
4. territorial and subject-matter scope;
5. powers activated;
6. powers never activated;
7. Indigenous authority and knowledge protections;
8. AUBI and material minimums;
9. local implementation rights;
10. refusal and conscientious objection;
11. temporary containment versus permanent action;
12. legislative and judicial review;
13. public reasoning and uncertainty;
14. maximum duration;
15. renewal threshold;
16. no serial emergency evasion;
17. demobilisation;
18. restoration and restitution;
19. after-action review;
20. sensor-change and record preservation.

Emergency waiver of local sensemaking should be limited to the immediate response window. It must not waive Indigenous territorial authority, permit compulsory knowledge disclosure, or create permanent institutional changes through temporary power.

# 7. Meta-Governance systemic-dynamics audit

## 7.1 Learning Velocity Index

The proposed index compares the rate of expansion in observational dimensionality with the rate of emergence of disturbance dimensions.

This is not currently an operational quantity. Major issues include:

- dimensionality depends on the representation chosen;
- adding indicators may add noise or correlated sensors;
- adding data does not establish learning;
- disturbance dimensions may be discovered only after failure;
- the system can improve its score by proliferating sensors;
- observation can expand while decision quality deteriorates;
- protected unobservability may correctly limit observation;
- the GIF-Council both computes the indicator and gains influence when it signals a deficit.

## 7.2 Recommended decomposition

Replace the scalar trigger with a **Learning and Adaptation Profile**:

- detection latency;
- missing-population discovery;
- observer diversity and independence;
- uncertainty calibration;
- challenge and correction performance;
- time from evidence to reasoned decision;
- implementation fidelity;
- independent outcome learning;
- model revision after falsification;
- ability to reopen closed decisions;
- protection against sensor proliferation and surveillance;
- capacity to preserve unresolved states.

No composite profile should automatically transfer authority.

## 7.3 Variety Gap Assessment

The variety-gap idea is useful as a diagnostic question:

> Does the institution possess enough independent observation, interpretation, action, and learning capacity for the disturbances it is expected to govern?

It becomes unsafe when represented as one measurable gap with a universal critical threshold.

A valid assessment should separate:

- environment variety;
- observation variety;
- action variety;
- latency;
- authority;
- capability;
- legitimacy;
- learning;
- ungovernable uncertainty.

## 7.4 Bypass Architecture

### Surviving value

A temporary external experiment may be appropriate when a core institution cannot adapt internally.

### Current risks

- Meta-Governance judges the failure;
- Meta-Governance charters the bypass;
- the bypass receives protection;
- it feeds evidence to the core;
- pressure escalates if the core does not accept the evidence;
- functions can be transferred to a successor;
- permanent coexistence is prohibited.

This architecture treats reintegration or replacement as the only valid outcomes. It excludes the possibility that durable polycentric coexistence is legitimate.

### Required Bypass Charter

Every bypass needs:

- legitimate territorial and domain consent;
- clear problem and limits;
- rights floor;
- non-extraction rule;
- no experimental reduction of protections;
- independent evaluation;
- participant exit;
- affected-nonparticipant standing;
- funding transparency;
- no self-expanding jurisdiction;
- sunset;
- several possible outcomes: dissolve, integrate, coexist, federate, or replace through separately authorized process.

A bypass may not acquire sovereignty merely by outperforming a legacy institution on selected metrics.

## 7.5 Functional transfer

Performance failure does not itself transfer legal authority. A functional transfer requires the authority that originally constituted the function—or another valid constitutional process—to approve the transfer.

## 7.6 Governance Sunset Commission

The Commission’s goal is valuable, but appointment by the GIF-Council creates dependence on the architecture it may need to dissolve.

A stronger design would use:

- mixed appointment sources;
- short non-renewable terms;
- conflict exclusions;
- affected-community panels;
- independent legal review;
- public reasoning;
- continuity plans;
- no authority to dissolve sovereign Indigenous bodies or institutions outside Treaty jurisdiction.

## 7.7 Liberatory Impermanence

The philosophical principle should remain a design orientation. “Natural coordination” or “consciousness evolution” should not become an operational threshold authorizing dissolution or withdrawal of public protections.

# 8. Treaty-specific audit

## 8.1 The document is not yet a treaty instrument

It lacks the conventional structural distinctions needed for a constitutional treaty:

- parties;
- definitions;
- obligations;
- institutions;
- competences;
- accession;
- reservations;
- amendment;
- withdrawal;
- dispute resolution;
- emergency powers;
- financing;
- transitional provisions;
- legal hierarchy;
- entry into force.

v1.3 should separate:

1. **Treaty Core** — constitutional provisions;
2. **Institutional Protocols** — operational details;
3. **Transition Playbook** — coalition and geopolitical strategy;
4. **Public Narrative** — advocacy and communication;
5. **Research and Pilot Portfolio** — exploratory mechanisms.

## 8.2 Hierarchy and jurisdiction

The current protocol gives:

- UNSC primacy for imminent security threats;
- binding Global Council for the Future authority for planetary boundaries unless overturned;
- absolute ICJ/ICC jurisdiction for fundamental rights;
- UNGA final budget authority.

It does not define the Earth Council’s relationship to those bodies, affected Indigenous nations, BAZs, Guardians, Meta-Governance councils, or domain authorities.

## 8.3 Global Council for the Future

An advisory foresight body becomes a powerful executive-legislative actor when its recommendations are presumptively binding. Foresight expertise does not itself create democratic authority.

Its appropriate functions are:

- long-horizon assessment;
- slow-variable warnings;
- public reasons;
- mandatory reconsideration;
- emergency-brake proposals;
- standing to challenge short-term decisions.

Binding authority requires a separate constitutional basis.

## 8.4 Corporate representation

Mandatory corporate representation in global advisory bodies creates a direct capture pathway. Business knowledge can be consulted without granting corporations a constitutionally guaranteed governance constituency equivalent to peoples, rights-holders, or sovereign communities.

## 8.5 Principle Conflict Resolution

A “neutral” Global Arbitration Panel cannot be presumed neutral in disputes involving Indigenous law, colonial boundaries, sacred sites, or inherent sovereignty.

For those matters:

- Indigenous forums have primary jurisdiction;
- Treaty bodies may adjudicate their own obligations;
- joint forums may be created by consent;
- the panel cannot determine the validity of Traditional Knowledge.

## 8.6 Indexes, rankings, and certifications

Global Reform Indexes and Future-Ready certifications may encourage learning, but they also create conformity incentives and compress complex constitutional differences into competitive scores.

They should be:

- voluntary;
- multidimensional;
- transparent about normative choices;
- non-punitive;
- open to counter-assessment;
- barred from substituting for legal or democratic legitimacy.

## 8.7 Public claims and targets

Cost estimates, precedent claims, quotations, biodiversity percentages, and effectiveness claims require external source verification before public release. This audit does not validate them.

# 9. Indigenous-framework-specific audit

## 9.1 Recommended split

### Part A — Indigenous invitation and sovereign process

This part should be short and restrained. It should:

- acknowledge authorship limits;
- hold space for Indigenous-led development;
- state the Red Lines;
- provide resources and questions rather than institutional answers;
- avoid claiming to synthesize “Indigenous wisdom” as one system.

### Part B — GGF obligations toward Indigenous peoples

This part can be operational because it governs the GGF’s own conduct:

- recognition, not creation, of Indigenous authorities;
- FPIC and territorial consent;
- data and knowledge sovereignty;
- protected non-transfer;
- reparations;
- land rematriation support;
- Indigenous-law interfaces;
- non-retaliation for withdrawal;
- no GGF use of knowledge without authority;
- funding controlled through invited processes;
- affected-nation vetoes;
- Earth Council interface held open.

### Part C — Provisional mechanisms for co-development

BAZs, Treaty of the Circle, AI tools, GPH, and economic interfaces should remain explicitly provisional until Indigenous-led co-development occurs.

## 9.2 Homogenization risk

The document uses examples and terms from many peoples as elements of one planetary framework. That may be inspirational, but it can imply interchangeability among distinct legal, spiritual, territorial, and governance traditions.

Each concept should be attributed, contextualized, and used only with appropriate authority. Generic phrases such as “Indigenous wisdom,” “traditional governance,” or “ceremonial governance” should not stand in for specific peoples’ laws.

## 9.3 Quotations and teachings

The document’s attributed proverbs and generalized “Indigenous teachings” should undergo Indigenous-led source, attribution, and permission review. Some widely circulated sayings have uncertain or non-Indigenous origins. This audit does not determine their authenticity.

## 9.4 Indigenous AI Oracles

The name and concept risk turning living knowledge-holders into data sources for an AI representation.

Any technical system must be:

- optional;
- community-owned;
- purpose-limited;
- locally hosted where desired;
- capable of functioning without model training on protected knowledge;
- revocable;
- unable to speak as the community;
- barred from making binding cultural or ecological determinations.

A bounded system may help authorized knowledge-holders manage records or scenario information. It cannot become an oracle whose output substitutes for living authority.

## 9.5 GPH and economic algorithms

Community-controlled ecological and cultural assessment may be valuable. It must not be silently integrated into AUBI algorithms, global exchange rates, eligibility, or debt conversion.

Population flourishing, contribution allocation, ecological evidence, operational telemetry, and voluntary narratives must remain distinct.

# 11. Shared constitutional interface

## 11.1 Plural legitimacy map

For every institution and power, record:

- constituting authority;
- participating parties;
- territorial scope;
- subject-matter scope;
- rights protected;
- Indigenous authorities affected;
- decision process;
- veto and refusal rights;
- appeal and adjudication;
- funding;
- sunset;
- devolution or dissolution;
- non-member effects.

## 11.2 Decision-chain separation

Consequential actions should distinguish:

1. observation;
2. interpretation;
3. proposal;
4. authorization;
5. implementation;
6. adjudication;
7. outcome evaluation;
8. reversal or restoration.

The same body should not control all eight.

## 11.3 Minimum authority principle

> **A higher-scale or meta-governance body may exercise only the least authority necessary to address a demonstrated cross-boundary condition, rights violation, shared-commons duty, or constitutionally defined crisis. Authority returns when the justification ends.**

## 11.4 Indigenous sovereignty principle

> **Indigenous sovereignty is recognized, not delegated by the GGF. The GGF cannot define who legitimately speaks for an Indigenous people, create an Indigenous authority, compel disclosure of protected knowledge, or substitute global representation for affected-nation consent.**

## 11.5 Observability principle

> **An institutional indicator, model disagreement, variety-gap estimate, crisis alert, ecological threshold, or public-trust measure may justify attention or bounded containment. It does not by itself authorize guilt, sanction, dissolution, replacement, authority transfer, or economic invalidation.**

## 11.6 Protected continuity principle

Dissolution, charter revocation, emergency intervention, or institutional transfer must preserve:

- human rights;
- AUBI Layer 1;
- personal balances;
- essential services;
- records needed for claims and appeals;
- community data and knowledge control;
- worker and participant rights;
- restoration for erroneous action.

# 15. Final assessment

The three frameworks are not incoherent because they value different things. They are incoherent because the same values are implemented through incompatible authority models.

The Treaty says:

- global law must become effective;
- planetary threats require binding coordination;
- models and institutions must remain plural and bounded.

The Indigenous framework says:

- sovereignty cannot be granted by the GGF;
- communities may reject the framework;
- ecological authority must be structural rather than advisory.

Meta-Governance says:

- coordination must resist capture;
- institutions must learn and dissolve;
- bypasses may succeed where core systems fail.

All three claims can coexist, but only if the stack accepts that:

1. no institution creates all legitimacy;
2. observation does not grant authority;
3. superior performance does not automatically transfer jurisdiction;
4. Indigenous sovereignty is recognized rather than integrated by quota;
5. crisis does not erase constitutional boundaries;
6. open knowledge does not override protected knowledge;
7. veto and revocation must stop harmful authority without creating uncontrolled collateral harm;
8. coordination remains a delegated function, not a path to sovereignty;
9. dissolution preserves rights and continuity;
10. unresolved disagreement is sometimes a legitimate constitutional state.

The governing principle for the next stack should be:

> **Coordinate globally where the condition is genuinely shared; preserve authority where knowledge, rights, and responsibility are local; recognize Indigenous sovereignty rather than administrating it; and never allow an observation system to convert its own diagnosis into unchecked power.**

The Indigenous-interface principle should be:

> **The GGF may offer resources, recognition, and binding obligations upon itself. It may not create the Indigenous authority from which it seeks legitimacy.**

The Meta-Governance principle should be:

> **Meta-Governance may reveal a coordination failure, convene the legitimate authorities, and support bounded experiments. It may not acquire the failed institution’s powers merely because its own audit says the institution failed.**

The Treaty principle should be:

> **Planetary authority must be explicit about who constituted it, whom it binds, which harms justify crossing jurisdictional boundaries, which powers remain prohibited, and how authority returns when the justification ends.**

With those changes, the three documents could become a coherent constitutional stack:

- Indigenous sovereignty as a recognized source of authority and limit;
- the Treaty as a legitimate, bounded constitutional compact;
- Meta-Governance as the coordination protocol operating between plural authorities.

Without those changes, the stack risks reproducing what it explicitly opposes: a globally benevolent architecture that treats its own models of crisis, learning, and stewardship as sufficient grounds for expanding control.
