# Executive summary

A constitutional order cannot legitimately announce itself into existence. Someone must draft, convene, raise preparatory funds, test tools, organize public deliberation, preserve records, and prepare first-seating procedures before ordinary Treaty institutions exist. Those functions are unavoidable.

The mistake is to treat those preparatory functions as a source of public authority.

Genesis Protocol v0.9 therefore begins with a strict constitutional boundary:

> **Genesis prepares the conditions under which authority may be validly constituted; it does not itself constitute that authority.**

The Global Governance Frameworks initiative is an open-source design and research project. It possesses no inherent constitutional, representative, fiscal, regulatory, judicial, emergency, diplomatic, or Treaty-making authority. Successful demonstrations, technical competence, moral urgency, public attention, donor support, or authorship of the framework create no jurisdiction over any person, people, community, territory, commons, institution, or state.

Before the Treaty enters into force, every consequential Genesis act must identify an independently valid authority source. Depending on the act, that source may be:

- voluntary individual consent;
- the lawful charter of a private or civil-society body;
- municipal, regional, national, or other existing public law;
- Indigenous authority identified by the relevant people;
- a contract, trust, or compact among participants;
- a formal negotiating mandate from a prospective Party;
- a lawful referendum, legislative act, or other public authorization within a constituted jurisdiction.

After Treaty entry into force, public powers derive from Treaty v1.3, valid implementing law, lawful compacts, Indigenous authority within its own jurisdiction, and separately ratified protocols. No authority derives from the GGF initiative merely because it proposed the architecture.

The Protocol uses six phase gates:

1. **Design and voluntary preparation** — open-source drafting and lawful administrative organization.
2. **Demonstration and learning** — locally authorized pilots with participant rights, independent evaluation, and no conversion of participation into political consent.
3. **Negotiation readiness** — independent assessment of whether formal Treaty negotiation is worth inviting.
4. **Voluntary founding coalition and Treaty negotiation** — mandated participants negotiate; the GGF draft becomes one input rather than canonical law.
5. **Ratification and entry into force** — governed entirely by Treaty v1.3 and each participant’s constitutional processes.
6. **First seating, handoff, dissolution, and retrospective** — Treaty institutions are lawfully seated; founding bodies transfer control and cease any privileged role.

The process may legitimately end without a Treaty. Pilots may succeed while the Treaty fails to attract authorization. Indigenous peoples may decline engagement. States may refuse ratification. Publics may remain divided. An emergency may arise before entry into force. Genesis must be able to respond to all of those conditions without inventing a sovereign, lowering constitutional thresholds, punishing exit, or converting readiness into authority.

# 1. Purpose, scope, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

The purpose of this Protocol is to define a lawful, transparent, rights-bounded process for:

- developing and testing voluntary coordination tools;
- supporting independent evaluation and public learning;
- inviting formal negotiation of Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3;
- enabling public, governmental, Indigenous, civic, and professional participation without aggregating their distinct authorities;
- documenting ratification, accession, and activation;
- preparing the first seating of Treaty institutions under the Treaty’s own rules;
- transferring assets, records, contracts, and administrative functions;
- dissolving founding structures without creating a hidden successor centre;
- preserving the possibility that the founding effort should pause, be redesigned, or end.

## 1.2 GGF Institutional Non-Authority Principle

The Global Governance Frameworks initiative possesses no inherent:

- public authority;
- constitutional authority;
- representative authority;
- legislative or regulatory authority;
- fiscal or taxation authority;
- judicial or adjudicative authority;
- emergency or deployment authority;
- Treaty-making authority;
- authority to represent Indigenous peoples;
- authority to determine the electorate called “humanity”;
- authority to create jurisdiction by declaring a problem global.

Its authorship, research, technical competence, public recognition, financial support, successful demonstrations, moral commitments, or possession of infrastructure do not create such authority.

The GGF may publish, propose, research, convene voluntary discussions, provide technical assistance, and participate under the same lawful conditions as other civil-society actors. It may not treat its own framework architecture as enacted law.

## 1.3 No authority by branding or continuity

No entity acquires constitutional standing by using the names “GGF,” “Genesis,” “Treaty,” “Global Stewardship,” “Earth Council,” “Meta-Governance,” or related terminology.

A body’s authority depends on its lawful source, defined competence, affected jurisdiction, and accountable authorization—not on continuity with the framework’s authors, repositories, domains, funders, or public identity.

## 1.4 Demonstration is not jurisdiction

Evidence that a mechanism works may justify:

- continued experimentation;
- local adoption;
- public support;
- further funding;
- negotiation;
- replication under new lawful authorization.

It does not justify:

- authority over non-participants;
- Treaty jurisdiction;
- taxation;
- criminal or civil adjudication;
- compulsory sanctions;
- armed or police deployment;
- representation of Indigenous peoples;
- creation of a global electorate;
- appointment of Treaty officers;
- constitutional amendment.

## 1.5 Conscience, culture, and voluntary association

Participation in Genesis does not require ideological, spiritual, cultural, or developmental agreement with the GGF’s wider vision.

No Genesis body has authority over:

- personal conscience;
- religious or spiritual practice;
- family and intimate life;
- cultural identity;
- lawful political disagreement;
- refusal to endorse the GGF as a movement.

A participant may use a tool, join a pilot, negotiate a compact, or support a Treaty provision while rejecting other GGF concepts.

## 1.6 Constitutional hierarchy of documents

This Protocol is subordinate to:

1. inherent Indigenous sovereignty and applicable Indigenous law;
2. fundamental rights and applicable law;
3. Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3 after valid entry into force;
4. lawful compacts, charters, contracts, and implementing instruments within their jurisdiction;
5. the Constitutional Interface Specification v0.1 for framework-boundary interpretation.

Where this Protocol conflicts with a valid higher authority, the higher authority governs. Genesis may record and challenge the conflict; it may not silently expand its own competence.

# 2. Authority provenance and plural authorization

## 2.1 No single founding sovereign

Genesis does not create a “Pluriversal Sovereign” or any other composite global sovereign.

Public, Indigenous, democratic, contractual, professional, territorial, and commons authorities are not interchangeable votes within one pre-existing constitutional body. They may coordinate through a **Plural Founding Compact**, but one authorization channel does not manufacture another.

## 2.2 Valid pre-entry authority sources

Before Treaty entry into force, a Genesis act must derive from one or more of the following:

### A. Individual consent

A person may authorize use of their time, data, participation, creative work, or property, subject to applicable rights and the ability to withdraw.

### B. Organizational authority

A civil-society organization, cooperative, company, university, foundation, or other body may act within its lawful charter and governance rules.

### C. Existing public law

A municipality, region, state, public agency, or international organization may authorize a pilot, grant, negotiation, or administrative act within its existing legal competence.

### D. Indigenous authority

An Indigenous people or nation may authorize, refuse, condition, or structure engagement through authorities and processes it determines. Genesis does not certify who those authorities are.

### E. Contract, compact, or trust

Participants may create binding relations among themselves through lawful contracts, compacts, fiscal sponsorships, trusts, or consortium agreements. Such instruments bind only within their valid scope.

### F. Negotiating mandate

A prospective Treaty participant may authorize representatives to negotiate, observe, or propose text. A negotiating mandate is not ratification.

### G. Treaty and implementing law

After entry into force, Treaty institutions act only under powers validly activated by Treaty v1.3, implementing law, or separately ratified protocols.

## 2.3 Authority Provenance Record

Every consequential Genesis act requires a public **Authority Provenance Record** before implementation.

The record states:

1. the proposed act;
2. the actor;
3. the people, territory, institution, resource, or rights affected;
4. the claimed source of authority;
5. the legal or contractual instrument;
6. the exact scope and duration;
7. whether participation is voluntary or compulsory;
8. the rights and protected interests involved;
9. the limits and prohibited uses;
10. the challenge and appeal route;
11. the termination or handoff condition;
12. unresolved authority disputes.

Where no valid authority source can be identified, the consequential act does not proceed.

> **No provenance record, no consequential action.**

## 2.4 Authority disputes

If authority is contested:

- the claim and counterclaim are published where disclosure is safe;
- affected parties receive notice and an opportunity to respond;
- irreversible action pauses unless delay risks grave and imminent harm;
- any interim protection is narrow, reversible, and grounded in existing lawful authority;
- an independent lawful forum reviews the dispute;
- Genesis does not adjudicate its own competence.

## 2.5 Distinct authorization records

Genesis keeps separate records for:

- pilot authorization;
- public consultation;
- negotiating participation;
- Indigenous consent, refusal, or non-engagement;
- Treaty adoption;
- ratification or accession;
- entry into force;
- institution first seating;
- activation of coercive protocols.

No record is treated as a substitute for another.

# 3. The open-source GGF design commons and the Pre-Genesis Facilitation Consortium

## 3.1 The GGF design commons

The GGF may continue as an open-source design, research, translation, simulation, and public-education commons.

Its legitimate functions include:

- publishing draft frameworks;
- receiving criticism and alternative proposals;
- maintaining transparent version histories;
- producing tools and reference implementations;
- supporting independent research;
- offering non-binding technical assistance;
- documenting unresolved questions and failed mechanisms.

The design commons has no privileged constitutional role. Once formal Treaty negotiation begins, negotiating participants may modify, reject, fork, or replace GGF drafts.

## 3.2 Separate facilitation entity

Where administrative work requires a legal person, participants may form or appoint a **Pre-Genesis Facilitation Consortium** under ordinary law.

The Consortium is not the GGF as a constitutional institution. It is an administrative service arrangement whose authority comes only from:

- its charter;
- participant contracts;
- host-government mandates;
- grant agreements;
- fiscal or trust law;
- other valid instruments named in its Authority Provenance Records.

## 3.3 Permitted Consortium functions

The Consortium may:

- administer voluntary funds;
- contract translation, accessibility, security, evaluation, and logistics services;
- support locally authorized demonstrations;
- maintain public and protected records;
- organize voluntary meetings;
- facilitate drafting and public comment;
- provide secretarial services to a negotiating conference;
- prepare non-binding first-seating options;
- preserve evidence and refer emergencies to competent bodies;
- execute an authorized handoff.

## 3.4 Prohibited Consortium functions

The Consortium may not:

- speak for humanity, Indigenous peoples, states, cities, BAZs, or participants without a mandate;
- create a binding global electorate;
- declare the Treaty in force;
- appoint permanent Treaty officeholders;
- exercise Treaty jurisdiction;
- legislate, tax, adjudicate, sanction, or deploy;
- certify an Indigenous representative or universal Indigenous body;
- convert pilot participation into political consent;
- use access to AUBI, essential services, funding, or technical support as leverage for ratification;
- make its own readiness assessment constitutionally binding;
- control the Treaty text after negotiating participants assume custody;
- become the permanent Secretariat through continuity of name or personnel.

## 3.5 Charter requirements

The Consortium charter specifies:

- legal domicile and applicable law;
- founders, members, funders, affiliates, and vendors;
- narrow purposes and prohibited powers;
- governing and fiduciary bodies;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- procurement and donor-independence rules;
- data controllers and processors;
- intellectual-property and open-license arrangements;
- complaints, whistleblowing, and non-retaliation;
- public-record and protected-non-transfer rules;
- independent audit;
- amendment limits;
- renewal, suspension, and dissolution;
- asset and record disposition if the Treaty never enters into force.

## 3.6 No automatic founder succession

No founder, author, donor, vendor, pilot operator, or Consortium member gains a right to:

- a negotiating seat;
- public office;
- judicial or Guardian appointment;
- Treaty procurement;
- permanent employment;
- institutional veto;
- ownership of post-entry data or infrastructure.

Later service requires the ordinary process applicable to the role.

# 4. Demonstration and learning portfolio

## 4.1 Purpose

Demonstrations test whether particular coordination tools can provide value under real conditions before larger claims are made.

They do not test whether the GGF deserves sovereignty.

## 4.2 Local authorization and ownership

Each pilot is authorized and owned by the lawful host or participating compact. A GGF contributor or Consortium may provide support but does not become the governing authority.

A pilot may involve:

- a complementary currency or payment bridge;
- participatory public commissioning;
- a cross-boundary Commons Trust interface;
- a Community Provider or labour-governance mechanism;
- civic deliberation;
- institutional coordination;
- data or observability tools;
- emergency-preparedness coordination;
- other bounded mechanisms.

No specific technology, currency, BAZ form, or framework bundle is required for inclusion in the portfolio.

## 4.3 Pilot Constitutional Contract

Before launch, each pilot adopts a **Pilot Constitutional Contract** specifying:

1. lawful host authority;
2. scope, purpose, and duration;
3. participant eligibility and consent;
4. rights, refusal, withdrawal, and non-retaliation;
5. material and service continuity;
6. worker and volunteer protections;
7. funding and compensation;
8. data ownership, minimization, retention, and deletion;
9. Indigenous authority and protected knowledge where applicable;
10. evaluation questions and limitations;
11. complaint, correction, and appeal;
12. publication and community control of disclosure;
13. asset and record disposition;
14. wind-down and exit;
15. explicit non-conversion of participation into Treaty consent.

## 4.4 Demonstration diversity without legitimacy extraction

The portfolio should seek variation in:

- geography;
- income and resource conditions;
- institutional capacity;
- political systems;
- urban, rural, and mobile populations;
- language and digital access;
- ecological conditions;
- governance traditions;
- success and failure modes.

No Indigenous community, low-resource jurisdiction, conflict-affected population, or Global South institution is recruited merely to satisfy a legitimacy quota.

Categories are self-described where possible, and the portfolio publishes how category definitions and selection decisions were made.

## 4.5 Independent evaluation

Evaluation must be institutionally distinguishable from implementation and fundraising.

Every evaluation identifies:

- evaluator funding and conflicts;
- source dependencies;
- missing populations;
- counterfactual or comparison limits;
- harms and burden distribution;
- unexpected outcomes;
- transferability limits;
- unresolved causal interpretations;
- community disagreement;
- whether the pilot should continue, change, or end.

## 4.6 Failure Library and protected disclosure

Genesis maintains a public learning repository containing:

- negative and null findings;
- implementation failures;
- rights violations and corrective actions;
- mechanisms that did not transfer;
- discontinued pilots;
- abandoned hypotheses;
- dissenting evaluations.

A community is not compelled to expose sensitive failures publicly in order to satisfy a constitutional gate. Aggregate, anonymized, delayed, or bounded disclosure may be used where communities face safety, political, cultural, or reputational risks.

Sacred, restricted, personal, security-sensitive, or community-controlled knowledge may remain protected through bounded attestation.

## 4.7 Pilot withdrawal

A pilot host or participant may withdraw under the Pilot Constitutional Contract without:

- loss of unrelated public services;
- reduction of AUBI Layer 1;
- reputational blacklisting;
- conversion of sunk funding into political debt;
- forced publication;
- loss of lawful compensation or personal balances.

Surviving duties may include safety, compensation, data return, evidence preservation, remediation, and settlement of valid obligations.

## 4.8 Demonstration Readiness Gate

The demonstration portfolio may support a finding that formal Treaty negotiation should be invited.

It cannot establish that:

- the Treaty is legitimate;
- the Treaty has entered into force;
- any public has consented;
- Indigenous peoples have authorized an interface;
- coercive powers are active.

# 5. Founding Readiness Review

## 5.1 Purpose

The **Founding Readiness Review** asks only:

> Is there enough evidence, administrative capacity, public information, and participant interest to invite a formal negotiation process without misrepresenting the invitation as constitutional authorization?

## 5.2 Independent review body

The Pre-Genesis Facilitation Consortium does not certify its own readiness.

A temporary **Founding Readiness Review Panel** is established through a publicly documented agreement among participating pilot hosts, independent evaluators, and prospective negotiating participants. Its Authority Provenance Record identifies who appointed it and what its finding can authorize.

The Panel has no power to:

- declare Treaty entry into force;
- create an electorate;
- compel negotiation;
- bind non-participants;
- appoint Treaty institutions;
- activate emergency or coercive powers.

## 5.3 Review dimensions

The Panel publishes separate findings on:

- pilot rights and outcomes;
- transferability and failure evidence;
- founder, funder, vendor, and evaluator concentration;
- accessibility and multilingual capacity;
- digital and offline participation;
- privacy and data sovereignty;
- Indigenous relationship and non-interference;
- financial and administrative readiness;
- legal readiness for negotiation;
- public understanding and contestation;
- unresolved authority claims;
- capacity for orderly failure and wind-down.

No composite score determines readiness.

## 5.4 State Estimate Record and Independence Map

The Panel publishes:

- at least two plausible interpretations of the evidence;
- confidence and uncertainty;
- known missing information;
- dissenting panel views;
- an Observation Independence Map showing shared data, funding, personnel, and institutional dependencies.

## 5.5 Possible findings

The Panel may conclude:

1. invite formal negotiation;
2. continue demonstrations and reassess;
3. narrow the proposed Treaty scope;
4. redesign the facilitation architecture;
5. pause because rights or authority disputes are unresolved;
6. dissolve the founding initiative without successor.

A positive finding authorizes only an invitation to negotiate.

# 6. Voluntary founding coalition and Plural Founding Compact

## 6.1 Voluntary coalition

States, cities, regions, BAZs, commons institutions, Indigenous peoples where they choose, civil-society organizations, labour and professional bodies, research institutions, and other actors may form a voluntary coalition to explore and negotiate Treaty arrangements.

Participation does not itself imply:

- Treaty accession;
- delegation of public authority;
- acceptance of every GGF framework;
- endorsement of a global government;
- consent to jurisdiction;
- consent by represented or affected populations beyond the participant’s valid mandate.

## 6.2 Participation statuses

The coalition may recognize clearly labelled statuses:

- **Observer** — receives public materials and may attend open sessions;
- **Consultative participant** — submits evidence, proposals, and critiques;
- **Pilot participant** — hosts or supports a locally authorized demonstration;
- **Negotiating participant** — holds a valid mandate to negotiate text;
- **Prospective Party** — intends to consider ratification under its constitutional process;
- **Technical service provider** — performs an administrative or expert function without political standing.

Statuses confer only the powers stated in their participation records.

## 6.3 Plural Founding Compact

The coalition adopts a **Plural Founding Compact** recording separate authorization channels rather than merging them into one sovereign.

The Compact recognizes:

- public-party negotiating mandates;
- jurisdiction-specific democratic authorization;
- Indigenous consent, refusal, and non-engagement through each affected people’s own process;
- lawful compacts for cities, BAZs, commons bodies, and other public participants;
- voluntary civil-society and practitioner participation;
- separate ratification and activation of coercive powers.

## 6.4 No GGF veto or canonical-text privilege

Once the negotiating conference accepts custody of the process:

- the GGF source draft becomes one proposal among others;
- authors have no veto over amendments;
- repository maintainers have no power to determine authentic law;
- donors and vendors have no negotiating vote unless separately mandated;
- the conference may rename, restructure, reject, or replace the proposal.

## 6.5 Attraction without exclusion

Genesis may make useful infrastructure available to participants where capacity and interoperability permit.

Every access condition must state:

- the operational capability required;
- why it is necessary;
- whether an equivalent local implementation qualifies;
- what assistance is available;
- whether the condition is technical, financial, legal, or political;
- what humanitarian and rights minima remain protected;
- how the condition may be challenged.

Non-participation does not trigger retaliation, generalized trade penalties, reputational downgrading, or loss of individual material rights.

## 6.6 Lawful interface adoption

“Permeation” is not used to justify informal institutional infiltration or action without legal authority.

Existing institutions may adopt GGF-compatible interfaces only through:

- lawful internal authorization;
- accountable ownership;
- transparent procurement;
- narrow data and operational scope;
- worker and whistleblower protection;
- termination and data-return rules;
- no transfer of institutional authority to the GGF.

# 7. Indigenous relationship, non-creation, and non-engagement

## 7.1 Recognition without institutional manufacture

Genesis recognizes that Indigenous sovereignty is inherent and does not arise from the GGF, the Treaty, a state, a global vote, or participation in a founding coalition.

Genesis does not create, name, select, certify, fund into existence, or presume:

- a universal Earth Council;
- a single global Indigenous chamber;
- a universal Indigenous representative;
- a representation quota that substitutes for sovereignty;
- a “Traditional Knowledge proxy” for places where Indigenous participation is absent;
- an empty Indigenous seat whose absence can be symbolically counted as consent.

## 7.2 Indigenous-created interfaces

Indigenous peoples and nations may, through their own processes:

- establish bilateral or multilateral interfaces with prospective Parties;
- create councils, confederations, networks, delegations, or no common body;
- authorize observation or consultation without consenting to Treaty jurisdiction;
- define protected knowledge and disclosure boundaries;
- negotiate covenants concerning territory, commons, rights, and responsibilities;
- refuse or withdraw from engagement.

Genesis records such interfaces but does not authenticate Indigenous legitimacy beyond the terms provided by the people concerned.

## 7.3 Affected-nation authority

For actions affecting Indigenous territory, law, rights, knowledge, culture, identity, sacred relations, or custodial responsibilities, the relevant authority is determined by the affected people or nation—not by a numerical sample of Indigenous participants elsewhere.

Support from one or many Indigenous peoples does not authorize action affecting another.

## 7.4 Non-engagement as a protected status

Non-engagement means:

- no representation claimed;
- no consent inferred;
- no symbolic empty seat;
- no reduction of rights;
- no exclusion from otherwise applicable protections;
- no use of absence to validate unrelated Treaty jurisdiction;
- no pressure through funding, publicity, deadlines, or economic dependence.

## 7.5 Indigenous Interface and Non-Engagement Record

Where Indigenous interests may be implicated, Genesis creates a record stating:

1. the affected people or peoples identified by themselves or through a contested-standing record;
2. the authority or process named by each people;
3. the subject and territorial scope;
4. whether engagement is observation, dialogue, negotiation, consent, compact, or refusal;
5. knowledge and data restrictions;
6. internal plurality and dissent;
7. duration and withdrawal;
8. unresolved standing conflicts;
9. what the record does not authorize.

## 7.6 Reparative and relationship-building funding

The GGF may advocate reparations and may help raise voluntary funds. It cannot unilaterally determine:

- one global Indigenous beneficiary;
- the correct amount of reparation;
- a universal custodian;
- allocation among peoples;
- a required relationship between funding and Treaty participation.

Any reparative funding instrument must:

- have a valid legal and financial basis;
- be non-conditional on political support or interface creation;
- use Indigenous-selected custodianship and allocation processes;
- preserve non-participation;
- separate donor reporting from knowledge extraction;
- state whether it is reparation, restitution, unrestricted support, or programme funding.

## 7.7 Protected non-transfer

Indigenous authorities may communicate:

- consent;
- refusal;
- a condition;
- an attestation;
- a territorial or legal boundary;
- a finding that consultation was inadequate;

without disclosing the knowledge, ceremony, deliberation, or sacred relation underlying the decision.

Open-source, audit, transparency, and institutional-memory duties do not override protected non-transfer.

# 8. Constituent public deliberation and authorization

## 8.1 No fictitious world electorate

Genesis does not claim that humanity already forms one legally constituted electorate.

A transnational public vote, survey, platform, or citizens’ assembly may express support, opposition, proposals, or understanding. It does not bind a rejecting jurisdiction unless valid law has already established its electorate, administration, jurisdiction, and legal effect.

## 8.2 Constituent Public Authorization Portfolio

Public authorization is documented through a portfolio of constituted processes, which may include:

- national or subnational referendums;
- legislative ratification;
- constitutional conventions;
- citizens’ assemblies informing ratification;
- municipal or BAZ public decisions within lawful competence;
- public hearings and consultations;
- Indigenous processes separate from state public authorization;
- civil-society and professional endorsements with clearly limited effect.

No aggregate global percentage overrides the constitutional requirements of participating jurisdictions.

## 8.3 Transnational deliberative forum

The coalition may convene a transnational deliberative forum to:

- compare experiences across jurisdictions;
- identify missing populations;
- deliberate proposed Treaty provisions;
- publish majority and minority recommendations;
- test accessibility and comprehension;
- propose amendments to negotiators.

The forum is consultative unless a valid constituting instrument assigns a more specific role.

## 8.4 Civic participation and identity

No participant is required to use the Love Ledger, hold a contribution record, possess a smartphone, or demonstrate social usefulness.

A Civic Participation and Identity Protocol must provide:

- offline and physical participation;
- low-documentation pathways;
- stateless and displaced-person access;
- disability accommodation;
- multilingual support;
- privacy and data minimization;
- duplicate-prevention appropriate to the process;
- correction and challenge;
- protection from contribution, reputation, wealth, and employment tests;
- alternatives where legal identity systems are unsafe or exclusionary.

## 8.5 Deliberative record

Public summaries must distinguish:

- the proposal or decision;
- legal effect;
- majority rationale;
- minority and dissenting rationales;
- evidence and uncertainty;
- affected-party accounts;
- independent adversarial review;
- plain-language and culturally adapted summaries;
- correction history;
- unresolved questions.

No summary is labelled “the people’s rationale” merely because a selected sample produced it.

## 8.6 Cultural and jurisdictional interface

Where decision forms, ceremony, time, language, or authority differ, participants may create a Cultural and Jurisdictional Interface Record identifying:

- participating authorities and traditions;
- applicable law and decision forms;
- protected practices and knowledge;
- translation limits;
- acceptable mediation;
- refusal and non-participation;
- unresolved-state procedures.

Genesis does not impose a universal typology of Indigenous, Western, African, Asian, religious, or other cultures.

# 9. Treaty negotiation, drafting, and adoption

## 9.1 Negotiating conference

Formal Treaty negotiation begins only when participating actors with valid mandates establish a negotiating conference or equivalent process.

The conference adopts its own rules covering:

- membership and credentials;
- observers and consultative participants;
- agenda control;
- drafting committees;
- public access and protected sessions;
- translation and authentic working texts;
- conflict-of-interest disclosure;
- Indigenous interfaces;
- affected-party standing;
- amendment and adoption of the negotiating text;
- dissent and reservations;
- replacement or dismissal of administrative service providers.

## 9.2 Mandate verification

Each negotiating participant supplies a **Voluntary Coalition Participation Record** stating:

- who authorized participation;
- the scope of the mandate;
- whom the participant may and may not represent;
- whether the mandate includes negotiation, adoption, signature, or only observation;
- reporting and consultation duties;
- expiry and withdrawal.

## 9.3 Custody of the text

Upon establishment of the negotiating conference:

- the working text is held under conference authority;
- the GGF repository remains a historical and public-development source;
- the conference selects an independent document custodian;
- all amendments and version histories are preserved;
- no private trademark, domain, or repository right determines authentic Treaty language;
- authentic texts are later determined under Treaty v1.3.

## 9.4 Open drafting with protected limits

Draft Treaty provisions, comments, alternatives, and dissent should be public by default.

Disclosure may be bounded for:

- personal privacy;
- Indigenous protected knowledge;
- legitimate security needs;
- confidential mediation;
- whistleblower protection;
- sensitive diplomatic negotiation.

A protected session must record its authority, purpose, participants, duration, and what can later be disclosed.

## 9.5 Adoption is not ratification

Adoption of a final negotiating text means only that the conference has produced a text for signature, ratification, accession, or further domestic consideration.

It does not:

- bind non-ratifying participants;
- activate Treaty institutions;
- create taxation, judicial, sanction, or deployment authority;
- convert consultative public support into legal consent;
- authorize action affecting Indigenous peoples without the relevant process.

## 9.6 Independent depositary preparation

Before adoption, negotiating participants identify a proposed depositary or equivalent record authority independent of the GGF authors and facilitation consortium.

The depositary’s functions, after lawful appointment, may include:

- custody of authentic texts;
- receipt of signatures, ratifications, accessions, reservations, and withdrawals;
- publication of status records;
- verification of entry-into-force conditions;
- no substantive reinterpretation of the Treaty.

# 10. Ratification, accession, and entry into force

## 10.1 Treaty control

Ratification, accession, reservations, withdrawal, entry into force, provisional application, and limited initial activation are governed entirely by Treaty for Our Only Home v1.3 and applicable law.

Genesis does not create an additional activation threshold.

## 10.2 Jurisdiction-specific public authorization

Each prospective Party determines, under its constitutional order, whether Treaty participation requires:

- legislative approval;
- referendum;
- executive signature plus ratification;
- regional or federal consent;
- judicial review;
- other public authorization.

Genesis records the process but does not certify domestic constitutional validity where it lacks lawful standing.

## 10.3 Indigenous consent and Treaty ratification are distinct

State ratification does not extinguish Indigenous rights or replace affected-nation consent where Treaty action implicates Indigenous jurisdiction.

Indigenous participation, non-participation, compact authorization, and affected-nation consent remain distinct from state ratification.

## 10.4 Treaty Ratification and Activation Matrix

The depositary or another Treaty-authorized body maintains a matrix showing:

- signature status;
- ratification or accession;
- reservations and declarations;
- domestic implementation required;
- entry-into-force status;
- limited initial powers activated;
- powers awaiting separate protocols;
- applicable regional or issue-specific compacts;
- withdrawal notices;
- unresolved validity disputes.

## 10.5 Independent verification

No GGF author, Consortium officer, donor, software system, or dashboard may declare the Treaty in force.

Entry into force is verified by the lawful depositary under the Treaty’s stated conditions, with public evidence and a challenge procedure.

## 10.6 Non-Parties and dissenting publics

Non-Parties are not bound merely because the Treaty has entered into force for Parties.

Within Parties, dissent, abstention, and opposition remain lawful unless conduct independently violates valid law. Genesis does not treat political disagreement as a legitimacy defect or capture signal.

# 11. Pre-entry administrative coordination and emergency referral

## 11.1 Administrative boundary

Before Treaty entry into force, the Consortium or another authorized service body may perform only voluntary and legally grounded administrative functions.

It may not exercise powers that presuppose Treaty jurisdiction.

## 11.2 Permitted pre-entry functions

A valid Pre-Entry Administrative Delegation may authorize:

- meeting and translation logistics;
- public-information services;
- voluntary expert coordination;
- consent-based data sharing;
- administration of grants and contracts;
- support to existing lawful emergency bodies;
- preservation of evidence;
- readiness planning;
- referral of alleged harms or emergencies to competent authorities;
- maintenance of non-binding contact networks.

## 11.3 Prohibited pre-entry functions

No pre-entry body may:

- adjudicate rights, Treaty disputes, or crimes;
- issue binding orders to states, communities, institutions, or individuals;
- levy compulsory contributions;
- impose sanctions;
- deploy armed, police, or coercive personnel;
- suspend rights;
- classify itself as a global emergency authority;
- create permanent emergency institutions;
- appoint a judge or enforcement director under Treaty law.

A delegator cannot grant powers it does not possess.

## 11.4 Emergency referral protocol

Where an urgent threat arises before entry into force, the pre-entry coordinator:

1. identifies the threat and uncertainty;
2. identifies competent existing authorities;
3. informs affected populations where safe;
4. facilitates voluntary information sharing;
5. offers pre-authorized logistical or humanitarian support;
6. preserves evidence under lawful procedures;
7. records referrals and responses;
8. avoids implying that referral creates jurisdiction.

## 11.5 Pre-authorized humanitarian grants

Voluntary funds may support humanitarian action before Treaty entry into force where:

- the fund instrument permits it;
- the receiving body has lawful authority;
- beneficiaries are not required to support the Treaty;
- aid is not conditioned on data extraction, political participation, or framework adoption;
- procurement, safety, and audit rules are defined.

## 11.6 No transitional emergency directors

Genesis does not create emergency directors, interim judges, or a provisional global enforcement mechanism.

After Treaty entry into force, any emergency measure follows Treaty v1.3 Article 17 and the relevant separately activated protocols.

# 12. Lawful first seating of Treaty institutions

## 12.1 First-seating principle

Genesis may prepare administrative plans for the first seating of an institution. It may not supply the institution’s constitutional authority.

Every **Institution First-Seating Record** must identify:

1. the Treaty article or ratified protocol creating the institution;
2. the powers active at first seating;
3. the appointing or constituting authority;
4. nomination channels;
5. qualification criteria;
6. conflict, independence, and accessibility rules;
7. public notice and challenge;
8. confirmation procedure;
9. term, removal, and vacancy rules;
10. data, budget, staff, and premises;
11. functions not yet activated;
12. start date and handoff source.

## 12.2 Assembly of Parties

The lawful depositary invites Parties to submit credentials under Treaty v1.3 Article 8.

A temporary credentials process may verify formal authorization. It does not determine the legitimacy of a state, government, or disputed territory beyond its assigned mandate.

## 12.3 Peoples’ Chamber

The transitional Peoples’ Chamber is seated under Treaty v1.3 Article 9 and a separate Civic Participation and Identity Protocol.

Selection must not depend on:

- Love Ledger identity;
- contribution histories;
- wealth or employment;
- digital-only access;
- ideological endorsement of the GGF.

Its first-seating record distinguishes deliberative, legislative, appointment, and consultative functions.

## 12.4 Global Council for the Future

The first-seating plan states how expertise, affected experience, geographic diversity, youth and future-generations standing, uncertainty, and conflicts of interest are handled under Treaty authority.

The Council remains advisory unless the Treaty expressly assigns another function.

## 12.5 Council of Guardians

Guardians may perform the limited reconsideration and principle-review functions provided by Treaty v1.3.

They do not become:

- a sovereign council;
- a general appointing authority;
- an emergency executive;
- an amendment veto-holder beyond the Treaty;
- a substitute for Indigenous authority;
- a fallback government when institutions fail.

## 12.6 Office of the Adversary and Equity Ombudsperson

Their first-seating procedures protect:

- independence from institutions they review;
- access to information subject to protected limits;
- public reporting;
- whistleblower protection;
- no power to convert criticism into punishment without lawful adjudication.

## 12.7 Meta-Governance Coordination Council

The Meta-Governance Coordination Council is seated only for the delegated functions in Treaty v1.3 and Integrated Meta-Governance v1.5.

It may coordinate interfaces, convene competent authorities, maintain records, and facilitate mediation.

It may not:

- decide jurisdiction;
- amend the Treaty;
- charter substantive regulators without authority;
- supervise every framework;
- transfer or dissolve institutions through its own diagnosis;
- select a successor government.

## 12.8 Arbitration and mediation

Mediation bodies may help parties resolve disputes voluntarily. Binding adjudication requires jurisdiction accepted under Treaty or other valid law.

## 12.9 Indigenous interfaces

There is no universal Indigenous chamber to seat.

Treaty institutions establish interfaces only with Indigenous-created or Indigenous-authorized bodies and only within the scope of the relevant agreement or affected-nation process.

## 12.10 Judicial and enforcement institutions

A tribunal, criminal jurisdiction, compulsory sanctions system, forensic body with compulsory powers, or deployment service is not seated under this base Protocol unless the relevant separately ratified Treaty protocol is in force.

Genesis may maintain a placeholder first-seating plan marked **inactive**.

## 12.11 Specialized and practitioner institutions

Implementation experience may support practitioner nomination and learning. It does not automatically create public regulatory authority.

Each specialized body distinguishes:

- advice;
- professional self-governance;
- technical standards;
- public regulation;
- financial administration;
- adjudication;
- operational coordination.

The source of authority for each function is stated separately.

# 13. Coercive-protocol non-activation and limited initial authority

## 13.1 Powers inactive under Genesis alone

The following powers remain inactive unless and until separately authorized under Treaty v1.3 and applicable law:

- compulsory Treaty taxation;
- criminal jurisdiction;
- compulsory sanctions;
- armed or police deployment;
- compulsory seizure or asset freezing;
- permanent transfer of public jurisdiction;
- binding emergency orders beyond valid existing law;
- involuntary data collection beyond applicable law;
- authority to invalidate an institution or economy.

## 13.2 Coercive-Protocol Non-Activation Register

The depositary maintains a public register stating for each proposed coercive power:

- governing Treaty article;
- required protocol;
- ratification status;
- Parties bound;
- implementing law required;
- institutions not yet seatable;
- interim prohibition;
- review and sunset requirements;
- protected rights and continuity.

Silence or technical readiness is not activation.

## 13.3 Limited initial activation

At Treaty entry into force, only the powers expressly permitted by Treaty v1.3 Article 24 activate.

The initial order should prioritize:

- deliberation;
- coordination;
- public information;
- voluntary compacts;
- funding already lawfully contributed;
- independent review;
- preparation of additional protocols.

## 13.4 No emergency shortcut

An emergency cannot activate a coercive protocol that has never been lawfully constituted.

Existing lawful authorities remain responsible before activation. Treaty bodies may coordinate within their active powers and may invite accelerated lawful ratification, but may not treat urgency as a substitute for authority.

# 14. Genesis finance, reparative commitments, and asset custody

## 14.1 Financial non-authority

Before Treaty entry into force, all Genesis funding is voluntary or derived from existing lawful public budgets.

Genesis cannot self-authorize:

- a Peace Tithe;
- a global tax;
- assessed contributions;
- compulsory military-budget transfers;
- sovereign-debt conversion;
- mandatory Hearts or Leaves integration;
- a Global Commons Fund levy.

Those mechanisms require the authority, protocols, and financial architecture specified by Treaty v1.3 and Financial Systems v3.4.

## 14.2 Genesis Finance Plan

Scenario budgets, fundraising targets, staffing assumptions, pilot allocations, and proposed future revenue are maintained in a separate **Genesis Finance Plan** rather than treated as constitutional facts.

The Plan states:

- legal fund holders;
- expected and committed resources;
- restricted and unrestricted funds;
- donor conditions;
- expenditure ceilings;
- currency and settlement arrangements;
- audit and reporting;
- uncertainty and funding gaps;
- scale-down priorities;
- no-ratification outcomes.

Changing a planning assumption does not amend constitutional legitimacy.

## 14.3 Donor independence

Donors may not receive, formally or informally:

- negotiating or appointment seats;
- pilot-selection control;
- privileged access to participant data;
- future procurement preference;
- veto over negative evaluation;
- authority to define Indigenous beneficiaries;
- naming rights that imply constitutional ownership;
- control of the public narrative or canonical Treaty text.

All material donor relationships and intermediaries are disclosed.

## 14.4 Genesis Fund Custody and Continuity Record

Every fund used for Genesis purposes records:

1. legal owner and fiduciary;
2. governing law and instrument;
3. authorized purposes;
4. donor restrictions and conflicts;
5. beneficiaries and standing;
6. procurement rules;
7. participant and worker claims;
8. personal balances or entitlements protected;
9. audit and insolvency provisions;
10. transfer conditions after Treaty entry;
11. return, redirection, or wind-down if the Treaty fails;
12. records and privacy;
13. complaint and remedy.

## 14.5 AUBI boundary

Genesis may support lawful AUBI pilots or recognize material security as an enabling condition for participation.

It may not:

- make AUBI Layer 1 conditional on Treaty support;
- withdraw material support from opponents or non-participants;
- portray recipients as obligated transition agents;
- combine AUBI identity with civic voting or candidate data;
- promise full AUBI deployment from funds that cannot support it;
- treat AUBI adoption as automatic consent to other GGF frameworks.

## 14.6 Hearts, Leaves, and complementary currencies

Locally authorized Hearts, Leaves, or other complementary-currency demonstrations defer to Financial Systems v3.4, AUBI v2.5, Nested Economies v3.2, and applicable law.

Pilot balances, wages, provider claims, redemption expectations, and exit rights must be protected during wind-down. Participation in a currency pilot does not create Treaty or political consent.

## 14.7 Sovereign debt

Sovereign Debt Transformation remains inactive pending a dedicated lawful protocol addressing authority, debtor consent, affected-community consent, creditor treatment, baselines, additionality, ecological permanence, distribution, adjudication, and exit.

It is not advertised as a guaranteed Treaty Partner benefit during Genesis.

## 14.8 Reparative commitments

Prospective Parties and donors may make reparative commitments before entry into force through valid legal instruments.

Such commitments should distinguish:

- legal reparation;
- restitution;
- unrestricted solidarity funding;
- research or programme grants;
- prospective Treaty obligations.

They must not purchase consent, representation, or Treaty participation.

## 14.9 Transfer after entry into force

Genesis assets do not automatically become Treaty assets.

Transfer requires:

- a lawful receiving institution;
- authority to accept the asset;
- fulfilment of donor and beneficiary rights;
- independent valuation and reconciliation;
- data and privacy review;
- public transfer record;
- continuity for workers, participants, and valid claims.

# 15. Digital infrastructure, data sovereignty, and institutional memory

## 15.1 Technology neutrality

Genesis requires no particular blockchain, digital identity, AI system, cryptographic token, cloud provider, or distributed architecture.

Technology is selected according to:

- purpose;
- security;
- accessibility;
- cost;
- local capacity;
- interoperability;
- privacy;
- resilience;
- exit and migration;
- environmental impact.

Low-tech and offline operation must remain possible for core participatory functions.

## 15.2 Data separation

The following data pipelines remain distinguishable:

- pilot operations;
- public deliberation;
- identity and duplicate prevention;
- candidate qualification;
- funding and procurement;
- evaluation;
- complaints and whistleblowing;
- Indigenous or community-controlled information;
- Treaty ratification records;
- institutional first seating.

Data collected for one purpose is not automatically available for another.

## 15.3 No universal civic or reputation ledger

Genesis does not use:

- Love Ledger records;
- contribution histories;
- generalized trust scores;
- social graphs;
- employment records;
- Hearts or Leaves activity;

as a universal identity, voting, eligibility, candidate, loyalty, or reputation system.

## 15.4 Data rights

Participants receive, subject to lawful limits:

- notice of collection and purpose;
- access;
- correction;
- deletion or retention explanation;
- portability where appropriate;
- opt-out from non-essential processing;
- human review;
- complaint and remedy;
- protection from adverse inference based on sparse data or non-participation.

## 15.5 Indigenous and community data sovereignty

Where an Indigenous people or community controls knowledge or data, the relevant authority determines:

- whether collection occurs;
- storage location;
- access;
- permitted analysis;
- retention;
- publication;
- withdrawal;
- destruction or return;
- bounded attestations.

A transparency obligation does not override this authority.

## 15.6 Federated institutional memory

Genesis preserves a record of:

- decisions and mandates;
- versions and amendments;
- dissent and uncertainty;
- funding and conflicts;
- pilot outcomes and failures;
- authority-provenance claims;
- unresolved disputes;
- handoffs and dissolutions.

The record is federated and purpose-limited. No central system is entitled to complete visibility.

## 15.7 Open-source boundary

Software, public procedures, non-sensitive models, and adopted public texts should be open where feasible.

Open-source duties do not compel disclosure of:

- personal data;
- sacred or restricted knowledge;
- confidential mediation;
- legitimate security information;
- whistleblower identity;
- community-controlled information;
- proprietary material that participants were not authorized to publish.

## 15.8 Cybersecurity and continuity

Security design includes:

- threat modelling;
- least privilege;
- multi-party control of critical credentials;
- independent testing;
- incident disclosure;
- offline recovery;
- vendor-exit plans;
- backup and migration;
- protection against founders retaining hidden access;
- continuity if a platform, funder, or provider fails.

“Quantum-resistant” or other technical claims are treated as implementation assessments, not constitutional legitimacy.

# 16. Integrity, observation, appointments, and mandate drift

## 16.1 Observation does not confer authority

Metrics, audits, pattern detection, AI systems, surveys, and dashboards may reveal conditions requiring attention. They do not themselves determine:

- capture;
- fraud;
- illegitimacy;
- constitutional invalidity;
- jurisdiction;
- guilt;
- removal;
- process reset;
- institutional dissolution.

## 16.2 Correct intervention sequence

For a consequential integrity concern, Genesis uses the following sequence:

1. **Alert** — an observer reports a pattern or allegation.
2. **Observation Contract** — the decision and required evidence are specified.
3. **State estimates** — independent reviewers publish competing interpretations, confidence, and missing information.
4. **Notice** — affected persons and bodies receive the evidence.
5. **Response** — contextual evidence, correction, and dissent are admitted.
6. **Interim protection** — only narrow reversible measures are used where delay risks serious harm and a valid authority permits them.
7. **Authorized decision** — a body with lawful standing decides.
8. **Appeal** — affected parties may challenge.
9. **Outcome review** — independent evaluation assesses the intervention.

## 16.3 Genesis Conditions Dashboard

The dashboard reports separate dimensions rather than one health or legitimacy score:

- legal and authority readiness;
- pilot rights and outcomes;
- public authorization;
- Indigenous relationship and non-interference;
- founder, funder, and vendor concentration;
- financial custody;
- technical accessibility;
- privacy and data sovereignty;
- institutional independence;
- handoff readiness;
- emergency-power absence or containment;
- unresolved disputes;
- missing populations;
- exit and wind-down capacity.

No dashboard threshold activates constitutional authority.

## 16.4 Founding Intent Record

Each founding or transitional body records:

- initial purpose;
- scope;
- prohibited powers;
- assumptions;
- expected dependencies;
- rights and red lines;
- unresolved questions;
- reasons for creation;
- handoff or dissolution conditions.

The record preserves memory but is not an immutable expression of founder wisdom.

## 16.5 Institutional Mandate and Dependency Review

A periodic review compares:

- founding purpose;
- current lawful mandate;
- actual activities;
- affected populations;
- dependencies and exit costs;
- alleged unauthorized powers;
- outcome evidence;
- dissenting interpretations;
- proposed narrowing, amendment, federation, restoration, or dissolution.

Unauthorized action may be provisionally stopped only by a competent authority applying due process—not by an automatic drift count.

## 16.6 Candidate review separation

Candidate processes distinguish:

- identity and credential verification;
- role qualification;
- conflict-of-interest investigation;
- security review where lawful and necessary;
- evidence of misconduct;
- political or democratic confirmation.

Each function has published criteria, notice, correction, response, accommodation, proportional evidence, and appeal.

There is no “common sense authenticity” tribunal or unreviewed citizen disqualification power.

## 16.7 Independent challenge

Before Treaty entry, independent auditors, affected-party advocates, journalists, civil-society reviewers, and an external ombuds function may challenge Genesis claims under their own lawful mandates.

After entry, the Office of the Adversary, Equity Ombudsperson, courts, audit bodies, and other Treaty institutions act only within their assigned powers.

## 16.8 Process pauses

A pre-entry process may be paused only under a rule established before the concern arose, identifying:

- the authorized actor;
- evidentiary threshold;
- maximum duration;
- scope;
- continuity protections;
- response and appeal;
- conditions for resumption or termination.

A pause cannot create new jurisdiction or become an indefinite substitute for a decision.

# 17. Founder separation, control continuity, and handoff

## 17.1 Control continuity rather than headcount alone

Founder capture is not prevented merely because founders leave formal office.

The relevant question is who controls:

- funds;
- domains and trademarks;
- code repositories;
- signing keys and credentials;
- communication channels;
- data and candidate lists;
- donor and vendor relationships;
- legal advice and privileged records;
- institutional memory;
- agenda and drafting tools;
- successor selection.

## 17.2 Founder, Funder, Vendor, and Affiliate Disclosure

The Consortium maintains a current disclosure of:

- formal founders and officers;
- paid and unpaid core contributors;
- affiliated legal entities;
- donors and intermediaries;
- vendors and subcontractors;
- related-party transactions;
- intellectual-property holders;
- administrators of critical systems;
- family, employment, financial, and governance conflicts.

## 17.3 Independent transition trustee

Before formal Treaty adoption, negotiating participants appoint or authorize an independent transition trustee, depositary office, or equivalent lawful custodian to supervise the eventual handoff.

The custodian is not selected solely by the founders or the successor body the founders designed.

## 17.4 Founder Separation and Handoff Record

The handoff records:

1. all assets and liabilities;
2. funds and restricted obligations;
3. domains, repositories, trademarks, licenses, and documentation;
4. access credentials and key rotation;
5. data controllers, processors, retention, and deletion;
6. donor, vendor, employment, and service contracts;
7. candidate, participant, and stakeholder databases;
8. public communication channels;
9. decision logs, dissents, and unresolved disputes;
10. privileged legal and security information;
11. functions terminating;
12. functions transferred and their authority source;
13. protected records not transferred;
14. worker and participant continuity;
15. independent verification.

## 17.5 Personnel transition

There is no automatic right of founders to permanent roles and no single universal percentage that guarantees independence.

Role-specific rules may include:

- open recruitment;
- independent nomination;
- cooling-off periods;
- conflict-based exclusion;
- non-renewable transitional terms;
- limits on concentration in legal, treasury, communications, procurement, technical, and agenda-setting roles;
- preservation of technical memory without preserving control.

## 17.6 No Catalyst Team recurrence

The Protocol does not create a successor “Catalyst Team” or general coherence body after dissolving the Consortium.

Necessary administrative functions pass to the specific Treaty institutions authorized to perform them. The GGF design commons may continue as independent civil society without privileged access.

## 17.7 Narrative and authorship handoff

Official Treaty communications after entry are governed by Treaty institutions.

Founders remain free to explain, criticize, or advocate as private or civil-society actors, but may not present their interpretation as the official constitutional meaning solely because they authored earlier drafts.

# 18. Dissolution, failure, withdrawal, and retrospective

## 18.1 Genesis may legitimately fail

The process must permit the conclusions that:

- demonstrations are not sufficiently beneficial;
- harms exceed benefits;
- the Treaty scope is too broad;
- public authorization is inadequate;
- Indigenous authority disputes remain unresolved;
- financing is unsafe or insufficient;
- negotiations should pause;
- the Treaty should not enter into force;
- the founding initiative should dissolve without successor.

Failure to create the Treaty is not a constitutional emergency.

## 18.2 Dissolution triggers

The Consortium’s constitutional-facilitation mandate ends upon the earliest applicable condition:

- the negotiating conference replaces it as service provider;
- Treaty entry into force and completion of the authorized handoff;
- termination under its charter;
- loss of lawful funding or capacity;
- an independent finding of unremedied serious rights or integrity failure;
- failure to reach negotiation or ratification milestones within a charter-defined review period;
- participant decision to discontinue the effort.

The GGF design commons may continue, but not as a privileged founding authority.

## 18.3 Orderly wind-down

Wind-down protects:

- participant rights;
- worker compensation and notice;
- pilot continuity or safe closure;
- personal balances and lawful claims;
- data return, deletion, or preservation;
- Indigenous and community-controlled records;
- restricted donor funds;
- open-source materials;
- pending complaints and remediation;
- evidence of alleged harm.

## 18.4 Withdrawal and non-retaliation

Participants may withdraw according to their governing instrument.

Withdrawal does not erase valid surviving obligations such as:

- compensation owed;
- harms caused;
- confidentiality;
- evidence preservation;
- agreed transition duties;
- lawful adjudication already accepted;
- participant and worker protection.

Those obligations are not penalties for leaving.

Genesis imposes no frozen seat, generalized reputation penalty, financial retaliation, or loss of unrelated rights for withdrawal.

## 18.5 Founding Body Dissolution Certificate

An independent custodian issues a public certificate confirming:

- authority terminated;
- assets reconciled;
- credentials revoked or transferred;
- contracts closed or assigned;
- data disposition completed;
- workers and participants notified;
- unresolved claims preserved;
- records archived with protected limits;
- no automatic successor centre created.

## 18.6 Genesis retrospective

After handoff or dissolution, independent evaluators and affected communities produce a **Genesis Retrospective and Unresolved Questions Register**.

It includes:

- what worked and under what conditions;
- harms, exclusions, and failures;
- founder and funder influence;
- communities that declined or withdrew;
- authority disputes;
- institutional mechanisms abandoned;
- costs and opportunity costs;
- whether handoff actually reduced founder control;
- recommendations that future actors may accept or reject.

Affected communities control disclosure of their protected information.

# 19. Review, amendment, and protocol forks

## 19.1 Pre-entry procedural amendment

Before Treaty entry into force, the Consortium may amend only its voluntary administrative procedures under its charter.

It cannot amend itself into possession of:

- public authority;
- Treaty jurisdiction;
- compulsory funding;
- Indigenous representation;
- emergency command;
- permanent institutional status.

Material amendments publish an updated Authority Provenance Record, impact assessment, dissent, and effective date.

## 19.2 Treaty amendment after entry

After Treaty entry into force:

- Treaty amendments follow Treaty v1.3 Article 21;
- institutional mandates follow the Treaty and relevant protocols;
- Genesis does not operate a competing amendment constitution;
- Meta-Governance and Guardians have only the amendment roles expressly assigned by the Treaty.

## 19.3 No emergency amendment shortcut

Genesis has no power to make emergency constitutional amendments.

Emergency action is narrow, temporary, and governed by valid law. It cannot alter the rules that determine constitutional authority or extend a pre-entry body’s mandate.

## 19.4 Protected principles and amendable design

The following are governing safeguards rather than claims that the founders are infallible:

- no GGF self-authorization;
- no authority from demonstrated competence alone;
- no aggregation of Indigenous sovereignties;
- no fictitious electorate;
- no undelegated coercive power;
- no AUBI political conditionality;
- no automatic metric-to-constitutional judgment;
- no founder control continuity;
- no punitive exit;
- ability to stop Genesis.

The implementation of these safeguards remains open to correction through lawful processes.

## 19.5 Forks and alternatives

The GGF source materials may be forked, adapted, translated, or rejected under their licenses.

A fork does not inherit:

- negotiating status;
- Treaty authenticity;
- participant mandates;
- Indigenous consent;
- donor funds;
- institutional credentials;
- constitutional authority.

Authority must be independently constituted for each process.

## 19.6 Periodic protocol review

During active Genesis phases, a review occurs at each phase gate and at least annually. It examines:

- whether the Protocol remains necessary;
- whether functions should move to another lawful body;
- authority-provenance defects;
- rights and exclusion;
- founder and funder concentration;
- unresolved disputes;
- capacity for dissolution.

# 20. Phase architecture, gates, and falsification tests

## 20.1 Phase A — Open design and preparation

### Phase A activities

- open-source framework development;
- public critique and translation;
- formation or appointment of a lawful facilitation consortium;
- founder, funder, vendor, and affiliate disclosure;
- preparation of pilot contracts and finance plans.

### Gate to Phase B

- Consortium charter validly adopted;
- prohibited powers explicit;
- Authority Provenance Record system operational;
- complaint and audit routes established;
- no claim of public or constitutional authority.

## 20.2 Phase B — Demonstration and learning

### Phase B activities

- locally authorized pilots;
- independent evaluation;
- Failure Library with protected disclosure;
- testing of offline, privacy, exit, and continuity mechanisms;
- public learning without political-consent claims.

### Gate to Phase C

A portfolio exists sufficient for independent readiness review. No minimum number, geographic quota, currency adoption count, or Indigenous participation percentage is itself constitutional.

## 20.3 Phase C — Founding readiness

### Phase C activities

- independent Founding Readiness State Estimate;
- Observation Independence Map;
- public response and dissent;
- assessment of legal, administrative, accessibility, financial, and wind-down capacity.

### Gate to Phase D

The independent Panel concludes that formal negotiation may responsibly be invited. Participants remain free to decline.

## 20.4 Phase D — Voluntary coalition and Treaty negotiation

### Phase D activities

- mandate verification;
- adoption of a Plural Founding Compact;
- establishment of negotiating rules;
- Indigenous interface and non-engagement records;
- public and affected-party deliberation;
- drafting, adoption, and depositary preparation.

### Gate to Phase E

A final text is adopted for ratification. Adoption creates no Treaty jurisdiction.

## 20.5 Phase E — Ratification and entry into force

### Phase E activities

- jurisdiction-specific public authorization;
- state and other eligible Party ratification or accession;
- reservations and declarations;
- independent depositary verification;
- publication of the Ratification and Activation Matrix.

### Gate to Phase F

Treaty v1.3 entry-into-force conditions are satisfied. Only limited initial powers activate. Coercive protocols remain inactive unless separately ratified.

## 20.6 Phase F — First seating, handoff, and dissolution

### Phase F activities

- institution-specific first-seating records;
- lawful constitution of initial Treaty bodies;
- transfer of authorized assets and records;
- revocation and rotation of founder credentials;
- dissolution of the Consortium’s facilitation mandate;
- independent retrospective.

### Completion condition

The Treaty institutions can operate within their lawful mandates without relying on GGF authors, founders, vendors, repositories, or informal networks for constitutional authority or hidden control.

## 20.7 Phase gates, not calendar inevitability

The phases have no automatic calendar progression. A process may remain in a phase, return to an earlier design stage, narrow its scope, or end.

Time passing does not:

- lower ratification thresholds;
- convert consultation into consent;
- activate missing institutions;
- authorize provisional representatives;
- expand emergency powers;
- make an unresolved authority claim valid.

## 20.8 Core falsification tests

v0.9 is not ready for operational use unless it can pass the following tests:

1. **No pilot-to-jurisdiction conversion:** a successful pilot grants no external authority without a new legal act.
2. **No GGF authority:** every consequential act can identify a non-GGF authority source.
3. **No Indigenous aggregation:** no subset authorizes an interface for non-participating peoples.
4. **No fictitious electorate:** no transnational vote is misrepresented as binding world consent.
5. **No undelegated emergency power:** pre-entry bodies cannot adjudicate, compel, sanction, or deploy.
6. **No founder control continuity:** assets, data, credentials, channels, and contracts are transferred or neutralized.
7. **No Meta-Governance jurisdiction:** coordination does not become appointment, amendment, transfer, or dissolution authority.
8. **No Love Ledger civic identity:** public participation is independent of contribution records.
9. **No AUBI political conditionality:** individual material security does not depend on Treaty alignment.
10. **No automatic capture judgment:** metrics alert but do not constitutionally condemn.
11. **No Guardian fallback government:** review bodies do not become executives when institutions fail.
12. **No competing amendment constitution:** Treaty amendment remains with Treaty v1.3.
13. **No punitive exit:** withdrawal preserves valid duties without retaliation.
14. **No canonical-author privilege:** negotiators may reject or replace GGF text.
15. **Failure can stop Genesis:** the process can dissolve without inventing a successor.

## 20.9 Final legitimacy test

> **Could the demonstrations succeed, the founders dissolve, Indigenous peoples decline engagement, some states refuse ratification, the public remain divided, and an emergency arise before entry into force—and could the Protocol still respond lawfully without inventing a sovereign, lowering a threshold, punishing exit, or converting readiness into authority?**

Where the answer is yes, Genesis functions as a legitimate preparation and convening protocol.

Where the answer is no, it remains a hidden constitution.

# Conclusion

Genesis is not the birth of a sovereign institution from a design document. It is the disciplined preparation of evidence, relationships, public deliberation, lawful mandates, ratification records, first-seating plans, and a founder exit.

The GGF may contribute ideas, labour, code, facilitation, funding, and institutional imagination. None of those contributions grants it public authority.

The Protocol succeeds when:

- pilots remain local unless separately authorized;
- negotiators can reject the source architecture;
- Indigenous peoples can engage, refuse, or remain outside without symbolic substitution;
- publics authorize through constituted processes;
- Parties ratify under their own law;
- the Treaty determines its own entry into force;
- coercive powers remain inactive until separately constituted;
- founders transfer control rather than merely changing titles;
- the process can end without declaring its failure an emergency.

The governing maxim is:

> **Prepare without presuming. Convene without representing. Demonstrate without claiming jurisdiction. Ratify before governing. Transfer before entrenchment. Dissolve when the founding function is complete.**
