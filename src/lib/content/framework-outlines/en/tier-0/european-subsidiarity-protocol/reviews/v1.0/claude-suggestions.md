This is a substantial improvement. The audience separation is clean, the CAF formula fix is correct, the dependency architecture reframe works much better, and the framework revision protocol fills a real gap. Most of what I flagged is addressed well.

Here's what I'd push on next:

**The existing EU subsidiarity infrastructure is invisible.** The EU already has a Subsidiarity Control Mechanism, the Better Regulation agenda, REFIT, and the "yellow card" procedure where national parliaments can flag subsidiarity violations. An EU official's first reaction will be: "How does this relate to what we already do?" The document reads as if there's no existing subsidiarity implementation at all — just "political negotiation." Positioning the GSI as an upgrade to these existing mechanisms (rather than implicitly dismissing them) would significantly improve reception and reduce defensiveness.

**The "efficiency cost" of overriding GSI recommendations is undefined.** The European Semester analogy in Section 3.4 is clever, but the Semester publishes quantifiable deviations from fiscal targets — things denominated in euros and percentage points. What unit does the GSI's "efficiency cost" come in? If a national government overrides an OGT recommendation, what exactly gets published — a score differential? An estimated cost figure? A qualitative assessment? Without specifying this, the transparency mechanism is aspirational rather than operational.

**Phase 1 scope needs narrowing.** The document implies the Parallel Diagnostic runs across the full four-dimension, sixteen-indicator framework in 3-5 regions simultaneously. For €15-25M over 18 months, this is ambitious. A stronger pitch might explicitly select 2-3 policy domains for the initial diagnostic (care policy is the obvious lead candidate given the document's framing) and declare the others as Phase 2 expansions. This also gives you a tighter proof-of-concept narrative: "We tested the GSI on care policy in these regions, and here's what it found" is more compelling than "We ran a broad diagnostic."

**The Phase 1 budget may be undercosted.** Co-developing a novel statistical methodology with Eurostat is itself a multi-year, multi-million euro undertaking — Eurostat's typical framework partnerships run well above €5M. If the estimate feels light to a budget official, it undermines the document's credibility on the more ambitious claims. Better to present a realistic range (even if higher) or explicitly scope what "co-development" means at Phase 1 — perhaps "methodological consultation and feasibility assessment" rather than full co-development.

**The European Parliament is nearly absent.** Enhanced Cooperation under Article 20 TEU requires EP consent. The Parliament appears once as "observers" in the governance table. Given that the EP has historically been the most active institutional defender of subsidiarity (through the Conference of Parliamentary Committees), this is a missed opportunity. The EP should probably appear in the target institutions and as a stakeholder in the transition mechanics, not just as a monitoring body.

**Forkability creates a political weapon problem.** The anti-gaming protocols say any implementation can use alternative weightings. This is scientifically admirable but politically naive — it means competing GSI implementations could produce contradictory routing recommendations, each cited by different actors to support their preferred outcome. Consider constraining forkability to the research/comparison layer while designating the Eurostat-validated version as the "reference implementation" for any policy-relevant use. You can be open-source without being authority-neutral.

**"Recognition infrastructure" remains abstract.** Section 4 explains what the Civic Value Registry proves to institutions, but not what it offers to citizens. What does participation look like from a citizen's perspective? What's the interaction, the incentive to register informal care work? Even one paragraph describing the citizen experience would ground this — otherwise it reads as a data extraction system with nice privacy properties, which is exactly the framing you want to avoid.

**The Resilience Dividend is deliberately vague.** Section 12 describes it as "a localized baseline that decouples citizen economic security from rigid institutional compliance" — this is a UBI-adjacent proposal that avoids the term. An EU policy audience will see through the circumlocution immediately, and the vagueness will read as evasiveness rather than strategic framing. You don't need to specify amounts, but being explicit about the mechanism (cash transfer? tax credit? service entitlement?) and its relationship to existing minimum income schemes would strengthen it. Alternatively, if the Resilience Dividend isn't ready for this document, consider deferring it entirely to the actuation annex rather than half-describing it.

**Minor structural note:** Sections 5 ("What remains unchanged") and 6 ("Co-production with data sovereignty") are both short and could be folded into adjacent sections. Section 5 largely restates constraints from Section 15, and Section 6 restates architecture from Section 4.3. At 367 lines this document is lean, so it's not urgent, but these feel like remnants of the longer v0.7 structure.

Overall this reads like a credible policy proposal now rather than a strategy document wearing a policy costume. The tone is right, the structure is clean, and the fork into annexes was the correct move.
