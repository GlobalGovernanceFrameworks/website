# Federalism Diagnostics: The Subsidiarity Layer for Brazil
### A Governance Routing Protocol for SUS and Social Protection

**Version:** 0.2 (Brazil Adaptation — Revised Specification)
**Target:** CEBRAP, FGV DAPP, Fiocruz, IPEA
**Architectural Status:** Open Standard — Forkable

---

## Executive summary

Brazil's 1988 Constitution made a remarkable promise: universal health care, decentralized to 5,570 municipalities, governed through social participation. SUS (Sistema Único de Saúde) is the largest publicly funded health system in the world by population coverage — 220 million people.

The promise of decentralization is constitutionally mandated. Its delivery is uneven.

A municipality in Maranhão, where 57% of workers are informal and 64% of the population lives below the poverty line, receives health resources through the same allocation formulas as a municipality in Santa Catarina, where informality is 25% and poverty is a fraction of the national average. The Family Health Strategy covers 60% of the population — but coverage, quality, and local capacity vary dramatically across regions that the allocation system treats as equivalent.

This is the **Averaging Problem**: federal policy that cannot see local variation delivers resources that systematically mismatch local need.

**Federalism Diagnostics** provides the missing measurement layer. It introduces the Global Subsidiarity Index (GSI) — a diagnostic protocol that measures the gap between where governance decisions are made and where the relevant knowledge exists. Applied to SUS, it identifies which health policy domains require federal coordination, which require state-level management, and which require municipal adaptation — based on empirical measurement rather than political negotiation.

This is not a proposal for decentralization. It is a diagnostic tool that makes the costs of governance-tier mismatch visible and quantifiable — enabling SUS to deliver on the constitutional promise of responsive, localized, equitable health care.

## 1. The structural problem: Governance latency in SUS

### 1.1 The constitutional promise vs. operational reality

Brazil's 1988 Constitution (Article 198) establishes SUS on four pillars: universalization, integrality, equity, and decentralization with social participation. The Health Care Law (Law 8080/90) assigns administrative responsibilities to each level of government: federal, state, and municipal.

In practice, the federal government controls financing and policy design while municipalities bear the delivery burden — often without the capacity, data, or autonomy to adapt federal programmes to local conditions. The result is a structural mismatch that academic literature has documented extensively:

- **Regional health disparities persist despite 35 years of SUS.** The Northeast and North regions have systematically worse health outcomes, lower physician density, and higher reliance on informal care networks — yet receive allocations through formulas that cannot capture this variation at the municipal level (Lancet Regional Health Americas, 2023).
- **The Family Health Strategy (FHS) covers ~60% of the population** but coverage quality varies dramatically. In São Paulo, FHS teams serve populations with high formal employment and extensive private health alternatives. In rural Maranhão, FHS teams are the only health infrastructure — yet both receive comparable per-team federal transfers.
- **The COVID-19 response demonstrated both the strengths and failures of decentralization.** When the federal government failed to coordinate, state and municipal governments compensated — but the absence of a diagnostic routing system meant coordination was improvised rather than systematic (ScienceDirect, February 2026).

### 1.2 The Averaging Problem in numbers

| Indicator | Maranhão (Northeast) | Santa Catarina (South) | National Average |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|------------------|
| Informality rate | 57.0% | 24.9% | 37.8% |
| Poverty rate | 63.8% (Litoral/Baixada) | Below national average | 27.4% |
| Per capita monthly income | ~R$1,319 (Northeast avg) | ~R$2,499 (South avg) | R$2,020 |
| FHS coverage | High (primary/only care) | Supplemented by private sector | ~60% nationally |

Sources: IBGE PNAD Contínua (2025); IBGE Summary of Social Indicators (2024).

**The same federal allocation formula covers both contexts.** This is not a failure of intent — it is a failure of measurement. The system cannot see the difference, so it cannot respond to it.

### 1.3 What the federal government gains from better diagnostics

This protocol is not a critique of centralization. It is a tool that makes federal programmes more effective:

- **Improved targeting:** Bolsa Família, BPC (Benefício de Prestação Continuada), Pronaf, and SUS transfers can be allocated based on measured local capacity rather than administrative formulas
- **Visible outcomes:** Municipalities that receive GSI-informed allocations can be measured against control municipalities — creating evidence for what works
- **Reduced inefficiency:** The gap between federal allocation and local need — currently unmeasured — becomes quantifiable, enabling data-driven policy adjustment
- **Constitutional compliance:** The 1988 Constitution mandates decentralization. The GSI provides the empirical infrastructure to implement that mandate effectively

**Fiscal politics acknowledgment:** Any tool that recommends different allocation patterns intersects with the fiscal architecture that drives Brazilian federalism — FPE/FPM transfer formulas, the minimum health spending floors established by LC 141/2012, and the ongoing tensions around Emenda 29 compliance. The GSI does not propose changes to these instruments. It provides diagnostic evidence about their effects — making visible, for example, when LC 141/2012 floors produce different real-world outcomes in high-informality vs. low-informality municipalities. Whether and how to adjust fiscal instruments in response to this evidence remains a political decision. The GSI informs that decision; it does not make it.

## 2. The diagnostic engine: The Global Subsidiarity Index for Brazil

### 2.1 Theoretical foundation

The GSI operationalizes Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety: a governance system must match the complexity of the society it governs. Applied to Brazil, this means the governance routing for health policy in a municipality with 57% informality should differ from one with 25% informality — not because of ideology, but because the relevant knowledge exists at different scales.

### 2.2 Four dimensions, sixteen indicators (SUS domain)

The GSI measures governance architecture across four dimensions. For Phase 1 (SUS health delivery), indicators are mapped to Brazilian data sources:

**Dimension 1 — Decision Proximity:** How close are health decisions to those affected?

| Indicator | Measurement | Data Source |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| 1.1 Administrative Distance | Layers between patient and resource allocation decision | IBGE municipal administrative data; SUS governance structure |
| 1.2 Fiscal Sovereignty | % of municipal health budget controlled locally vs. federal transfers | SIOPS (Health Budget Information System) |
| 1.3 Regulatory Autonomy | Municipal ability to adapt federal health programmes to local conditions | Municipal health council records; FHS implementation analysis |
| 1.4 Emergency Response Latitude | Local authority during health emergencies | CEMADEN data; COVID-19 municipal response analysis |

**Dimension 2 — Knowledge Inclusion:** Whose intelligence informs health policy?

| Indicator | Measurement | Data Source |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| 2.1 Expert vs. Community Balance | % health policy inputs from medical professionals vs. community health workers/councils | Municipal Health Council records; FHS team composition |
| 2.2 Participatory Mechanism Quality | Effectiveness of municipal health councils | IBGE Munic survey (municipal governance); council activity records |
| 2.3 Informal Care Integration | Recognition of informal care networks in health delivery | Civic Value Registry (pilot data); PNAD Contínua (informal employment) |
| 2.4 Feedback Loop Efficiency | Time from health problem identification to policy adjustment | DATASUS notification data; municipal health surveillance |

**Dimension 3 — Resilience Architecture:** How health systems handle disruption.

| Indicator | Measurement | Data Source |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| 3.1 Health System Redundancy | Backup mechanisms (private sector, informal care, telemedicine) | ANS (private insurance data); DATASUS infrastructure |
| 3.2 Adaptive Capacity | Speed of response to health emergencies (COVID, dengue, etc.) | DATASUS epidemiological surveillance; municipal response records |
| 3.3 Diversity Resilience | Health service effectiveness across racial, economic, and geographic groups | IBGE PNAD by race/income; SIM (Mortality Information System) |
| 3.4 Long-term Sustainability | Balance of curative vs. preventive investment | SIOPS expenditure data; FHS coverage vs. hospital expenditure ratio |

**Dimension 4 — Cohesion & Integration:** How decentralized health systems maintain quality.

| Indicator | Measurement | Data Source |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| 4.1 Inter-Level Coordination | Effectiveness of CIBs (Bipartite Intermanagerial Committees) and CITs (Tripartite) | CIB/CIT meeting records; referral network analysis |
| 4.2 Shared Standards Alignment | Adherence to federal quality standards across municipalities | PMAQ (Quality Improvement Programme) data |
| 4.3 Referral System Effectiveness | Wait times and bottlenecks in specialist referrals | DATASUS queue data; specialist wait time records |
| 4.4 Solidarity Mechanisms | Effectiveness of inter-municipal health consortia | IBGE Munic data on consortia; CONASEMS (municipal health secretaries) records |

### 2.3 Brazil Complexity Adjustment Factor (BR-CAF)

Comparing health delivery in São Paulo with health delivery in rural Amazonas requires contextual calibration.

**Formula:** `BR-CAF = (w₁G + w₂E + w₃D + w₄T) / C`

Where:
- **G** = Geographic Diversity Score (0–10): Biome complexity, terrain, distance to referral centres. Sources: IBGE geographic data, RAIS.
- **E** = Ethnolinguistic Diversity Score (0–10): Indigenous populations, quilombola communities, immigrant communities. Sources: IBGE Census 2022, FUNAI.
- **D** = Development Disparity Score (0–10): Intra-municipal Gini, urban-rural divide, informality rate. Sources: PNAD Contínua, IBGE municipal GDP.
- **T** = Threat Environment Score (0–10): Epidemiological risk (dengue, Zika, Chagas), climate vulnerability, environmental health threats. Sources: DATASUS, CEMADEN.
- **C** = Central Capacity Score (1–10): Municipal health management effectiveness, PMAQ scores, digital infrastructure maturity. Sources: PMAQ, IBGE Munic, CNES (health facility registry).

**Default weights:** w₁ = 0.25, w₂ = 0.25, w₃ = 0.30, w₄ = 0.20. Development disparity is weighted highest because intra-country inequality is Brazil's defining governance challenge.

**Temporal recalculation:** The BR-CAF is not a static snapshot. The Threat Environment score (T) in particular shifts seasonally — dengue cycles can dramatically change a municipality's governance needs within months. The BR-CAF is recalculated quarterly using rolling 12-month epidemiological data from DATASUS, with emergency recalculation triggered by CEMADEN severe weather alerts or DATASUS epidemic threshold notifications. Other components (G, E, D, C) are updated annually using IBGE/PNAD Contínua releases.

### 2.4 The Optimal Governance Tier (OGT) Matrix

The GSI translates diagnostic outputs into routing recommendations mapped to Brazil's existing administrative structure: União → Estado → Município.

| GSI Signal | BR-CAF Context | OGT Recommendation |
|------------|----------------|---------------------|
| High Knowledge Inclusion requirement + High local complexity | BR-CAF > 4.0 | Municipal delivery with municipal adaptation autonomy |
| High Inter-Level Coordination need + Specialist bottleneck | Any | State-level coordination via CIB; federal minimum standards |
| High Resilience deficit + High informality | BR-CAF > 4.0 | Federal minimum standard + municipal implementation via FHS + informal care integration |
| Low diversity + High capacity | BR-CAF < 2.0 | Federal programme delivered through standard municipal channels |
| High epidemic risk + Multi-municipal scope | Any | State-level coordination with federal epidemiological support |
| Indigenous population within DSEI jurisdiction | Any | SESAI/DSEI parallel governance (see Section 2.5) |

Thresholds are illustrative. Operational values will be calibrated during the Parallel Diagnostic Phase.

### 2.5 Indigenous health subsystem (SESAI/DSEI)

Brazil operates a parallel governance structure for indigenous health: 34 Distritos Sanitários Especiais Indígenas (DSEIs), managed by the Secretaria Especial de Saúde Indígena (SESAI), operating outside normal municipal SUS governance. This creates a routing complexity the GSI must address directly.

**Design principle:** The GSI does not subsume DSEI governance into municipal routing. Indigenous health territories cross municipal boundaries and are governed by distinct protocols. Instead:

- **DSEI areas are flagged in the BR-CAF** as a distinct governance category, not mapped to the União → Estado → Município hierarchy.
- **GSI indicators for DSEI areas** draw from SIASI (Indigenous Health Information System) rather than standard DATASUS, and include culturally specific indicators (traditional medicine integration, indigenous health agent coverage).
- **Routing recommendations** for DSEI areas go to SESAI, not to municipal secretaries — preserving the constitutional autonomy of the indigenous health subsystem.
- **Pilot inclusion:** If an Amazonian municipality is selected for Phase 1, coordination with the relevant DSEI is mandatory, not optional. SESAI is added to the partner table for municipalities that overlap with DSEI jurisdictions.

### 2.6 Subsidiarity Drag

When a health policy is implemented at a different governance tier than the GSI recommends, the protocol publishes the estimated cost — in administrative overhead and fiscal terms — as **Subsidiarity Drag**. This makes the cost of governance-tier mismatch visible without overriding democratic decision-making.

Phase 1 yields a validated cost coefficient for SUS health delivery only. Other domains (education, social protection, disaster response) require independent calibration in future phases.

### 2.7 Data Confidence Tiers

**Tier 1 (Gold):** IBGE + DATASUS + independent academic validation + community health council input.

**Tier 2 (Silver):** IBGE + DATASUS corroborated by at least one independent source (IPEA, Fiocruz study).

**Tier 3 (Bronze):** Estimated via proxy from existing datasets (PNAD Contínua, SIOPS, DATASUS). Proxies labeled with methodology and uncertainty margin.

**Bootstrapping:** Brazil's data infrastructure is exceptionally strong for the Global South. Most SUS-domain indicators can launch at Silver tier immediately using existing IBGE/DATASUS data. Gold tier requires community health council integration, which develops during the pilot.

## 3. The Civic Value Registry: Making informal care visible

### 3.1 The Brazilian context

Brazil's informality rate is approximately 38% nationally (PNAD Contínua, 2025) — but this national figure masks dramatic regional variation: 57% in Maranhão, 25% in Santa Catarina. More importantly for SUS, informal care networks — community health agents, extended family eldercare, religious community support groups, quilombola health practices — constitute a significant share of actual health delivery in high-informality regions. These networks are invisible to DATASUS and SUS allocation formulas.

### 3.2 Function

A voluntary, LGPD-compliant registry that makes informal care capacity visible to SUS planning systems. It does not replace Cadastro Único (CadÚnico) — it extends it to capture care capacity that CadÚnico was not designed to measure.

### 3.3 Citizen experience and e-SUS AB integration

ACS workers already collect household data through **e-SUS AB (Atenção Básica)** — the digital system that supports FHS data collection nationally. The Civic Value Registry is designed as a **module or API extension within e-SUS AB**, not a parallel system. It adds a care capacity assessment to the existing ACS household visit workflow: recording which households provide informal care (eldercare, childcare, neighbour support), the approximate hours, and the community networks involved. Households consent to aggregated data being used for SUS planning. Individual data never leaves the municipality.

This integration is non-negotiable for operational viability. An ACS worker will not adopt a separate app or paper form alongside e-SUS AB. The Registry must live inside the system they already use.

For citizens outside FHS coverage, participation is via smartphone app or community health post registration. Participation is voluntary. No linkage to taxation or welfare conditionality.

### 3.4 Privacy and LGPD compliance

The registry operates under Brazil's Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados (LGPD, Law 13.709/2018):

- Voluntary participation; explicit consent
- Data minimization: only care capacity indicators, not financial or identity data
- Local storage: raw data stays at municipal level
- Aggregated proofs: federal and state systems receive anonymized aggregate statistics only
- No taxation or welfare conditionality linkage

### 3.5 Integration with SUS

Registry data feeds into GSI Dimension 2 (Knowledge Inclusion) indicators via the e-SUS AB data pipeline, enabling SUS allocation formulas to account for informal care capacity. Municipalities with high informal care networks may need recognition infrastructure (ACS support, caregiver training) rather than expensive formal facility construction.

## 4. Deployment model: SUS health delivery first

### Phase 1: Parallel Diagnostic Phase (18–24 months)

**Scope:** SUS health delivery in 4–6 pilot municipalities.

**Municipality selection criteria:**
- At least one high-BR-CAF municipality (Nordeste: high informality, high poverty, high FHS dependency)
- At least one low-BR-CAF municipality (Sul/Sudeste: low informality, private sector alternatives)
- At least one Amazonian municipality (extreme geographic challenge, indigenous health needs)
- At least one medium-complexity state capital (urban SUS challenges: specialist queues, hospital overcrowding)

**Partners:**

| Role | Partner |
|------|---------|
| Academic architecture | CEBRAP (Brazilian Centre for Analysis and Planning); FGV DAPP (Public Policy Analysis) |
| Health system expertise | Fiocruz (Oswaldo Cruz Foundation — SUS's research backbone) |
| Data infrastructure | IBGE (statistical authority); DATASUS (health informatics) |
| Policy bridge | IPEA (Institute for Applied Economic Research) |
| Municipal engagement | CONASEMS (National Council of Municipal Health Secretaries) |
| Indigenous health (if Amazonian pilot) | SESAI; relevant DSEI |

**Cost estimate:**

| Category | Estimated Cost (R$ million) | Notes |
|----------|---------------------------|-------|
| GSI methodology development + IBGE/DATASUS consultation | R$3–6M | Indicator specification, data mapping, academic validation, e-SUS AB module design |
| Civic Value Registry pilot (4–6 municipalities) | R$3–5M | e-SUS AB integration, app development, privacy infrastructure |
| Parallel Diagnostic audit | R$2–4M | Data collection, analysis, comparison with actual SUS outcomes |
| Independent evaluation | R$1–2M | Fiocruz-led evaluation |
| CONASEMS coordination + municipal engagement | R$1–3M | Training, stakeholder engagement, community health council integration |
| Contingency (25%) | R$3–5M | Accounts for institutional onboarding delays — Brazilian institutional engagement timelines are typically longer than estimated |
| **Total** | **R$13–25M** | 18–24 month pilot |

The range is deliberately wide. The lower bound assumes smooth institutional onboarding and existing e-SUS AB compatibility. The upper bound accounts for the reality that IBGE, DATASUS, and CONASEMS engagement processes are institutionally complex and cannot be rushed.

**Potential funding sources:**
- Fiocruz research budget
- FAPESP, FAPERJ, or other state research foundations (fundações de amparo à pesquisa)
- IDB (Inter-American Development Bank) governance innovation programmes
- ECLAC technical cooperation
- Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) health systems research

**Success metrics:**

| Metric | Type | Baseline | Target |
|--------|------|----------|--------|
| SUS allocation-need gap published | Output | Unmeasured | GSI-measured gap published for all pilot municipalities |
| Informal care capacity measured | Output | 0 (invisible to DATASUS) | Care capacity data in e-SUS AB for pilot municipalities |
| Avoidable hospitalization convergence | Outcome | Divergent rates between high-CAF and low-CAF municipalities (DATASUS baseline) | Measurable convergence in ICSAP (internações por condições sensíveis à atenção primária) rates between pilot municipalities receiving GSI-informed allocation vs. control municipalities |
| Specialist referral wait time | Outcome | Current DATASUS queue data (baseline established Month 1) | ≥15% reduction in referral wait times in pilot municipalities with GSI-informed CIB routing |
| Academic publication | Output | None | ≥1 peer-reviewed paper applying GSI to Brazilian SUS data |
| Health council integration | Process | Variable | Health councils in pilot municipalities actively using GSI data in annual planning |

### Phase 2: State-level expansion (Years 2–3)

- GSI applied across all municipalities in 2–3 pilot states
- Collaboration with state health secretaries (SES) and CIBs (Bipartite Intermanagerial Committees)
- Integration with PMAQ quality improvement programme
- Domain expansion: education (FUNDEB allocation), social protection (Bolsa Família targeting)

### Phase 3: National integration (Years 4–5)

- GSI integrated into federal SUS allocation methodology
- Civic Value Registry scaled through FHS infrastructure nationally
- Results presented to IDB/ECLAC as replicable Latin American methodology
- Academic anchor paper positions GSI as a tool for federal governance diagnostics across the region

## 5. Framework revision protocol

The GSI is designed to improve through use. If the Parallel Diagnostic Phase reveals systematic diagnostic errors in the Brazilian context:

1. **Error detection:** Comparison of GSI routing recommendations against actual SUS outcomes
2. **Root cause analysis:** Fiocruz-led panel determines whether mismatches are data quality issues or structural model limitations
3. **Revision proposal:** Published for open comment through CEBRAP/FGV academic networks
4. **Version control:** All implementations specify framework version

---

## 6. Falsifiability

If the Parallel Diagnostic Phase demonstrates that GSI-informed SUS allocation does not improve health delivery outcomes compared to existing allocation formulas, the core hypothesis is falsified. Negative results are published through the same academic channels as positive results. The data collected still constitutes Brazil's first systematic municipal-level governance architecture diagnostic for health delivery — valuable regardless of whether the routing algorithm outperforms existing processes.

## 7. Risk mitigation

| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|------------|
| Political perception as federalism challenge | Frame as SUS effectiveness tool; federal government gains better targeting metrics |
| Fiscal instrument sensitivity (FPE/FPM, LC 141/2012) | GSI diagnoses effects but does not propose fiscal changes; political decisions remain with legislature |
| Municipal capacity limitations | e-SUS AB integration reduces burden; ACS workflow module, not separate system; CONASEMS coordination |
| Privacy violation | LGPD compliance; local storage; aggregated proofs only; no taxation linkage |
| Data quality variation | Confidence Tiers; Silver-tier launch using existing IBGE/DATASUS; convergence pathway documented |
| Indigenous health governance bypass | DSEI areas flagged as distinct category; SESAI routing preserved; SIASI data sources |
| Institutional capture | Distributed governance; Fiocruz academic independence; community health council oversight |
| Institutional onboarding delays | Cost estimate includes 20% contingency; phased funding tied to milestones; 18–24 month range acknowledges Brazilian institutional timelines |
| Academic echo chamber | Peer review through international networks; IDB/ECLAC technical validation |
