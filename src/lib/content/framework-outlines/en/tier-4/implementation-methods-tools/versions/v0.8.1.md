**Tier:** 4 — Implementation methods and practical tools

## Constitutional doctrine

> **A tool is not authority. A resource card is not a licence. A recommendation is not a classification. Facilitation is not representation. A rating is not legitimacy. A pause is not adjudication. Community refusal is not jurisdiction over others. A harm report is not a finding. Certification is not a monopoly. A failure story is not public property. An emergency tool is not emergency power.**

The framework exists to make useful governance methods discoverable, adaptable, accessible, and learnable. It does not make their use lawful merely by publishing them.

---

## 1. Role and boundaries

### 1.1 What this framework owns

The framework owns the design and stewardship of a practical tool commons:

- tool discovery and advisory recommendations;
- resource cards and authority-effect warnings;
- low-tech, oral, paper, SMS, assisted, and digital equivalents;
- accessibility and participation guidance;
- cultural adaptation methods;
- tool forking, attribution, versioning, deprecation, and migration;
- facilitator training and accountability models;
- pilot templates and monitoring methods;
- protected learning artifacts, Failure Library, and Story Bank practices;
- conformance testing.

### 1.2 What this framework does not own

It does not itself create:

- a council, BAZ, charter, public office, tribunal, ombudsperson, guardian, or authority;
- affected Indigenous representation, FPIC, title, sacred status, or legal standing;
- emergency declaration, mission command, compulsory data access, or resource requisition;
- public budget, grant entitlement, payment, tax, sanction, or clawback;
- permit, licence, inspection, investigation, adjudication, search, seizure, detention, force, or enforcement;
- benefit eligibility, migration status, child guardianship, or essential-service restriction.

### 1.3 The permission rule

No permission is needed to:

- read or copy an openly licensed tool;
- discuss, translate, simulate, or critique it;
- fork it within the licence;
- use it for non-consequential learning;
- use it voluntarily inside one’s own lawful private or internal scope.

Separate authority is required when tool use affects rights, non-participants, public resources, institutional powers, protected domains, essential services, or legally consequential decisions.

## 2. Guiding principles

### Low-tech first; equal dignity throughout

Every digital tool must have a viable analog or assisted path. Paper, oral, sign-language, SMS, offline, and human-assisted records receive equal substantive standing and remedy.

### Subsidiarity within actual authority

Use the most local effective actor that possesses lawful authority, adequate capacity, and accountability. Locality is not omnipotence.

### Cultural plurality through self-description

Communities adapt tools through their own language, law, ceremony, and decision practices. External systems may not assign cultural identity or maturity for consequential routing.

### Anti-colonial practice through affected-nation authority

The specific affected Indigenous nation or people controls its representatives, consent, protected knowledge, territorial and relational authority, and whether the tool is appropriate.

### Modularity without authority aggregation

Tools may be combined, but their powers do not combine. A stack of advisory tools remains advisory.

### Harm-aware and allegation-safe

Suspected harm receives immediate protection and review. A report does not become a finding of guilt through a library flag.

### Human responsibility at consequential boundaries

AI may assist discovery, translation, comparison, accessibility, and drafting. It may not determine authority, consent, legal status, cultural identity, emergency activation, or protected-domain consequences.

### Accessibility as infrastructure

Accommodation, interpretation, childcare, disability support, pacing, and independent assistance are standard implementation costs, not optional rewards for cooperation.

### Learning without extraction

Participants and communities retain rights over stories, protected knowledge, identifiable testimony, and optional data. Failure is not a licence for permanent exposure.

## 3. Safe quick start

1. **Describe your goal and constraints.** Use self-description rather than an externally assigned readiness category.
2. **Review tool options.** Compare authority effect, prerequisites, accessibility, maintenance, risks, and alternatives.
3. **Choose or fork a tool.** Preserve attribution, rights, protected-domain warnings, and compatibility metadata.
4. **Determine effect.** Is this discussion, voluntary internal use, or a consequential pilot?
5. **For consequential use, follow GMEAIA.** Map affected parties, consent, authority, protected domains, authorization, activation, monitoring, remedy, and closure.
6. **Learn safely.** Publish only bounded lessons with purpose-specific consent.

## 4. The Tool Finder

### 4.1 Advisory function

The Tool Finder asks users to select:

- goal;
- urgency claim;
- available time and resources;
- accessibility and language needs;
- connectivity and preferred format;
- maintenance capacity;
- protected-domain indicators;
- whether the intended use is discussion, voluntary internal practice, or consequential action.

It returns multiple options with reasons, assumptions, alternatives, and limitations.

### 4.2 Prohibited inferences

The Finder may not infer or score:

- cultural, Indigenous, religious, developmental, psychological, or political identity;
- whether a community is mature, legitimate, trustworthy, or competent to govern;
- consent or representation;
- legal authority;
- emergency status;
- eligibility for funding or benefits;
- propensity for violence, fraud, extremism, or non-compliance.

### 4.3 Crisis input

Selecting “immediate crisis” does not activate a 72-hour kit. It produces:

- a clear warning that urgency is asserted;
- non-consequential safety and listening tools;
- an EGP `sense()` template;
- a CDEE referral path;
- a reminder that declaration, command, information access, finance, and coercive action require competent authority.

### 4.4 Example journey groups

| Goal | Examples | Default authority effect |
|---|---|---|
| Start an initiative | talking circle, stakeholder map, draft charter worksheet | none or advisory |
| Improve decisions | deep listening, option comparison, agenda design | advisory |
| Resolve conflict | voluntary mediation, restorative conversation preparation | voluntary internal; protected-domain review when consequential |
| Grow capacity | facilitator learning, tool stewardship, accessibility planning | none or advisory |
| Transition or close | records inventory, closure checklist, successor mapping | advisory; authority required for institutional effect |
| Crisis support | safety checklist, EGP signal, CDEE referral | none or referral only |

## 6. Analog, offline, oral, and assisted parity

Digital tools require equivalent paper, oral, audio, sign-language, SMS, offline, or assisted options where the function can be performed without digital technology.

A scribe, interpreter, or digital steward must be identified as a recorder. Users must be able to review, correct, receive, restrict, and challenge the record.

Synchronization rules must preserve conflicts. “Last write wins” is prohibited for consent, authority, activation, remedy, and closure.

## 7. Cultural adaptation and Indigenous authority

### 7.1 Adaptation questions

Before use, ask:

- Who defines the problem?
- Who is affected but absent?
- Which law, custom, or charter governs the process?
- Which practices are self-described rather than externally assigned?
- What knowledge cannot be recorded, translated, digitized, or published?
- Who may refuse, withdraw, or fork?
- What would constitute extraction or misrepresentation?

### 7.2 Retired universal quota and certification rules

The source’s universal 40% design quota and certification by a global cultural body are retired as standing rules. Participation thresholds may be designed by the affected people for a specific process; no percentage substitutes for affected-nation authority, internal plurality, consent, or benefit sharing.

### 7.3 Bounded community refusal

A community may refuse a tool’s use:

- in its own governance process;
- on its own systems, property, records, and voluntary programmes;
- where its law or rights govern the affected territory, knowledge, or relationship;
- where it is an affected party entitled to consent or challenge.

Refusal does not automatically authorize the community to bind another lawful community, prevent individual voluntary use, or control outsiders beyond its jurisdiction.

## 8. Tool commons governance

### 8.1 Fork freely; preserve constitutional metadata

Openly licensed tools may be forked. A fork must preserve:

- parent version and attribution;
- authority-effect classification;
- protected-domain warnings;
- accessibility and offline metadata;
- known harms and security notices;
- incompatibilities and migration information.

### 8.2 Core stewardship

Core maintainers must use public proposals, impact review, compatibility tests, security review, dissent records, migration support, and rollback.

Emergency security releases may restrict or disable a technical function. They cannot expand substantive powers.

### 8.3 Ratings

Ratings are user reports, not institutional truth. Systems must address brigading, retaliation, minority experiences, version differences, conflicts, and correction.

No rating may determine rights, credibility, employment, funding, participation, or community legitimacy.

## 9. Harm prevention, response, and remedy

Potential warning categories include capture, exclusion, coercion, surveillance, retaliation, accessibility failure, cultural or spiritual harm, unsafe technology, and dependency on a captured vendor.

The safe sequence is:

`harm signal → immediate safety assessment → competent interim protection → evidence and causation review → decision and dissent → remediation or remedy → correction → learning artifact → closure`

A library administrator may delist or warn about a tool. It cannot suspend a public institution, essential service, licence, benefit, or legal authority.

## 10. Accessibility by design

Minimum requirements include:

- WCAG-compatible digital materials where relevant;
- captions, transcripts, sign-language and interpretation options;
- keyboard, voice, switch, and assistive-technology compatibility;
- plain language and multiple levels of detail;
- visual and non-visual equivalents;
- asynchronous participation;
- flexible timelines and rest;
- sensory-safe alternatives;
- trauma-aware choice and support;
- no forced disclosure beyond what accommodation requires.

Illustrative reading levels may guide drafting but cannot replace user testing or become a barrier to preserving precise legal meaning.

## 11. Pilots, evaluation, and scaling

Pilot levels are complexity descriptions only:

- single-tool test;
- multi-tool stack;
- full implementation journey;
- multi-site or network proposal.

They create no authority or automatic progression.

Pilot assessment considers:

- safety and rights;
- affected-party experience;
- accessibility;
- outcomes and distribution;
- causal uncertainty and external conditions;
- maintenance and resource burden;
- data and vendor risks;
- rollback and closure;
- dissent.

Satisfaction targets such as 70%, 80%, or 90% may be local hypotheses. They are not universal pass thresholds.

A successful pilot may inform a new scaling proposal. The new context requires new authority, consent, affected-party mapping, risk analysis, resources, monitoring, and remedy.

## 12. Facilitator and support infrastructure

Training levels describe learning pathways, not public ranks.

A facilitator must have:

- relevant competence and limitations;
- a lawful or community mandate;
- exact scope and duration;
- conflicts and funding disclosure;
- confidentiality and data duties;
- safe complaints and independent review;
- removal, replacement, liability, and succession.

Community recognition, apprenticeship, professional education, lived experience, and locally defined competence may provide equivalent routes. GGF certification is never the sole valid route.

## 13. AI, digital tools, and vendors

AI may assist:

- tool discovery;
- translation and accessibility;
- comparison and missing-field detection;
- summarization;
- non-consequential simulations;
- expiry reminders;
- duplicate detection.

AI may not decide:

- cultural identity or community type;
- readiness, trustworthiness, legitimacy, or risk status;
- affected Indigenous representation;
- consent;
- authority or jurisdiction;
- emergency activation;
- eligibility, payment, funding suspension, or clawback;
- guilt, harm attribution, certification, or scaling.

Vendors require transparent contracts, beneficial ownership, export, open formats, security, accessibility, offline continuity, deletion, incident response, and exit.

---

## 14. Emergency and coercive tools

The former “72-Hour Governance Activation Kit,” “Emergency Ombudsperson Protocol,” and emergency support tiers are reclassified as templates and support resources.

They may:

- organize information;
- support local pre-existing authority;
- help create EGP signals;
- prepare CDEE referrals;
- support an accepted ECRC/CDEE mission;
- provide accessibility, documentation, and logistics.

They may not declare emergencies, appoint commanders, compel information, requisition resources, establish checkpoints, search, seize, detain, use force, impose sanctions, or renew powers.

---

## 15. Monitoring, Failure Library, and Story Bank

### Failure Library

A Failure Library entry is a bounded learning artifact, not an investigation file, blacklist, or compulsory confession.

Publication requires:

- de-identification risk review;
- purpose-specific consent where identifiable;
- affected-community and protected-knowledge review;
- separation from active evidence and complaints;
- access and retention controls;
- correction and withdrawal;
- disclosure of uncertainty and dissent.

Reading relevant lessons may be recommended. Access to a protected or compulsory library cannot be a universal precondition for piloting.

### Story Bank

Narrators control wording, attribution, edits, access, reuse, translation, AI training, compensation, and withdrawal. A story cannot be treated as proof that a framework caused success.

---

## 16. Lifecycle and closure

Tool and pilot states include:

`draft → published → active_use → under_review → restricted → deprecated → withdrawn_for_safety → archived`

Pilot closure separately addresses:

- participants and affected people;
- essential support;
- rollback;
- data disposition;
- payments, contracts, and debts;
- remedy and restoration;
- pending complaints and appeals;
- successor custody;
- authority return;
- public learning artifacts.

Low use may justify archival review, but not automatic retirement after a fixed period.

## 19. Constitutional readiness gates

| Gate | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| G01 | Every tool states an exact source, steward, version, status, and authority-effect label. |
| G02 | No template, guide, score, dashboard, certificate, ritual, or software function creates public authority. |
| G03 | Tool Finder inputs are self-described, minimal, contestable, and non-dispositive. |
| G04 | Tool Finder outputs are advisory, explainable, versioned, and show alternatives and limits. |
| G05 | Every consequential recommendation displays protected-domain and authority requirements. |
| G06 | Analog, oral, paper, SMS, assisted, and digital paths have equal substantive standing. |
| G07 | Accessibility and participation support are unconditional and cannot require agreement. |
| G08 | Cultural categories are never externally assigned for consequential routing. |
| G09 | The specific affected Indigenous nation controls representation, consent, and protected knowledge. |
| G10 | Cultural review is advisory and cannot become external approval or veto. |
| G11 | Community refusal is bounded to the refusing community’s participation, assets, data, and lawful authority. |
| G12 | Individual refusal, withdrawal, and non-retaliation are supported where participation is voluntary. |
| G13 | Forking a tool does not inherit institutional powers or remove rights safeguards. |
| G14 | Core maintainers use public change control, compatibility review, rollback, and dissent. |
| G15 | Deprecation distinguishes safety withdrawal, supersession, low use, and legal incompatibility. |
| G16 | Facilitator certification is one qualification route, not a monopoly or office. |
| G17 | Facilitator appointment, scope, conflicts, complaints, removal, liability, and succession are explicit. |
| G18 | AI and automated tools have prohibited-decision lists and responsible human or institutional owners. |
| G19 | Data purpose, access, retention, publication, correction, and deletion are explicit. |
| G20 | Vendors cannot own governance authority or trap community records. |
| G21 | A pilot identifies authority, affected parties, consent, protected domains, rights, clocks, rollback, remedy, and closure. |
| G22 | EGP adoption registration is distinct from operational activation. |
| G23 | Metrics and ratings trigger review only and cannot determine legitimacy, rights, funding, fault, or scale. |
| G24 | Harm reports receive protection but remain allegations until independently reviewed. |
| G25 | Interim pauses are separately authorized, proportionate, expiring, reviewable, and preserve essential support. |
| G26 | Pilot success creates a scaling proposal, not authority to expand. |
| G27 | Certification states a narrow claim, version, evidence, scope, expiry, correction, and appeal. |
| G28 | Failure Library and Story Bank publication require purpose-specific consent and withdrawal routes. |
| G29 | Funding, payment, support, audit, suspension, clawback, and appeal remain separate records. |
| G30 | Urgency selectors and emergency kits issue EGP signals and CDEE referrals only. |
| G31 | Coercive actions route to controlling substantive authority and SCPA. |
| G32 | Termination, closure, authority return, unresolved duties, migration, and data disposition are complete. |

All gates are mandatory before claiming full framework conformance. A gate may be inapplicable only with a reasoned record identifying why the function cannot arise in the implementation.

## 20. Compound tests

| Test | Scenario | Required outcome |
|---|---|---|
| T01 | Tool Finder receives “immediate crisis” | It recommends safe listening and referral tools, labels urgency as asserted, and does not activate an emergency kit. |
| T02 | A user selects “form a council” | The finder presents convening and charter templates with authority-effect `none`; no council or jurisdiction is created. |
| T03 | A community downloads a tool | Reading and copying require no permission, but consequential use still requires authority and consent. |
| T04 | A community forks a citizen-assembly guide | The fork preserves attribution and rights safeguards but inherits no public decision power. |
| T05 | An AI finder infers a community is Indigenous | The classification is rejected; self-description and affected-nation authority are required. |
| T06 | A global cultural council certifies a local adaptation | The certificate is advisory and cannot replace affected-nation or local authority. |
| T07 | A community refuses a tool for its own meetings | The refusal is honoured without penalty or loss of support. |
| T08 | One community tries to veto another community’s lawful independent pilot | The refusal does not extend beyond the first community’s lawful scope and affected interests. |
| T09 | A participant withdraws from a voluntary pilot | Participation and optional data use stop without loss of essential support. |
| T10 | A certified facilitator is unavailable | A lawfully appointed local equivalent may serve with documented competence and accountability. |
| T11 | A facilitator claims authority to consent for participants | The claim is rejected; facilitation and representation remain separate. |
| T12 | A facilitator has an undisclosed vendor commission | The conflict triggers recusal, independent review, correction, and possible remedy. |
| T13 | A digital voting tool has no analog path | The tool is non-conforming until an equal alternative exists. |
| T14 | An oral decision is later digitized by a scribe | The original lawful process retains dignity; the scribe cannot alter or own the decision. |
| T15 | A blockchain ledger contains a false participant allegation | The record is corrected, access restricted or deleted where required, and downstream uses reassessed. |
| T16 | A vendor ends service during an active pilot | Export, offline continuity, rollback, and service-transition plans protect users. |
| T17 | AI recommends an AUBI eligibility tool | The recommendation is advisory; benefits and automated allocation route to protected-domain authority. |
| T18 | A pilot receives funding | Funding does not activate it; separate authorization and activation remain required. |
| T19 | A pilot is adopted in EGP but activation is absent | Its state remains authorized-inactive or claim-only. |
| T20 | A tool collects sensitive health data | Protected access, purpose limitation, consent or law, retention, deletion, and independent review apply. |
| T21 | A harm flag alleges surveillance | Collection may be safely paused by competent authority; the allegation is independently reviewed. |
| T22 | A library administrator suspends a public service tool | The administrator may delist the tool from the library but cannot suspend the public service without authority. |
| T23 | A tool receives low cultural-fit ratings | The ratings trigger review but do not prove harm or justify removal from another community. |
| T24 | A popular tool receives five stars | Popularity does not certify safety, legality, or universal fit. |
| T25 | A pilot exceeds 90% satisfaction | Success supports a new scaling proposal; no automatic tier advancement occurs. |
| T26 | A pilot fails due to wildfire and funding collapse | External causes are separated from tool or facilitator fault. |
| T27 | A quality mark expires | The tool may continue where otherwise lawful, but cannot display the expired claim as current. |
| T28 | A participant story is submitted to the Story Bank | Narrator control, editing, attribution, compensation, access, and withdrawal are recorded. |
| T29 | A whistleblower’s case enters the Failure Library | The active evidentiary record remains protected; only a bounded learning artifact may be published. |
| T30 | A community withdraws publication consent | Public artifacts and downstream copies are corrected or restricted as far as feasible. |
| T31 | A grant funder dislikes a community’s adaptation | Accessibility and participation support cannot be withdrawn for disagreement. |
| T32 | A 72-hour kit is used during a flood | It supports an accepted CDEE/ECRC mission but does not create command or compulsory powers. |
| T33 | A tool suggests search or detention | The action fails closed and routes to the substantive framework and SCPA. |
| T34 | A deprecated tool is still safe and legally valid | Users may remain on it with disclosed risks, support status, and migration options. |
| T35 | A tool is withdrawn for a critical security flaw | The steward may stop distribution and issue rollback guidance without expanding substantive authority. |
| T36 | A pilot ends with unpaid facilitators and unresolved harm | Temporary authority ends, but payments, remedy, data, and claims remain open until discharged. |

## 21. Adoption states

The framework distinguishes:

- `proposed`;
- `advisory`;
- `voluntarily_adopted`;
- `contractually_binding`;
- `locally_enacted`;
- `nationally_enacted`;
- `treaty_active`;
- `suspended`;
- `expired`;
- `terminated`.

Publication in the tool library activates none of these states by itself.
