# Planetary Immune System v2.2.1

## Catastrophic Risk Foresight, Readiness, and Coordinated Response Protocol

> **Governing principle:** Catastrophic severity may justify urgent attention, preparation, and lawful action. It does not create jurisdiction, residual command, or a right to override ordinary institutions.

---

# Executive summary

Human civilization faces risks whose consequences can cross borders, sectors, generations, and ecological systems: engineered or naturally emerging biological hazards, severe cyber and infrastructure failures, nuclear escalation, frontier-technology failures, abrupt ecological disruption, asteroid or space-weather hazards, cascading disasters, and risks not yet well classified.

The need for foresight and readiness is real. So is the constitutional danger of treating the language of existential risk as a source of exceptional authority. Planetary Immune System v2.1 concentrated observation, risk tiering, crisis activation, emergency command, global response teams, sanctions, fiscal mechanisms, and public mobilisation around one imagined emergency architecture. That design risked becoming a dormant world executive activated by uncertain models.

Version 2.2.1 retains the valuable core of the earlier framework:

- catastrophic-risk foresight;
- plural modelling and adversarial review;
- dependency and cascading-failure analysis;
- decentralized resilience;
- preparedness exercises;
- knowledge and capability preservation;
- mutual-aid readiness;
- local response by default;
- public catastrophic-risk literacy;
- Global South, Indigenous, youth, disability, worker, and frontline perspectives;
- reversible action and option preservation.

It retires or defers:

- reconfiguration of Meta-Governance into an Existential Security Council;
- a Planetary Immune System “takeover” of normal functions;
- observer-issued emergency declarations;
- a single elite global response force;
- automatic coercive action from risk tiers or dashboards;
- self-authorized taxes, sanctions, patent revocation, trade restrictions, facility shutdowns, or defence-budget reallocations;
- model-generated triage or bunker access;
- economic punishment for non-participation;
- official existential narratives or compelled civilizational worldviews.

The Protocol is governed by the **Emergency, Catastrophic-Risk, and Continuity Interface Specification v0.1**. It therefore separates:

1. hazard alerts;
2. plural state estimation;
3. standing and jurisdiction review;
4. lawful declaration by a competent authority;
5. power-specific activation;
6. mission-bounded operational coordination;
7. justice, review, and enforcement;
8. termination, restoration, compensation, and learning.

The **Existential Risk Observatory (ERO)** remains an observer. It may collect evidence, publish or protect state estimates, compare models, map dependencies, identify unresolved risk, request review, recommend preparation, and convene competent authorities. It may not declare a legally operative emergency, command personnel, shut facilities, classify actors as hostile, allocate life-sustaining resources, impose sanctions, redirect public money, authorize surveillance, or deploy force.

The Protocol does not create one response corps. It maintains interfaces and readiness standards for separately constituted capabilities, including:

- public-health and laboratory response;
- hazardous-material and environmental containment;
- disaster rescue and humanitarian logistics;
- cyber and infrastructure assistance;
- space-hazard monitoring and coordination;
- continuity of food, water, energy, communications, health, finance, and governance;
- unarmed international assistance;
- armed defensive capability only under a separate valid legal basis and command structure.

The constitutional rule is:

> **No Emergency Authority Provenance Record, no consequential emergency action.**

The anti-takeover rule is:

> **The Planetary Immune System is a readiness and coordination protocol, not a government waiting for catastrophe.**

# 1. Purpose, status, and non-authority

## 1.1 Purpose

This Protocol helps competent authorities, communities, operators, researchers, and mutual-aid networks prepare for catastrophic and existential risks without creating a residual global sovereign. It provides common methods for foresight, readiness, scenario comparison, dependency mapping, exercises, assistance requests, action-module handoffs, continuity planning, and post-crisis learning.

## 1.2 Legal status

This document is a constitutional and operational interface. It is not itself:

- a treaty or emergency statute;
- an emergency declaration;
- a licence, permit, warrant, injunction, deployment order, or appropriation;
- a source of police, military, health, cyber, fiscal, regulatory, or judicial jurisdiction;
- an authorization to enter territory, access systems, obtain data, compel work, or redirect assets.

It becomes operative only through valid adoption, incorporation, compact, contract, host request, Indigenous law, domestic law, Treaty procedure, or another independently valid instrument.

## 1.3 Non-authority clause

The Protocol does not itself:

- declare or renew an emergency;
- convert Meta-Governance into an executive command;
- override national, municipal, BAZ, Indigenous, professional, operator, or judicial authority;
- command health workers, scientists, infrastructure personnel, civil servants, police, armed forces, contractors, or volunteers;
- order quarantine, evacuation, detention, treatment, search, seizure, censorship, shutdown, access restriction, asset freeze, patent revocation, trade restriction, or use of force;
- levy a tax, redirect a budget, create debt, allocate AUBI, or condition essential support;
- determine legal guilt, hostility, negligence, liability, or compensation;
- decide whose life, culture, knowledge, territory, or future has greater worth.

## 1.4 No framework-to-power conversion

A PIS risk tier, dashboard, exercise result, ERO report, scientific panel, model ensemble, public petition, World Risk Assembly, Office of the Adversary challenge, or GGF framework reference does not create legal authority.

> **No provenance record, no consequential action.**

# 2. Relationship to the emergency constitutional interface

## 2.1 Controlling interface

All emergency-capable uses of this Protocol follow the **Emergency, Catastrophic-Risk, and Continuity Interface Specification v0.1**. Where this document is inconsistent with that Specification, the Specification controls for GGF constitutional alignment.

## 2.2 Required common sequence

The Protocol recognizes the following sequence:

1. ordinary prevention and preparedness;
2. non-binding alert;
3. plural and independent state estimates;
4. standing and jurisdiction mapping;
5. declaration by a competent authority under valid law;
6. power-by-power activation;
7. mission-bounded operational coordination;
8. review at the applicable seventy-two-hour, thirty-day, and ninety-day gates;
9. termination and return of authority;
10. restoration, compensation, data disposition, and after-action review.

## 2.3 No circular delegation

No PIS actor may claim authority from Meta-Governance merely because Meta-Governance cites the PIS. No Treaty, Aegis, health, disaster, biotech, technology, or justice institution may claim authority merely because another framework expects it to act.

Every consequential action must trace to an authority source outside the circular framework reference.

## 2.4 Underlying decision class remains

Emergency procedure may compress timing only where valid law permits. It does not convert:

- a scientific estimate into an executive order;
- a recommendation into a licence;
- a host request into territorial sovereignty;
- a cyber alert into authorization for intrusion;
- a public-health warning into authority over bodily integrity;
- a catastrophic-risk label into military jurisdiction;
- a coordination meeting into command.

# 3. Scope and exclusions

## 3.1 In-scope risk domains

The Protocol supports foresight and preparedness for:

- severe biological events, laboratory incidents, and pandemic-capable outbreaks;
- antimicrobial-resistance scenarios with systemic consequences;
- frontier AI or automated-system failures capable of cross-system disruption;
- severe cyber incidents affecting essential infrastructure;
- nuclear escalation, radiological incidents, and weapons-of-mass-destruction risks;
- catastrophic ecological thresholds and abrupt Earth-system disruption;
- cascading disasters and infrastructure failures;
- asteroid, comet, solar-storm, space-debris, and other space hazards;
- food, water, energy, logistics, communications, financial, or institutional continuity failures;
- compound risks where several domains interact;
- credible emerging risks whose mechanisms remain uncertain.

## 3.2 Exclusions

The Protocol does not govern ordinary sectoral policy merely because catastrophic scenarios can be imagined. Routine technology regulation belongs to competent domain authorities and Technology Governance v3.6. Routine public health belongs to valid health authorities. Ordinary disaster planning belongs to local and national institutions and Living Shield v4.4.1. Biosafety licensing belongs to valid biosafety law and Aethelred v1.2.1. Security and armed capabilities belong to separately constituted security law and Aegis v1.3.1.

## 3.3 Catastrophic label discipline

A risk must not be labelled catastrophic or existential solely to bypass ordinary procedure. Every PIS estimate must distinguish:

- severity of possible consequence;
- probability or plausibility;
- time horizon;
- confidence and disagreement;
- affected systems and populations;
- reversibility;
- available ordinary authorities;
- cost of delay;
- cost and risk of premature action.

# 4. Governing principles

## 4.1 Foresight without sovereignty

The ability to see a threat does not confer authority to govern it.

## 4.2 Local and ordinary response by default

Response begins with the smallest competent and capable authority. Cross-border assistance is added where requested, pre-authorized, or otherwise lawfully activated.

## 4.3 Functional separation

Observation, declaration, command, technical assistance, investigation, adjudication, enforcement, finance, and public communication remain institutionally separable.

## 4.4 Plural state estimation

No single model, institution, discipline, intelligence source, or epistemology may monopolize catastrophic-risk estimation. Plurality does not mean that every claim receives equal evidentiary weight.

## 4.5 Least coercive effective action

Where several responses can materially reduce risk, competent authorities should prefer the action that is effective while least impairing rights, livelihoods, essential services, institutional plurality, and future options.

## 4.6 Reversibility and option preservation

Preparedness should expand viable response options. Irreversible interventions require higher authority and evidentiary thresholds than reversible preparation, monitoring, stockpiling, exercises, or voluntary assistance.

## 4.7 Continuity without entrenchment

Essential services and institutions must survive crises. Emergency bodies and temporary command structures need not survive them.

## 4.8 Equity without discretionary worth ranking

Preparedness must address unequal exposure, colonial extraction, disability, poverty, displacement, and infrastructure gaps. It must not permit opaque scoring of whose lives, cultures, or territories deserve protection.

## 4.9 Indigenous authority and non-transfer

Indigenous peoples retain their own authority, law, knowledge protocols, refusal rights, and territorial interfaces. No universal Indigenous proxy is created.

## 4.10 Honest uncertainty

The Protocol may conclude that evidence is insufficient, models disagree, an intervention could worsen risk, or no authority exists to take a proposed action.

# 5. Functional architecture

The Protocol separates seven functions.

## 5.1 Foresight and observation

The ERO and participating observatories identify, compare, and document risks. They do not command.

## 5.2 Readiness and exercises

Preparedness networks maintain plans, capability inventories, mutual-aid arrangements, continuity options, and exercises. They do not create standing jurisdiction over participants.

## 5.3 Convening and interface maintenance

Meta-Governance or another lawful convenor may bring competent authorities together, maintain shared records, and identify unresolved interfaces. Convening is not executive authority.

## 5.4 Declaration

Only a competent authority under valid law may issue a legally operative declaration.

## 5.5 Operational coordination

Activated operational bodies coordinate only the mission, assets, territory, personnel, and duration assigned to them.

## 5.6 Justice and enforcement

Interim orders, liability, sanctions, warrants, seizures, compulsory access, facility closure, and remedies follow Justice Systems v1.4 and applicable law.

## 5.7 Termination, restoration, and learning

Every activation includes authority return, demobilisation, records disposition, compensation pathways, continuity handoff, and independent after-action review.

# 6. The Existential Risk Observatory

## 6.1 Role

The **Existential Risk Observatory (ERO)** is a federated observation and foresight function. It may be implemented by a network of universities, public agencies, Indigenous and community observatories, civil-society institutions, professional bodies, technical laboratories, and independent research organizations.

**The ERO is an observer, not a declarant or commander.**

The ERO is not one mandatory global data centre or intelligence agency.

## 6.2 Permitted functions

The ERO may:

- receive and issue non-binding hazard alerts;
- maintain plural Catastrophic-Risk State Estimates;
- compare models and assumptions;
- identify dependencies, bottlenecks, and cascading failure paths;
- maintain an unresolved-risk register;
- identify missing observations and preparedness gaps;
- propose exercises and research priorities;
- preserve evidence and model provenance;
- request review by competent authorities;
- publish public estimates and maintain protected annexes;
- support low-resource observatories and South–South collaboration;
- maintain contact directories and mutual-aid readiness interfaces.

## 6.3 Prohibited functions

The ERO may not:

- declare or renew an emergency;
- activate an operational corps;
- issue binding risk classifications;
- order facility shutdowns, model suspension, cyber intrusion, quarantine, evacuation, asset control, patent action, trade restriction, or armed deployment;
- assign criminal or hostile status;
- compel data or Traditional Knowledge;
- allocate scarce life-sustaining resources;
- redirect public funds;
- condition AUBI, services, market access, or research participation;
- become the final authority on an unresolved scientific question.

## 6.4 Federated governance

Any ERO network must disclose:

- participating institutions and authority sources;
- funding and conflicts of interest;
- model and data dependencies;
- public and protected outputs;
- challenge and correction routes;
- withdrawal and data-return procedures;
- continuity if a central node fails or loses trust.

No member receives privileged constitutional status by hosting infrastructure or maintaining the principal repository.

# 7. Alerts and plural state estimates

## 7.1 Hazard alerts

Any person or institution with relevant information may issue a Hazard Alert Record. An alert must distinguish observation from inference, identify urgency, explain uncertainty, and state whether immediate protective review is requested.

An alert has no coercive effect.

## 7.2 Catastrophic-Risk State Estimate

Each major estimate should record:

- the possible event or failure mode;
- causal pathway and evidence;
- affected populations, territories, systems, and generations;
- probability, plausibility, or scenario status;
- confidence and disagreement;
- time horizon and warning time;
- reversibility and irreversibility;
- dependencies and cascade paths;
- indicators that would increase or reduce concern;
- ordinary prevention and preparedness options;
- possible interventions and their own risks;
- data and model limitations;
- authorship, funding, conflicts, and dissent.

## 7.3 Minimum plurality

Before recommending extraordinary review, the ERO should seek independent estimates that differ in institutional affiliation, method, data source, or disciplinary perspective. Where time does not permit plurality, the estimate must state that limitation and trigger early review.

## 7.4 Epistemological pluralism

Scientific, engineering, operational, local, Indigenous, historical, and experiential knowledge may each contribute where relevant. Knowledge holders determine whether protected knowledge may be shared, transformed, digitized, modelled, published, or retained.

Pluralism does not authorize extraction or force ceremonial or cultural participation.

## 7.5 No metric-to-power automation

No risk score, model output, probability threshold, expected-loss figure, cultural-resilience index, adoption target, public sentiment measure, or dashboard status automatically declares an emergency or activates powers.

# 8. Adversarial review and the Office of the Adversary interface

## 8.1 Purpose

Catastrophic-risk institutions are vulnerable to groupthink, prestige cascades, secrecy, model monoculture, funding capture, and fear-driven action. Major estimates should therefore receive structured challenge.

## 8.2 Review functions

An adversarial review may test:

- whether the hazard mechanism is coherent;
- whether probability and consequence have been conflated;
- alternative explanations;
- observation independence;
- data quality and missingness;
- model sensitivity;
- institutional incentives;
- distributional consequences;
- risks created by the proposed intervention;
- reversibility and exit options;
- false-positive and false-negative costs;
- whether ordinary law can respond.

## 8.3 Independence

The challenger must not report to the operational commander or depend on the success of an intervention for funding, status, or continued existence.

## 8.4 No veto or command

The Office of the Adversary or equivalent body may publish challenge, request reconsideration, and trigger review where law so provides. It does not declare emergencies, command response, or adjudicate disputes.

# 9. Dependency, concentration, and continuity mapping

## 9.1 System dependency map

Preparedness must identify dependencies among:

- food and agriculture;
- water and sanitation;
- energy and fuel;
- telecommunications and cloud infrastructure;
- health and pharmaceuticals;
- financial settlement and payment systems;
- transport, ports, and logistics;
- public administration and identity systems;
- laboratories, compute, satellites, and specialized manufacturing;
- media and public communication;
- governance, courts, and records;
- ecological systems and local knowledge.

## 9.2 Concentration and substitution

Each map should identify chokepoints, correlated dependencies, single suppliers, geographic concentration, proprietary constraints, scarce skills, repair capacity, alternative providers, local substitutes, and graceful-degradation options.

## 9.3 Protected continuity profile

Continuity planning must preserve at minimum:

- life-sustaining care;
- safe water, food, shelter, sanitation, and energy;
- emergency communication;
- disability supports and assistive technology;
- child, elder, and dependent care;
- wages and lawful income continuity where possible;
- access to justice and urgent review;
- public records necessary for rights;
- Indigenous and community-controlled knowledge and infrastructure;
- non-digital alternatives where feasible.

## 9.4 No centralization by preparedness

A continuity map may reveal a need for redundancy. It does not authorize one PIS institution to own, control, or permanently integrate the mapped systems.

# 10. Readiness architecture

## 10.1 Readiness functions

PIS readiness supports:

- hazard-specific plans;
- interoperable but plural contact and request channels;
- capability inventories;
- pre-negotiated mutual-aid agreements;
- legal-authority mapping;
- multilingual and accessible public information;
- protected stockpiles and distributed reserves;
- offline and low-tech fallback procedures;
- workforce safety and surge agreements;
- continuity and recovery plans;
- termination and demobilisation templates.

## 10.2 Preparedness does not equal mobilization

Training, stockpiling, modelling, exercises, and mutual-aid planning do not authorize deployment. Actual use requires the authority identified in the relevant agreement or activation record.

## 10.3 Distributed reserves

Where practical, critical supplies, data, expertise, and repair capacity should be geographically and institutionally distributed. Distribution must not become compulsory transfer of Indigenous, community, proprietary, personal, or security-sensitive resources.

## 10.4 Readiness gaps

The ERO may publish a Readiness Gap Record describing unavailable authority, capability, finance, data, personnel, or continuity protection. A gap is not permission to act without authority.

# 11. Exercises, simulations, and institutional wind tunnels

## 11.1 Purpose

Exercises test assumptions, handoffs, time limits, rights protections, logistics, data flows, continuity, public communication, and restoration—not merely speed of command.

## 11.2 Required scenario diversity

The exercise portfolio should include:

- severe-looking false alarms;
- low-probability, high-consequence events;
- slowly developing catastrophic conditions;
- simultaneous hazards;
- disagreement among models;
- compromised or unavailable central institutions;
- refusal by an affected authority;
- Indigenous non-engagement;
- infrastructure and communications failure;
- wrongful intervention;
- successful local response requiring no global escalation;
- persistent conditions requiring transition to ordinary law.

## 11.3 No performative emergency

Exercises may not create real coercive powers, secretly surveil participants, manipulate public fear, or stage affected communities as legitimacy inputs. Participation terms, data use, risk, compensation, and withdrawal must be explicit.

## 11.4 Exercise evaluation

Evaluation should assess:

- whether authority was correctly identified;
- whether observation remained separate from command;
- whether the least coercive effective option was considered;
- whether essential continuity and rights were protected;
- whether false alarms were handled without punishment;
- whether authorities terminated and returned on time;
- whether dissent and uncertainty remained visible;
- whether the exercise revealed that no escalation was needed.

# 12. Public catastrophic-risk literacy

## 12.1 Purpose

Public education may help people understand risk, uncertainty, preparedness, mutual aid, misinformation, and the limits of emergency authority.

## 12.2 Protections

Existential or catastrophic-risk education must be:

- voluntary except where a lawful educational authority adopts age-appropriate curriculum;
- transparent about evidence and uncertainty;
- plural and open to criticism;
- independent from Treaty-ratification campaigns or institutional self-promotion;
- accessible by language, disability, literacy, and technology level;
- free from compelled spirituality, developmental classification, civilizational loyalty, or political worldview;
- aligned with Synoptic Protocol v4.2.

## 12.3 No fear-based mobilisation target

The number of people trained, app downloads, public concern, institutional trust, or support for a PIS programme is not a measure of reduced catastrophic risk.

---

# 13. Mutual aid and assistance network

## 13.1 Pre-negotiated agreements

Competent authorities may establish mutual-aid agreements specifying:

- assistance available;
- request and acceptance procedures;
- host authority;
- personnel status and professional recognition;
- command and safety rules;
- data access;
- equipment and asset custody;
- finance and reimbursement;
- liability and insurance;
- duration, withdrawal, and return;
- dispute and remedy routes.

## 13.2 Offers without request

An institution may offer assistance. It may not deploy into another jurisdiction merely because it considers the threat catastrophic, except under a separate valid legal basis for immediate rescue or collective defence.

## 13.3 No participation penalty

Refusal to join the PIS, ERO, a mutual-aid compact, an exercise, a data network, or a preparedness programme is not itself sanctionable and does not justify withholding unrelated essential assistance.

## 13.4 Humanitarian neutrality

Life-saving assistance must not be conditioned on political alignment, military cooperation, intellectual-property transfer, data surrender, Treaty ratification, GGF adoption, or public endorsement.

---

# 14. Declaration and activation interface

## 14.1 ERO recommendation

The ERO may recommend that identified competent authorities review whether a legal emergency threshold has been met. The recommendation must include the state estimate, alternatives, uncertainty, affected rights, and known authority gaps.

## 14.2 Declaration by competent authority

A declaration must identify:

- legal source;
- declarant;
- hazard and evidence;
- territory, persons, systems, and duration;
- ordinary procedures considered insufficient;
- rights affected;
- review, renewal, and termination;
- actions not authorized by the declaration.

## 14.3 Declaration does not equal activation

A declaration does not automatically activate:

- every PIS module;
- Meta-Governance command;
- Aegis, Shield, police, military, or intelligence functions;
- data access;
- funding transfers;
- facility shutdowns;
- quarantine, evacuation, or treatment;
- sanctions or trade controls.

Each power requires a separate activation schedule.

## 14.4 Emergency Authority Provenance Record

Every consequential activation records the actor, authority source, scope, affected rights, operational commander, evidence, finance, data rules, review, expiry, handoff, and restoration obligations.

# 15. Operating layers and time architecture

## 15.1 Layer A — Ordinary prevention and preparedness

Research, standards, exercises, mutual aid, stockpiles, continuity plans, and voluntary risk reduction operate under ordinary authority.

## 15.2 Layer B — Alert and state estimation

Observers issue alerts and estimates without coercive effect.

## 15.3 Layer C — Requested or pre-authorized assistance

Assistance proceeds under a host request, standing compact, operator mandate, contract, professional authority, or other valid instrument.

## 15.4 Layer D — Provisional emergency action

Narrow provisional action may last no more than seventy-two hours where valid law permits and delay would cause imminent serious harm. It requires immediate documentation and review.

## 15.5 Layer E — Continued or extraordinary response

Continued extraordinary authority requires a valid procedure, power-specific activation, and renewal no longer than thirty days at a time. Beyond ninety cumulative days, the condition receives extraordinary independent review and a transition plan to ordinary law or durable institutions.

## 15.6 No serial evasion

Changing the name of the declaration, commander, territory, module, or legal instrument does not restart the clock where the same extraordinary powers continue.

# 16. No takeover and no emergency executive

## 16.1 No PIS takeover

The PIS does not override normal functions, absorb domain institutions, suspend ordinary jurisdiction, or become the default authority when several systems are affected.

## 16.2 No UN-ESC reconfiguration

Meta-Governance bodies do not transform into an Existential Security Council. They may convene an emergency coordination session to:

- share state estimates;
- identify competent authorities;
- maintain the Standing and Jurisdiction Map;
- document requests and offers;
- identify interface conflicts;
- track reviews and expirations.

They may not command, deploy, tax, sanction, detain, seize, censor, renew powers, or adjudicate.

## 16.3 No residual authority

If no institution has lawful authority for a proposed act, the act remains unauthorized. The gap must be addressed through legislation, Treaty protocol, compact, host consent, judicial order, or another valid process.

## 16.4 Catastrophe does not cure invalidity

An intervention does not become lawful merely because it worked. Emergency performance does not create permanent jurisdiction, precedent, ownership, or institutional entitlement.

# 17. Action-module architecture

The Protocol coordinates interfaces among separately authorized modules. No module inherits another module’s powers.

## 17.1 Public-health and laboratory response

Uses the public-health and hazardous-material modules of the Emergency Interface Specification and Global Health v2.2.1 and Aethelred v1.2.1. Health assessment, declaration, medical operations, allocation, licensing, investigation, and adjudication remain separate.

## 17.2 Disaster rescue and humanitarian logistics

Uses Living Shield v4.4.1 and valid local, national, humanitarian, or mutual-aid authority. Rescue, evacuation, shelter, infrastructure stabilization, and recovery each retain their own rules.

## 17.3 Cyber and infrastructure assistance

Uses Technology Governance v3.6, Aurora Accord v1.3, Conduit Protocol, operator authority, and valid cyber law. Defensive assistance does not imply authority for offensive access, attribution, counterattack, or surveillance.

## 17.4 Biological and environmental containment

Uses valid biosafety, environmental, health, labour, Indigenous, and land authorities. A catastrophic-risk estimate cannot itself license release, compel access, or impose a sector-wide pause.

## 17.5 Space and natural-hazard coordination

May support asteroid observation, orbit computation, warning, civil defence planning, space-weather forecasting, and international mission coordination. Launch, interception, nuclear devices, airspace, liability, and military assets require their own legal authorities.

## 17.6 Nuclear and radiological risk

May support detection, continuity planning, humanitarian preparedness, and diplomatic interfaces. Weapons command, verification, inspection, sanctions, non-proliferation enforcement, and defensive action remain outside the Protocol unless separately authorized.

## 17.7 Unarmed international assistance

Technical, health, rescue, humanitarian, and environmental personnel operate under host consent, standing agreements, or another valid basis, with mission-bounded command and clear withdrawal.

## 17.8 Armed defensive capability

Armed action is not a PIS module. It requires a separately valid legal basis, civilian authority, rules of engagement, command responsibility, necessity, proportionality, review, and termination. No framework handoff can manufacture those powers.

# 18. Operational coordination and command

## 18.1 Mission-bounded command

Every activated operation identifies:

- the mission;
- commanding authority;
- personnel and assets;
- territory and facilities;
- permitted and prohibited acts;
- professional autonomy and safety;
- data and secrecy rules;
- communication duties;
- review and expiration;
- handoff and return.

## 18.2 Multiple commands

Where several authorities operate, the coordination record distinguishes command, liaison, technical advice, host authority, professional responsibility, and judicial review. A common coordination room does not create a single legal chain of command.

## 18.3 Professional autonomy

Clinicians, engineers, scientists, pilots, safety officers, humanitarian personnel, and other professionals retain applicable duties, refusal rights, and protected escalation routes. Political or operational leaders may not compel professional falsification.

## 18.4 Handoff

A handoff records responsibility, evidence, assets, credentials, unresolved risks, work obligations, data custody, public communication, and expiration. Responsibility cannot disappear between frameworks.

# 19. Rights, triage, and scarce-resource allocation

## 19.1 Rights and non-derogation

Every operation must identify protected rights and lawful derogations, if any. At minimum, emergency action protects dignity, non-discrimination, bodily integrity, due process, disability access, family communication, essential care, humane conditions, and access to urgent review.

## 19.2 Allocation is not an ERO function

The ERO and PIS coordination bodies do not allocate vaccines, food, shelter, evacuation places, compute, energy, protective equipment, medical treatment, or survival infrastructure.

## 19.3 Allocation rules

Where a competent authority allocates scarce life-sustaining resources, it must publish or protect a rule addressing:

- legitimate objective;
- clinical or operational relevance;
- non-discrimination;
- disability and age bias;
- children and dependants;
- pregnancy and caregiving;
- essential workers and reciprocal obligations;
- territorial and historical inequity;
- randomization where claims remain equal;
- conflicts of interest and anti-corruption;
- review, appeal, and recordkeeping.

## 19.4 No “equity ark” discretion

No council may select protected populations, cultural inheritances, or knowledge for preservation through opaque moral ranking. Continuity and preservation programmes require transparent criteria, community authority, non-transfer safeguards, and plural repositories.

---

# 20. Indigenous, local, and affected-community authority

## 20.1 Affected-nation standing

Where an action affects Indigenous peoples, territories, genetic resources, knowledge, infrastructure, or sacred sites, the relevant Indigenous nations and authorities have direct standing. No Earth Council or global representative substitutes for them.

## 20.2 Method and manner

Local and Indigenous authorities may control the method and manner of assistance within their jurisdiction where their law so provides. This does not authorize a PIS body to define their authority or declare that one universal veto exists.

## 20.3 Non-engagement

A people or community may decline participation in ERO data networks, exercises, public campaigns, registries, knowledge vaults, or PIS programmes without losing unrelated rights or essential assistance.

## 20.4 Protected knowledge

Traditional and local knowledge may be used only under the authority, consent, custody, and non-transfer conditions specified by its holders. Emergency urgency does not create permanent rights to digitize, model, publish, commercialize, retain, or train systems on that knowledge.

## 20.5 Immediate rescue

Immediate rescue without prior consultation is limited to preventing imminent death or serious injury where delay would cause the harm. It must be narrow, temporary, non-extractive, documented, and immediately reviewable. It creates no continuing access or jurisdiction.

# 21. Data, models, security, and secrecy

## 21.1 Aurora interface

All personal, relational, community, Indigenous-restricted, evidentiary, infrastructure, security-sensitive, biometric, health, and model-derived data follow Aurora Accord v1.3.

## 21.2 Data minimization

Preparedness and response collect only data necessary for a stated function. The Emergency Data Record identifies legal basis, fields, sources, recipients, retention, correction, deletion, access controls, protected non-transfer, and post-crisis disposition.

## 21.3 Model governance

Major risk models document:

- purpose and decision context;
- training or source data;
- assumptions;
- uncertainty and calibration;
- sensitivity;
- known failure modes;
- human interpretation;
- conflicts and funding;
- security limitations;
- prohibited uses.

A model does not become a commander or legal decision-maker.

## 21.4 Secrecy

Some vulnerability, intelligence, laboratory, cyber, or defence information may require protection. Secrecy must be scoped, reviewable, time-bounded where possible, and separated from the public evidence needed to justify rights-affecting action.

## 21.5 Knowledge vaults

Knowledge preservation should be federated, redundant, and authority-sensitive. A global vault must not become a compulsory repository or a means of extracting protected knowledge. Holders retain withdrawal, correction, access, succession, and non-transfer rights.

# 22. Public information and epistemic safeguards

## 22.1 Synoptic interface

Crisis communication follows Synoptic Protocol v4.2. Public institutions may warn, authenticate, translate, explain uncertainty, publish corrections, and identify official instructions within their lawful competence.

## 22.2 No propaganda function

PIS actors may not manufacture political narratives, conduct covert influence campaigns, classify dissent as misinformation merely because it challenges policy, or condition assistance on public endorsement.

## 22.3 Public estimates

Public risk communication should distinguish:

- observed facts;
- modelled scenarios;
- confidence;
- uncertainty and dissent;
- recommended voluntary preparation;
- legally binding instructions and their authority;
- corrections and changes.

## 22.4 Protected communication

Whistleblowers, researchers, journalists, community monitors, and professionals require lawful protection from retaliation. Source protection does not eliminate duties concerning fabricated evidence, conflicts, or safety-sensitive disclosure.

# 23. Finance, procurement, innovation, and intellectual property

## 23.1 No self-authorized finance

The Protocol does not create a Sin Tax, high-risk technology levy, defence-budget reallocation, Global Commons Fund appropriation, innovation portfolio, patent revocation, compulsory licence, or fixed spending allocation.

## 23.2 Lawful readiness finance

Preparedness may be funded through valid appropriations, grants, contracts, insurance, bonds, mutual-aid agreements, philanthropy, procurement, or separately ratified fiscal instruments.

## 23.3 Non-conditional essential support

Humanitarian assistance, baseline resilience, and life-saving support must not depend on Treaty ratification, PIS membership, data surrender, military transition, public narrative support, or ideological alignment.

## 23.4 Emergency procurement

Emergency procurement must record authority, need, competition or exception, pricing, conflicts, labour and environmental safeguards, delivery verification, audit, ownership, termination, and disposition of unused assets.

## 23.5 Innovation

Public-interest research in AI safety, biosafety, asteroid defence, resilient infrastructure, health, and ecological restoration may be supported. Research funding does not authorize deployment, compulsory data access, or automatic conversion of intellectual property.

## 23.6 Intellectual property actions

Compulsory licensing, patent limitation, open-science conditions, or access obligations require valid law or contract and must address continuity, safety, compensation where required, know-how, manufacturing capacity, and worker conditions.

---

# 24. Personnel, labour, and responder protection

## 24.1 Emergency work record

Mobilisation identifies role, employer or authority, consent or lawful duty, pay, hours, safety, insurance, accommodation, family support, disability access, data use, command, refusal and whistleblowing, duration, demobilisation, and remedy.

## 24.2 No unpaid moral conscription

Community members, Indigenous land defenders, health workers, maintainers, researchers, volunteers, or youth may not be treated as an inexhaustible “immune system” or compensated only through symbolic recognition, Hearts, Leaves, civic status, or moral praise.

## 24.3 Responder safety

Readiness includes protective equipment, training, rotation, psychosocial care, rest, hazard pay, insurance, evacuation, family communication, and post-deployment health monitoring.

## 24.4 Compulsory service

Compulsory service requires a separately valid legal basis, necessity, equality, conscientious-objection routes, safety, compensation, duration, and review. The Protocol does not create it.

## 24.5 Military personnel

Military logistics, engineering, aviation, medical, space, or communications capacities may support civilian missions under valid civilian and military authority. Their use does not transfer military command to the PIS or convert humanitarian operations into military governance.

---

# 25. Justice, investigation, and enforcement

## 25.1 Justice remains separate

Justice Systems v1.4 governs jurisdiction, standing, evidence, interim measures, hearings, appeals, remedies, recognition, and enforcement.

## 25.2 Risk estimate is not evidence of liability

An ERO estimate may inform an investigation. It does not establish guilt, negligence, intent, causation, hostility, or legal responsibility.

## 25.3 Facility action

Inspection, access restriction, suspension, shutdown, seizure, or control of a facility requires valid operator authority, regulatory power, judicial order, or emergency law, plus continuity and safety protections.

## 25.4 Sanctions and trade restrictions

Sanctions, market exclusions, trade restrictions, asset freezes, and patent actions require separately constituted authority and adjudication. Refusal to join a PIS programme is not an offence.

## 25.5 Investigation and evidence handoff

Operational responders preserve evidence without becoming investigators or prosecutors unless valid law combines those roles with appropriate safeguards. Handoffs document chain of custody, access, classification, protected knowledge, data rights, and disclosure limits.

## 25.6 Armed enforcement

No PIS actor may direct armed enforcement through a framework reference. Armed action follows the applicable Treaty decision class, domestic and international law, civilian command, rules of engagement, and independent review.

# 26. Termination, authority restoration, and continuity

## 26.1 Termination triggers

Temporary powers terminate when:

- the legal threshold no longer holds;
- the mission is complete;
- authority expires or is withdrawn;
- a reviewing body orders termination;
- the action is ineffective, disproportionate, or harmful;
- ordinary institutions can resume;
- the condition has become persistent and requires ordinary law.

## 26.2 Termination certificate

The certificate records ended powers, remaining hazards, unresolved cases, assets, personnel, contracts, data, secrecy, public communication, compensation, and successor responsibilities.

## 26.3 Authority return

Command, credentials, facilities, assets, systems, data access, licences, and decision rights return to the ordinary competent authority. Temporary operational control does not become ownership.

## 26.4 Service continuity

Termination must not abruptly remove life-sustaining services. The handoff identifies continuity funding, personnel, supplies, maintenance, local capacity, and accountability.

## 26.5 Institutional sunset

Temporary PIS coordination cells dissolve when their mission ends. Continued useful functions must be separately authorized under ordinary governance.

# 27. Wrongful action, restoration, and compensation

## 27.1 Wrongful-action record

A person or community may challenge an alert, declaration, activation, operation, data use, allocation, exclusion, secrecy decision, failure to protect, or failure to terminate.

## 27.2 Available restoration

Depending on valid law, restoration may include:

- cessation or reversal;
- release from restriction;
- correction of records;
- return or deletion of data;
- return of property or control;
- restoration of employment, licence, service, or access;
- medical, ecological, cultural, or livelihood repair;
- compensation;
- public correction or apology;
- institutional reform;
- disciplinary, civil, or criminal proceedings.

## 27.3 Good-faith false alarms

A good-faith alert or forecast that proves wrong should not automatically trigger punishment, repayment, loss of future support, or reputational sanction. Fraud, reckless disregard, manipulation, and avoidable procedural failure remain reviewable.

## 27.4 Failure to act

Institutions may also bear responsibility for failing to issue warnings, maintain required readiness, protect vulnerable populations, or use valid authority where duty and capacity existed.

# 28. After-action review and learning

## 28.1 Scope

Every significant activation should receive independent review of:

- hazard estimation;
- observation independence;
- authority provenance;
- declaration and activation;
- command and handoffs;
- rights and distributional effects;
- work and responder safety;
- data, secrecy, and communication;
- finance and procurement;
- termination and restoration;
- wrongful action and compensation;
- alternatives that were missed;
- whether escalation was necessary.

## 28.2 Independence and participation

Reviewers should be institutionally independent from those whose performance they assess. Affected people, workers, responders, Indigenous authorities, host institutions, dissenters, and non-participants must have safe routes to contribute.

## 28.3 Learning does not transfer authority

Successful coordination does not justify permanent command. A useful temporary function may continue only through ordinary authorization.

## 28.4 Failure may stop escalation

The review may conclude that:

- a risk was overstated;
- a model was unfit;
- a declaration was unnecessary;
- central coordination added little;
- local response was sufficient;
- an intervention worsened risk;
- a preparedness programme should not scale;
- the Protocol or an institution should be narrowed or dissolved.

Evaluation informs learning and requests to competent authorities. It does not automatically activate finance, intervention, sanction, or command.

# 32. Required operational records

## Record inheritance and conformance

1. **Specialization does not replace the canonical set.**  
   This framework's domain record list specializes but does not replace the records defined by the Emergency, Catastrophic-Risk, and Continuity Interface Specification and the Emergency and Catastrophic-Risk Cluster Record Profile. Every canonical record remains required whenever its applicability trigger occurs.

2. **Canonical identity.**  
   Every domain record shall identify the canonical `ECRC-##` record or records it `implements`, `extends`, `splits`, `contains`, or `imports`. A domain label may differ from the canonical title, but the canonical identifier, schema version, minimum common header, legal effect, access protections, and lifecycle obligations remain traceable.

3. **Direct import.**  
   A canonical record not mapped to a domain specialization is imported directly when triggered. Absence from a domain appendix, implementation guide, software form, dashboard, or local registry does not make the canonical record optional.

4. **Non-applicability.**  
   A record may be marked `not_applicable` only through a reasoned, event- or action-specific determination identifying: the trigger tested; the deciding function; the evidence and legal basis; the reason the trigger cannot arise or has not arisen; the records that would become required if conditions change; and the review or expiry condition. `Not applicable` does not mean unknown, inconvenient, deferred, or unavailable.

5. **Split records.**  
   Where several domain records jointly implement one canonical record, the framework shall declare a `splits` relationship, identify every component, allocate each required canonical field, designate the function that verifies completeness, and preserve one traceable lifecycle. No component inherits the authority or legal effect of another by implication.

6. **Containing packages.**  
   A domain package may contain several canonical records only when each canonical subrecord remains separately identifiable, reviewable, correctable, exportable, and closable, with its own responsible authority, legal effect, timestamp, expiry, and access class. The Investigation and Evidence Handoff Record (`ECRC-24`) and the Adjudication and Enforcement Handoff Record (`ECRC-25`) shall remain distinct even when displayed in one user interface or transmitted in one package.

7. **No record-to-power conversion.**  
   A record name, canonical identifier, completed form, model output, dashboard state, validation result, digital signature, institutional endorsement, or reference to another framework does not create authority. Consequential action requires a valid Emergency Authority Provenance Record (`ECRC-06`) and every other record required by the applicable activation package.

8. **Shared event identity without shared command.**  
   Records participating in a compound or cross-domain event shall carry the same `cluster_event_id` and identify their responsible function, authority, handoff state, evidence or data custodian, review time, and closure state. Shared identity coordinates information; it does not merge jurisdictions, transfer command, or establish a common legal classification.

9. **Handoff acceptance and retained duties.**  
   A handoff is effective only when the receiving function records acceptance, rejection, deficiency, or concurrent responsibility. A handoff transfers only the responsibility expressly named. It does not erase the sender's retained duties, source obligations, correction duties, evidence-preservation duties, or responsibility for prior acts.

10. **Lifecycle completeness.**  
    Any activation package that can restrict rights, deploy personnel or assets, process emergency data, spend extraordinary funds, or continue beyond a provisional period shall also represent renewal, termination, authority and asset restoration, emergency-data disposition, wrongful-action remedy, after-action review, institutional sunset or continuation, and unresolved risk or dissent. A package is not closed merely because a declaration or mission has ended.

11. **Access and protected material.**  
    Canonical identity does not require universal publication. Public summaries, protected annexes, restricted operational records, protected sovereignty or source records, and sealed adjudicative or security material shall follow the access classes in `ECRC/0.1`. Indigenous authority, non-engagement, protected knowledge, source protection, privacy, security, correction, retention, deletion, return, repatriation, and non-transfer obligations survive transport between frameworks.

12. **Correction, supersession, and integrity.**  
    Record identifiers remain immutable. Corrections, withdrawals, supersession, invalidation, and disputes shall preserve the prior state, reason, responsible actor, time, and linked replacement. Hashes and automated validation may support integrity but do not prove authenticity, accuracy, legal validity, or authority.

13. **Precedence and conflict.**  
    Where a domain record conflicts with the canonical profile, the rule that better preserves valid authority provenance, functional separation, rights, protected knowledge, temporal limits, restoration, and review controls for cluster interoperability controls. A domain framework may impose stronger safeguards. It may not weaken the canonical safeguards through renaming, omission, aggregation, software implementation, or local practice.

14. **Version pinning.**  
    This framework conforms only to the exact profile and interface versions identified in its compatibility manifest. A later framework, profile, schema, or interface version is not substituted automatically. Material changes fail closed until a successful compatibility and scenario test is recorded.

15. **Conformance appendix.**  
    This framework shall publish a complete crosswalk for `ECRC-01` through `ECRC-33`, showing for each canonical record: domain record name; inheritance relationship; applicability rule; responsible function; default access class; required linked records; lifecycle closure; and any current gap. Conformance is not established by a general statement that another framework controls.

16. **Automated systems.**  
    Automated systems may check schema, completeness, links, expiry, consistency, signatures, and access rules. They may not determine whether an authority is legally valid, whether evidence is credible, whether a person is liable or guilty, whether an Indigenous authority has consented, or whether an emergency power should be exercised or renewed.

17. **Failure rule.**  
    Missing, invalid, expired, incompatible, or unresolved authority and lifecycle records block the affected consequential action. They do not transfer authority to a coordinating body, observatory, emergency council, court, fund, platform, model, or residual command.

# 35. Conclusion

The Planetary Immune System is most valuable when it makes civilization harder to surprise and easier to coordinate without requiring a permanent centre of command.

It observes before it orders. It prepares before it centralizes. It preserves options instead of converting uncertainty into sovereignty. It helps competent institutions find one another, see shared dependencies, request assistance, test plans, protect continuity, and learn from failure.

Its closing discipline is:

> **Foresee without ruling. Prepare without mobilizing. Coordinate without taking over. Act only through valid authority. Return every temporary power.**

## Appendix conformance rules

- A domain record carries the canonical identifier in its header and preserves the common 27-field header defined by `ECRC/0.1`.
- A `direct canonical import` is instantiated from the profile without waiting for a domain-specific form.
- `ECRC-24` and `ECRC-25` are separate records even when transmitted in one package or displayed in one interface.
- A package that reaches termination is not closed until required restoration, data disposition, claims transfer, unresolved-risk custody, and archival steps are complete.
- Any later interface, profile, clause, or domain-framework version requires a recorded compatibility test before substitution.
